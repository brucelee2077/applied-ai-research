01

Who is "it"?

How a sentence quietly tells you what a word points back to — and how a language model learns the same trick.

A tiny mystery, solved in a heartbeat

Read this sentence, slowly:

🔍
The sentence

"The cat sat on the mat, because it was tired."

Now answer one question: who is "it"?

You knew instantly. It is the cat — not the mat. But notice what just happened: nobody told you. The word "it" does not say "cat" on its face. You figured it out by quietly glancing back at the earlier words and picking the one that fit.

✨
The surprise

A language model — the kind of program behind the chat assistants you have used — does the exact same thing. The trick it uses has a name: attention. This whole module is about that one idea.

A detective re-reading the earlier pages

Picture a detective in the middle of a mystery novel. They reach a confusing line and stop. To make sense of it, they flip back through the earlier pages, looking for clues that matter for this exact line.

Some clues they stare at hard. Others they barely glance at. That choice — how much to focus on each earlier clue — is the heart of the whole thing.

🕵️
The detective = the word being read right now

When the model reaches "it", that word is the detective trying to make sense of itself.

📖
Flipping back through pages = checking the other words

The word looks back at "cat", "mat", "sat", and every other word in the sentence.

🔦
How hard they stare at a clue = the attention weight

A big stare on "cat", a faint glance at "mat". That amount of focus is called the attention weight.

⚠️
Where the detective story breaks down

A real detective reads one clue at a time, in order. Attention looks at every word at once, and it re-decides who matters all over again at each step of its thinking — not just once.

Every word asks one question

Here is the plain idea, stripped down. For each token (each little piece of text, usually a word), the model asks a single question:

💭
The one question

"Right now, who should I listen to?"

The model answers by giving every other word a focus score, then blending those words together using the scores. Words with a big score get heard loudly. Words with a tiny score barely whisper. This deciding-and-blending is what we call attention.

🎯

It IS: relevance

Attention measures relevance — how much each word matters to the word being read right now.

🔀

It IS: routing

It decides where information flows — which words feed into which. Engineers call this routing: a learned map of who listens to whom.

🚫

It is NOT: understanding

The model is not "getting" the story like a person does. It is only scoring relevance and blending. No grammar lesson, no rulebook.

🚫

It is NOT: memory

Nothing is saved for later. The focus scores are worked out fresh, from scratch, every single time.

Aha — attention is a spotlight

Think of each word holding a spotlight that it sweeps over all the other words. Bright where it cares, dim where it does not. Attention is simply that per-word spotlight — a fresh one for every word, every time.

02

The Library: Query, Key, Value

Every word plays three roles at once. Meet them.

You walk into a library, wanting dinosaurs

You do not know exactly which book you want. You only know what you are looking for: something about dinosaurs.

So how do you find it without reading every single book? You look at the labels on the shelves. You walk toward the ones that match. And the better a shelf matches, the more books you grab from it.

📚
This is exactly how a word finds meaning

In the last module, the word "it" needed to find "cat". It did not search the whole sentence blindly. It played a clever little library game. Let us learn the three pieces of that game.

🔎
Your request — "I want dinosaurs"

What you are looking for right now.

🏷️
The shelf labels — "Science", "History"

What each shelf advertises about itself.

📖
The actual books on the shelf

The real information you carry home.

Three roles every word plays

Here is the surprising part. In the library, three different people do these jobs. But inside an AI model, every word does all three jobs at once, for itself.

A word turns itself into three things. Engineers call them query, key, and value.

🔎

Query (Q)

What I am looking for. Like walking up to the desk and saying "I want dinosaurs." It is a question, not an answer.

🏷️

Key (K)

What I advertise about myself. Like the label on a shelf: "Science." Other words read my label to decide if I am worth listening to.

📖

Value (V)

The real information I carry. Like the actual books on the shelf. This is what I hand over when someone listens to me.

💡
The label is not the book

A shelf's label ("Science") is short and just for matching. The books are the real thing you take home. Keep these two apart in your mind — it is the most important idea in this whole module, and we will return to it.

Watch the words introduce themselves

Take the sentence: "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired." The word "it" wants to know what it stands for. Let us listen in as the words post their query, their key, and their value — and find their match.

I am also here! But my label says I am a flat object on the floor, not really a "doer". I probably match you a little, "it", but not a lot.

it

Let me compare. "cat" — animal, subject, early. That is a strong match for what I want! "mat" — flat object. That is a weak match. So I will listen mostly to "cat".

cat

Great, here is my real information (my Value): "furry animal, the one who sat, the one feeling tired." Take a big helping of it, since we matched so well.

it
"it" — walking away with a blend

Done! I now carry mostly "cat" information, with a tiny bit of "mat". I did not become "cat" — I just borrowed a big scoop of what "cat" carries. Now I know who I am. 🎉

Notice what happened. "it" compared its query to everyone's key. The best match decided who to listen to. Then it took a big helping of that word's value — not a copy of the label.

Good match, big scoop. Weak match, tiny scoop.

You almost never walk out with one single book. You walk out with a blend. The shelf that matched best gives you a big stack. A poorly matching shelf still gives you a little something.

1
Compare your request to every label

"it" checks its query against the key of "cat", "mat", and every other word. Each gets a match score.

2
Turn matches into scoop sizes

A strong match becomes a big scoop. A weak match becomes a small scoop. All the scoops together fill exactly one bag — no more, no less.

3
Take that much of each word's value

From "cat" you take a lot of its real information. From "mat" you take a little. You mix them into one blend.

🥤
It is a smoothie, not a single fruit

"it" does not pick one word and copy it. It pours in mostly "cat", a splash of "mat", a drop of others — and drinks the blend. That blended drink is the new meaning of "it".

The big idea: who you listen to vs. what you receive

This is the moment that makes attention click. There are two completely separate jobs happening, and people mix them up all the time.

🧭

Routing — Query vs Key

Comparing your request (Q) to the labels (K) only decides who you listen to. It is the matchmaking step. It moves zero real information by itself.

📦

Payload — the Value

The payload (V) is what you actually receive. The match decides the scoop size; the value is what fills the scoop.

💡
Why this matters so much

A word can match on one thing (its label) and hand over something completely different (its books). So "it" can find "cat" by matching labels — but what it carries home is "cat" the real information, not the word "cat" itself. Routing chooses; payload delivers.

Where the library picture breaks down

Every analogy has an edge. Here is this one's edge, in one sentence:

⚠️
In a real library, different people write the labels and stock the books

But in self-attention, every word creates its own query, key, and value at the same time — three learned versions of itself. A word writes its own label and stocks its own shelf.

🧩
Where do these three come from?

Each word starts as an embedding — the word turned into a list of numbers. The model learned three ways to reshape that list: one makes the query, one makes the key, one makes the value. Same word, three useful versions.

03

The Recipe

Four steps turn "who matches whom" into a real answer — in plain words, then the formula as one labeled reference.

A recipe is just steps

You already know the idea from the last module: each word makes a Query (what it wants), a Key (what it advertises), and a Value (what it carries).

But making those three things is not the answer yet. We still have to use them. That is what this module is about: the four simple steps that turn all those matches into a fresh meaning for each word.

🎯
The whole module in one breath

SCORE every word against every word. SHRINK the scores a little. Turn them into percentages with softmax. BLEND the payloads by those percentages. Four steps. That is the recipe.

Mixing paint to a target shade

Imagine you want a very specific shade of orange. You have jars of red, yellow, and a little blue. You do not pour them in randomly. You decide how much of each color to add based on how well that color helps you reach the shade you want.

🎯

Your target shade

The exact orange you are aiming for. This is the word's Query — what it is looking for.

🏷️

Each jar's label

"Warm red", "bright yellow". You judge how well each label fits your target. That judging is the score.

🥫

The paint inside

The real color you pour out. This is the Value — the payload that actually mixes into the cup.

🥤

A full cup

The amounts you pour must add up to one full cup — no more, no less. That "must add to a full cup" rule is softmax.

A jar with a great-matching label gets poured a lot. A jar with a weak label still gets a small splash. You walk away with a weighted blend — a mix, not a single color. That blend is the new meaning of the word.

⚠️
Where the paint picture breaks down

Paint mixing goes one way — you make one cup. But here, every word mixes its own blend at the same time, all in parallel.

The four steps

Here is the recipe, one card per step. Read them top to bottom — each step feeds the next.

1
SCORE — how well does each Query match each Key?

For every word, compare its Query against every other word's Key. A good match gets a high score; a poor match gets a low one. Behind the scenes this match is a dot product: multiply the matching numbers and add them up.

2
SHRINK — make the scores a little smaller

Divide every score by the same fixed number to scale them down. This keeps the next step gentle so it does not over-react to one big score. The full reason for this comes in Module 4.

3
SOFTMAX — turn the scores into percentages

Run the scores through softmax. Messy scores become clean percentages that add up to 100%. A bigger score gets a bigger slice of the cup.

4
BLEND — mix the Values by those percentages

Pour each word's Value into the cup using its percentage. Add them up. Out comes a brand-new set of numbers — the word's fresh, context-aware meaning.

💡
Routing versus payload

Steps 1–3 decide how much to pour (the routing). Step 4 decides what gets poured (the payload). The matching and the mixing are two separate jobs — that split is the heart of attention.

Softmax, in plain words

Softmax is the step that worries people the most, so let us make it boring. It takes any list of numbers and does two things, every time, with no exceptions:

💯
The percentages always add up to 100%

No matter what numbers you feed in, the slices fill exactly one full cup.

📈
A bigger score always gets a bigger slice

Softmax never flips the order. The strongest match keeps the largest share.

The same recipe, written as one formula

Everything you just learned has a one-line name in math. You do not need to read it like an equation — read it like a label that points at the four steps you already know.

🧮
The attention formula

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dₖ ) · V

Read left to right, it is the recipe. Here is what each piece is doing.

Q Kᵀ Step 1 — SCORE. All the match scores: every Query compared against every Key at once.
÷ √dₖ Step 2 — SHRINK. The gentle shrink: divide the scores by a fixed number so the next step stays calm.
softmax( … ) Step 3 — SOFTMAX. Turn the scores into percentages that add up to 100%.
· V Step 4 — BLEND. Pour and mix the Values (the payloads) by those percentages.

04

The Math, Precisely

You have the recipe. Now read it like an engineer — every shape, every symbol, and the one division that keeps the whole thing trainable.

A volume mixing desk

Picture the desk a sound engineer sits behind at a concert. A row of sliding faders. Each fader controls how loud one source plays — the vocals, the drums, the bass. Push a fader up, that source gets louder in the final mix. Pull it down, it fades into the background.

🎚️
Each fader = one attention weight

It sets how loudly one source plays in the final blend.

🔊
Each source = one Value vector

The actual sound — the payload — that gets mixed in.

🎛️
The master trim = the √dk division

It stops one fader from slamming to maximum and drowning everyone else out.

🔎
Where the desk breaks down

On a real desk a human sets the faders by hand. In attention, the faders are computed from the data on every forward pass — the model decides, fresh each time, how loud each source should be.

One formula, built up piece by piece

Here is the whole operation. We will not read it all at once — we will assemble it left to right, naming every part and its shape as we go.

🧮
The full formula

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT / √dk ) V

Step 1 — the three inputs. Every token in the sequence produces three vectors. Stack them over all n tokens and you get three matrices: matrices Q, K, and V.

Q — n × d_k Queries: what each of the n tokens is looking for. dk is the key/query dimension.
K — n × d_k Keys: what each token advertises. Same width dk as Q, so they can be compared.
V — n × d_v Values: the payload each token carries. Its width dv can differ from dk.

Step 2 — the scores. Multiply Q by K transposed (written KT). This takes the dot product of every query against every key, giving a score for every (query, key) pair.

Q K^T — n × n The raw similarity scores. One number for each of the n × n token pairs. This square is the expensive object — the whole next module is about its cost.

Step 3 — the weights. Divide by √dk, then apply softmax. The softmax runs row-wise: each row is one query, and that row becomes a set of weights over all the keys that sum to exactly 1.

d_k — scalar A single number (for example 64). We divide the scores by its square root before the softmax.
softmax — row-wise Applied per row, over the keys. Each query gets its own distribution that sums to 1. These are the faders on the desk.
output — n × d_v Multiply the weights by V. Each token's new, context-mixed representation. Same width dv as the Values.
💡
Read it as a chain of shapes

(n × dk) · (dk × n) → (n × n) → softmax → (n × n) · (n × dv) → (n × dv). Two matrix multiplies with a row-wise softmax in the middle. That is the entire operation.

The formula as real code

This is the exact function from the lab, reproduced line for line. The shapes in the comments are the same ones you just named. Read the left, then the plain English on the right.

CODE · python 
import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)   # numerical stability
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def scaled_dot_product_attention(Q, K, V, mask=None):
    # Q: (n, d_k)  K: (n, d_k)  V: (n, d_v)
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)           # (n, n)  <-- the O(n^2) object
    if mask is not None:                      # causal mask: forbid looking ahead
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, -np.inf)
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)        # row-wise: each query sums to 1
    return weights @ V, weights               # (n, d_v), (n, n)
PLAIN ENGLISH

Bring in NumPy, the library for fast math on grids of numbers.

Define softmax — the step that turns scores into faders that sum to 1.

Before taking exponentials, subtract the biggest value in each row. This is numerical stability — it stops the exponentials from overflowing, and it does not change the answer.

Raise e to the power of each (now shifted) score.

Divide each value by the row's total, so the row sums to exactly 1. Those are your weights.

Now the main function: it takes Q, K, V and an optional mask.

A reminder of the shapes: Q and K are n × dk, V is n × dv.

Read dk straight off Q — it is the width of each query vector.

The heart of it: multiply Q by K transposed and divide by √dk. The result is the n × n score grid — the object that costs O(n²).

If a causal mask is given, blank out every future position by setting its score to negative infinity, so it cannot be looked at.

Those blanked scores become exactly 0 after softmax — the model cannot look ahead.

Run softmax along the last axis. That means row-wise: each query becomes its own set of weights over the keys, summing to 1.

Multiply the weights by V to blend the payloads, and hand back both the blend (n × dv) and the weights (n × n).

⚠️
The single most common silent bug

axis=-1 is load-bearing. Softmax over the whole matrix, or over the columns (the queries), still produces numbers that sum to 1 — so nothing crashes — but the attention is now meaningless. The model trains, the loss looks plausible, and the behaviour is quietly wrong.

Why divide by √dk?

This is the master trim on the desk. Without it, one fader slams to the top and silences every other source. With it, the faders stay in a healthy range so the model can still blend several sources together.

Why it happens: a dot product adds up dk products. As dk grows, the scores grow with it — their spread grows like √dk. Feed very large, very spread-out numbers into softmax and it spikes onto a single token: one weight near 1, the rest near 0. A spike like that also flattens the gradient to almost nothing, so the model stops learning. Dividing by √dk cancels that growth and keeps the scores gentle.

Watch the same three scores [10, 5, 2] with the trim off, then on. (These are the verified numbers from the lab, with dk = 64.)

🍝 0.9%
🥗 0.0%

🎯
The takeaway in one line

Large dot products make softmax razor-sharp and gradients tiny. Dividing by √dk keeps the scores in a range where the model can still blend multiple sources and still learn.

One failure mode, one trade-off

⚠️

Failure mode: the wrong axis

Run softmax over the whole matrix, or over the columns instead of the rows, and it still sums to 1 — so nothing errors. But each query no longer gets its own distribution over the keys. Attention silently becomes nonsense. Pair this with skipping √dk at large dk: softmax becomes a one-hot spike, the gradient vanishes, and the layer stops learning.

⚖️

Trade-off: the n × n square

Computing Q KT as a full n × n matrix is exact and perfectly parallel — every token pair scored at once, ideal for GPUs. The price is O(n²) memory and compute. Double the sequence length and the square quadruples. That single cost shapes everything about long context — and is exactly the subject of the next module.

💡
Why these two are worth remembering

The wrong-axis bug is the one a senior engineer catches in code review because it never throws an error. The n × n trade-off is the one they raise in a design review, because it decides whether your model can read a whole book or just a paragraph.

05

The Cost of Attention

Attention reads every word against every other word. Double the text and you quadruple the work. Here is where long context gets expensive — and the trick that saves it.

Why does long context get so expensive?

You already know the core operation: every token compares itself against every other token. That sounds harmless for ten words. It is brutal for a hundred thousand.

Picture a party where every guest must shake hands with every other guest. With 5 guests that is about 10 handshakes. With 50 guests it is around 1,225. Add a few more people and the handshakes explode — far faster than the guest list grows.

🤝

Guests = tokens

Each token is a guest at the party. The sequence length we call n is the guest count.

💥

Handshakes = pairs

Every pair of tokens must be compared. The pairs grow with the square of the guest count.

🔁

Where it breaks

Real handshakes happen once. Attention redoes all the pairings at every layer of the network.

💡
The shape of the cost

Comparing every token to every other token is an O(n²) operation: the work grows with the square of the sequence length. This single fact drives almost every long-context engineering decision in modern language models.

Two objects, two very different growth rates

Inside an attention layer there are two memory objects that matter. One grows gently. One grows viciously. Telling them apart is the whole game.

📏
The token tensor — shape n × d — grows like O(n)

Each of the n tokens is a vector of length d (the model dimension). Add one token, add one row. Memory grows in a straight line.

🟥
The score matrix — shape n × n — grows like O(n²)

The materialized table of every-token-against-every-token. Add one token and you add a whole new row and a whole new column. Memory grows like the square.

Put real numbers on it. We will use fp16, where every number takes 2 bytes. This is the standard precision for the activations inside a running model.

n × d × 2 bytes Token tensor (fp16). n rows, each of width d, two bytes per number. Linear in n — this is the gentle one.
n × n × 2 bytes Score matrix (fp16). n rows by n columns, two bytes per number. Quadratic in n — this is the one that explodes.
📊
The ratio that tells the story

Divide the two: the score matrix is exactly n ÷ d times bigger than the token tensor. While n is smaller than d, the token tensor dominates. The moment n grows past d, the n × n matrix takes over — and from there it never looks back.

Watch the cost curves cross

This is the picture to keep in your head. Three memory-growth curves on log-log axes: the token tensor (n × d), the score matrix (n × n), and FlashAttention.

⚠️
Drag n and watch the wall appear

Slide n to the right. At first the token-tensor line sits on top. Then the n × n curve crosses above it and shoots past everything else — that crossing point is the long-context wall. Now tick the FlashAttention box: the n × n curve vanishes and a flat O(n) curve takes its place.

The 100×-per-10× rule

Here is the one number an interviewer wants to hear you reach for instantly. It falls straight out of the square.

1
Make the context 10× longer

Go from an 8,000-token prompt to an 80,000-token prompt. That is a 10× increase in n.

2
Attention memory grows ~100×

The n × n score matrix scales with n², and 10² is 100. So the attention memory balloons by roughly one hundred times.

3
Prefill compute grows ~100× too

The prefill pass does ~O(n²·d) FLOPs, so the work to read a 10× longer prompt also rises by about 100×.

💡
Long context is fundamentally a memory problem

The token tensor stays small. The n × n matrix is what you run out of room for first. So when someone asks "why can't this model just take a million tokens?", the honest first answer is not "not enough compute" — it is "not enough memory to hold all the pairwise scores."

FlashAttention: same answer, far less memory

FlashAttention is the trick that softens the wall. It is IO-aware: it organizes the work to avoid the expensive memory traffic, instead of changing the math.

🧮

Compute: unchanged

Still O(n²) FLOPs. The same number of multiply-adds. The exact same output, number for number.

📉

Memory: O(n)

It never builds the full n × n matrix. It walks through it in tiles, keeping only running totals — so attention memory drops to linear.

⚡

Why it is faster too

It keeps each tile inside fast on-chip memory and avoids round trips to slow HBM.

In production you rarely write the attention math by hand. You call one fused function, and the library uses a FlashAttention-backed kernel under the hood when it is available.

CODE 
import torch.nn.functional as F
# Fused, FlashAttention-backed when available. Does NOT materialize the n×n matrix.
out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
PLAIN ENGLISH

Pull in PyTorch's bundle of ready-made neural-network operations and call it F.

A note for the reader: this one call is fused (the whole attention is done in a single optimized step) and uses FlashAttention when the hardware supports it — so it never writes out the full n × n score table.

Run attention on the queries, keys, and values in one go. is_causal=True means each token may only look back at earlier tokens, never ahead. The result out is the new, context-mixed representation — identical to the slow way, just computed without ever holding the giant matrix in memory.

That last line is the FlashAttention curve from the chart. Same output as the slow path, but the memory it uses is the flat O(n) line, not the exploding O(n²) one.

⚠️
Failure mode & the trade-off worth naming

The first wall you hit at scale is an OOM crash from the n × n matrix (and a growing KV cache, which the next module covers). FlashAttention saves memory IO but does not reduce FLOPs — your compute bill is unchanged. If you need to cut compute, that means sparse or sliding-window attention, which lower the work by approximating the result instead of computing every pair exactly.

Training does all positions at once, so it is bound by the n × n compute you just measured. Inference reveals a completely different bottleneck — generating one token at a time, where the cost is loading the KV cache from memory every step. That is the next module.

06

Inference & the KV Cache

Why generating an answer is bottlenecked by re-reading, not by math — and how production systems fight back.

The model re-reads everything, every single word

When a model writes you an answer, it does it one token at a time. Before it writes each new token, it re-reads everything it has said so far. That re-reading, not the math, is the bottleneck.

✍️
Metaphor: a scribe taking dictation

Imagine a scribe writing down a long story word by word. Before adding each new word, the scribe re-reads the entire transcript so far to stay consistent. The pile of pages already written is the KV cache. Re-reading the whole pile before every new word is the cost we will study. The analogy breaks down in one place: a real scribe could just remember the recent context, but the model literally reloads the whole cache from memory at every step.

This module is the finale. You already know what attention computes. Now you will learn why the same operation behaves like two completely different machines: a fast parallel one while training, and a memory-starved sequential one while inference generates text.

One operation, two very different machines

The attention formula does not change between training and generation. What changes is the shape of the inputs — and that single difference flips the bottleneck from compute to memory.

⚡

Training — parallel

All N tokens are known up front. Q, K, and V are full N × d matrices. The whole N × N attention grid is one big parallel matmul. The GPU is doing heavy arithmetic, so this phase is compute-bound: speed is limited by how many math operations per second the chip can do.

🔁

Decode — autoregressive

Only one new token is born per step. Its Query is a single 1 × d row. The past Keys and Values are kept in a KV cache and just appended to, never recomputed. Per-token math is tiny, but every step you must re-read the whole cache from memory, so this phase is memory-bandwidth-bound.

The phrase to lock in: autoregressive generation.

💡
Key insight: the bottleneck moved, not the math

An autoregressive decode step does almost no arithmetic — a 1 × d query against the cache. The expensive part is HBM bandwidth: loading the whole growing cache from memory into the chip on every single step. A system can be memory-bandwidth bound while the math units sit mostly idle.

Why this is the wall

The cache size grows with sequence length × layers × heads × head-dimension. At step 1000 you re-read roughly 1000 rows; at step 8000 you re-read 8000. The arithmetic per step barely moved, but the bytes moved from HBM keep climbing. That is why long answers feel slower toward the end.

The one line that makes generation left-to-right

Why can the model only ever go forward, one token at a time? Because of a causal mask. Here is the exact line from the reference implementation. Read the plain-English column on the right.

CODE 
scores = np.where(mask, scores, -np.inf)
PLAIN ENGLISH

Wherever the mask says "you are allowed to look here," keep the real score.

Wherever it says "this is a future token," replace the score with negative infinity.

After softmax, negative infinity becomes a weight of zero — so a token can never listen to words that come after it. That is what forces generation to run left to right.

🔗
Mask + cache are the same idea, seen twice

During training, the causal mask hides the future inside one big parallel matmul, so every position is trained as if it only saw the past. During decoding, that "only the past exists" rule is automatic — the future tokens have not been generated yet. The KV cache is simply how we avoid recomputing that growing past from scratch on every step.

Five confusions that sink interviews

These are the misreads that separate "I memorized a diagram" from "I understand the mechanism." Get each one straight.

✕
"Attention means the model understands."

No. It computes relevance and routes information. Any understanding lives in the learned weights across the whole stack, not in one softmax.

✕
"If token A attends to B, A copies B's embedding."

No. A receives B's Value — the payload — scaled by the Query·Key weight. Routing decides how much; the Value is what actually flows. Routing is not the same as payload.

✕
"Softmax is taken over the whole matrix."

No. It is row-wise, over the keys. Each query gets its own probability distribution that sums to 1. Get the axis wrong and attention silently turns to nonsense.

✕
"Q, K, and V are three different inputs."

No. In self-attention they are three learned projections of the same input. A token labels itself and decides what it carries.

✕
"FlashAttention is a faster, approximate or sub-quadratic algorithm."

No. FlashAttention gives the exact same output and the same O(n²) compute. It only changes memory IO by never materializing the full n × n score matrix.

The fixes you should be able to name

Once you can say "decode is memory-bandwidth bound because we re-read the KV cache from HBM," every production fix becomes obvious — each one attacks the cache or the bandwidth directly.

🗜️

MQA / GQA

MQA / GQA share K and V across heads, so the cache shrinks. Less to load from HBM each step means a faster decode for the same model.

🧩

PagedAttention / vLLM

PagedAttention stores the cache in small fixed-size blocks, like memory pages. No fragmentation means one GPU can fit far more concurrent requests.

🔢

Quantized cache

Store the cached Keys and Values in fewer bits (for example 8-bit instead of 16-bit). Fewer bytes per row means less to read from HBM each step — directly attacking the bandwidth bottleneck.

🚌

Continuous batching

Continuous batching keeps the GPU full by swapping requests in and out as they finish, so each expensive cache read serves more users.

⚠️
Design trade-off to verbalize

MQA/GQA and a quantized cache both shrink the cache, but they can cost a little quality — sharing K/V across heads or using fewer bits throws away some precision. The staff-level answer names the lever and the price: "GQA cuts the cache and speeds decode, but I would A/B the eval scores before shipping, because sharing K/V can dent quality on long-context tasks." Naming the cost is the signal.
