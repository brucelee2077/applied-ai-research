1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is Flax Linen, and why does size matter today?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Tuesday–Thursday's tokenizer, data generator, and padding logic for the addition task. You've also touched Flax briefly back in Week 1 Friday. No prior deep Flax experience needed.

The problem: raw JAX has no idea of "a layer"

Raw JAX functions are stateless — they take inputs, return outputs, and hold no memory of their own. That's great for jit, grad, and vmap. But a neural network layer needs a weight matrix that persists between calls. Someone has to decide how that weight array gets created, named, stored, and threaded through jax.jit/jax.grad without breaking JAX's purity rules.

The answer: Flax Linen and the Module

Flax Linen (flax.linen, imported as nn) is the neural-network layer library built on top of raw JAX. Its answer to "where do weights live?" is the Module — a class you subclass. Decorate its call method with @nn.compact, and when you write nn.Dense(features=352) inside it, Linen lazily creates a weight matrix of the right shape the first time the module runs on real data.

Linen then hands that array back to you as an explicit parameter pytree — a nested dict of arrays, conventionally called params — instead of hiding it inside the object. You never hold "the model's weights" as secret internal state. You hold params, and you call model.apply({"params": params}, x) to run a forward pass.

📦
params = model.init(rng, dummy_x)["params"]  →  logits = model.apply({"params": params}, x)
params is just a pytree, so jax.grad, jax.jit, and jax.vmap already know how to differentiate, compile, and batch it — zero special-casing.

The second new idea: parameter budgeting

Today's model must land at ~10 million total parameters — not "reasonably sized," but a number you chose. That's because every later scaling-law data point (your own empirically-derived Chinchilla curve, built over the coming weeks) needs models whose parameter count N is precisely known. Today's ~10M is the first rung on a ladder of model sizes you'll build next.

2 · INTUITIONA cargo ship built from a fixed steel budget✓

You've been handed exactly 10 million kilograms of steel — your parameter budget — and told to design a shipping container ship. Every design choice spends steel from the same pile.

🚢📏
d_model = deck width

How wide each deck is — the size of the "workspace" every layer reads from and writes to. A wider deck costs steel quadratically for every deck-to-deck connector (attention's weight matrices scale as d_model²).

🚢🏗️
n_layers = number of decks

How many decks are stacked. Each extra deck costs a fixed, linear slice of the steel budget, and lets cargo get re-sorted and combined more times before reaching the far end.

In one line: you can't add width and depth freely — you have a hard steel ceiling, so today's job is solving the budgeting equation, not picking numbers that sound nice.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real ship's decks are physically identical, but in a Transformer, n_heads (the cargo lanes on each deck) doesn't cost extra steel by itself — splitting one wide attention computation into narrower parallel "lanes" is a free reshape of the same weight matrix, not an added expense. The budget is driven almost entirely by d_model and d_ff (the loading yard) and how many decks you stack — not by how many lanes you slice the deck into.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of parameter counting, and why they matter✓

The rules

  • Attention weights cost 4·d_model² — four square projection matrices W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O, each (d_model, d_model).
  • The MLP sub-layer costs 2·d_model·d_ff — one "up" projection (d_model, d_ff) and one "down" projection (d_ff, d_model).
  • With d_ff = 4·d_model (the common ratio), one full block costs 4d² + 8d² = 12·d_model² — the same heuristic used in Kaplan/Chinchilla-style scaling papers.
  • Stack n_layers blocks and the dominant cost is 12 · n_layers · d_model², plus the smaller embedding tables.
✅
total ≈ 12 · n_layers · d_model² + vocab_size·d_model + max_seq_len·d_model
Solve this for candidate (n_layers, d_model) pairs until the total lands near your target — that's the whole game.

Why it's a superpower

  • Deliberate, not accidental — every dimension is a knob you can later sweep systematically, because you know exactly why each one is the size it is.
  • Cheap to verify — jax.tree_util.tree_leaves(params) gives you the ground-truth count in one line, before you spend any compute training.
  • Composable — because params is a pytree, the model you size today plugs straight into jax.jit, jax.grad, and tomorrow's Optax optimizer with zero extra glue code.

Today's task (from the curriculum): write the actual Flax Linen module, size it to land at ~10 million parameters, and prove the count by walking the real params pytree — not by trusting your hand-derived formula alone.

Acceptance criteria

  • The script runs end-to-end and prints a single integer parameter count.
  • That printed count is within the "~10 million" target — 9.5M–10.5M is acceptable (the curriculum says "~10 million," not exactly 10,000,000).
  • d_model % n_heads == 0 holds for the chosen config, and this doesn't error inside nn.MultiHeadDotProductAttention.
  • A forward pass (model.apply({"params": params}, dummy_batch)) runs without shape errors and returns logits of shape (batch, seq_len, vocab_size).
  • sizing_notes.md's hand-computed total matches the script's printed total — a mismatch means a term (often biases or LayerNorm scale/bias) was missed.
