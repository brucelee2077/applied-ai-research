Phase 2 · Week 7 · Day 4 (Thu)

Distributed MoE Routing
The AllToAll Bottleneck

On one chip, sending a token to "expert 37" is just picking a row of a matrix. Once your 64 experts are spread across 8 or 64 physical chips, that same routing decision means the token's data has to leave its chip and travel over the network to wherever expert 37 lives. Today: the communication pattern that makes this happen, and why it can dominate the cost of running an MoE model.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why expert-parallel MoE needs an AllToAll collective (not AllReduce), describe the dispatch/compute/combine round trip, show why top-2 routing roughly doubles AllToAll traffic versus top-1, and measure a real AllToAll round trip in JAX.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is distributed MoE routing, and why does it need a network hop?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Monday's Mixture-of-Experts fundamentals (experts, the router, top-k) and Wednesday's load-balancing problem (capacity factor, dropped tokens). No prior distributed-systems or networking experience needed.

The setup: experts don't all fit on one chip

Earlier this week you learned that a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces one feed-forward block with many parallel copies — the experts — plus a small router network that sends each token to just one or two of them. A model with 64 or 128 experts, each the size of a full feed-forward layer, has far more total parameters than can fit in the memory of a single GPU or TPU chip.

The standard fix, used by GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) and the Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph, Shazeer, 2021), is called expert parallelism: spread the experts across many chips, one or a few experts per chip. So far this sounds like ordinary model sharding. The twist is what happens to the tokens.

The problem: routing decisions cross chip boundaries

Training and inference process tokens in batches spread across chips (data parallelism). The router's decision for a token is made independently of where that token's data happens to sit. So on every forward pass, a huge number of tokens get routed to an expert that lives on a different physical chip than the one currently holding that token's activation vector.

There is no way around this except one thing: physically move the token's data to the chip that owns the chosen expert, run the expert's math there, then move the result back. That movement is not a side detail — it is a full network communication step, once per MoE layer, on every forward and backward pass.

The mechanism: an AllToAll, not an AllReduce

If you have used distributed training before, you likely know AllReduce: every chip contributes a gradient, and every chip gets back the same summed result. MoE routing needs something different, called AllToAll: every chip sends a different, custom-picked batch of tokens to every other chip — whichever tokens that chip's router decided belong to an expert living there. Nobody receives the same thing. This personalized, all-pairs exchange is what today's lesson is about.

The full round trip has three parts: a dispatch AllToAll (send each token to its expert's chip), the expert compute (a local matrix multiply, no network involved), and a combine AllToAll (send the expert's output back to the token's original chip so the rest of the model can continue). Two network hops for every MoE layer, every step.

2 · INTUITIONA mail-sorting facility with specialists in different cities✓

Imagine a nationwide mail company. Every city has its own sorting office (a chip). But the company's specialists — the one person in the whole company who knows how to process a specific rare form — are not copied into every city. Each specialist sits in exactly one city (an expert lives on one chip).

📬➡️✈️
Dispatch = the outgoing flight

Every morning, every city sorts its letters by which specialist they need, then ships each bundle by air to the city that has that specialist. Every city sends a different bundle to every other city — nobody gets the same shipment.

🏢✍️➡️✈️
Combine = the return flight

Once the specialist processes a letter, the answer has to fly all the way back to the letter's home city, because that's where the customer (the rest of the model) is waiting for it.

In one line: the specialist's actual work (the expert's matrix multiply) is fast and local, but the two flights — there and back — are where the time and cost really go, especially as more cities (chips) get involved.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real flights are scheduled once a day, but the dispatch-compute-combine round trip happens on every single MoE layer, for every single batch, during both the forward and backward pass. And unlike separate flights, chips in a real cluster share the same network fabric, so many layers' AllToAll traffic can be competing for the same wires at once.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of AllToAll routing, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • Expert parallelism shards experts across N chips (usually a handful to a few dozen experts per chip). Each chip owns a fixed slice of experts and never moves them; tokens move instead.
  • AllToAll is a collective where every one of the N chips sends a distinct chunk of data to every other chip, and receives a distinct chunk back — an N×N personalized exchange, unlike AllReduce's "same answer to everyone."
  • Each MoE layer needs two AllToAlls: dispatch (token activations → the chip holding the chosen expert) and combine (expert outputs → the chip the token came from). Both happen in the forward pass; the backward pass needs the mirror-image AllToAlls for gradients.
  • Every chip must first pad or truncate to a fixed expert capacity (Wednesday's lesson) so the AllToAll message sizes are static and known ahead of time — a hard requirement of most distributed collectives, which expect fixed-size buffers, not a different size every step.
  • The payload size scales directly with top-k: each token is dispatched once per chosen expert, so top-2 routing (GShard-style) sends roughly 2x the token data across the network that top-1 routing (Switch-style) does, for the same batch.

Why it's the real bottleneck

  • Compute is cheap, communication is not — the expert's matrix multiply, once the tokens have arrived, is the same cheap FLOPs-per-active-param math from Friday's lesson. The AllToAll is pure data movement with zero useful FLOPs of its own.
  • Bisection bandwidth, not FLOPs, becomes the limit — as you add more chips (and thus more experts), the AllToAll has to move data across more network hops, and clusters have far less bandwidth between nodes (InfiniBand, Ethernet) than within a node (NVLink). A dense model rarely stresses this because AllReduce traffic is smaller and easier to overlap with compute; MoE's AllToAll pattern is harder to hide.
  • Load imbalance makes it worse — if the router sends more tokens to some chips than others (the load-balancing problem from Wednesday), those chips both compute more and receive more network traffic, so a routing skew becomes a communication skew too.
🔗
Causal chain: too many total params to fit on one chip → experts get sharded across chips (expert parallelism) → the router's per-token choice ignores physical chip boundaries → tokens must physically travel to their expert's chip and back → that travel is an AllToAll collective → AllToAll is network-bound, not compute-bound, so cluster topology (not just chip FLOPs) sets the real speed limit for MoE.

7 · PRODUCEMap and measure the AllToAll overhead in distributed MoE routing✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): map the network communication overhead generated when tokens must cross physical device boundaries to reach specific experts, then actually measure it.

Acceptance criteria

  • simulate_routing.py — a router (using jax.random) assigns a synthetic batch (configurable batch size B, expert count E, chip count N) to experts, and prints, per chip, tokens sent, tokens received, and per-destination message sizes. Total tokens sent across all chips must equal total tokens in the batch, accounting for any dropped tokens per Wednesday's capacity-factor lesson.
  • measure_alltoall.py — uses jax.lax.all_to_all (via jax.pmap, runnable on CPU with XLA_FLAGS=--xla_force_host_platform_device_count=8) to execute a real dispatch-and-combine round trip on synthetic token data, and times it with a printed wall-clock number.
  • sweep_results.csv — sweeps chip count N (e.g. 2/4/8) and batch size, recording measured AllToAll wall-clock time and computed theoretical payload size per row, with at least 3 rows of non-identical timings.
  • writeup.md — states the measured relationship between N and per-device AllToAll time, reproduces the top-1 vs. top-2 payload comparison from Demo ③ with real sweep numbers, and connects the result to why frontier labs care about network topology (NVLink/InfiniBand) specifically for MoE, not just raw compute.
