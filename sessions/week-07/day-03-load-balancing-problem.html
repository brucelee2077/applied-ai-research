Phase 2 · Week 7 · Day 3 (Wed)

The Load Balancing Problem
Why Routers Collapse

A trained router should spread tokens roughly evenly across experts. Left alone, it does the opposite: it learns to send almost everything to a handful of favorite experts and starve the rest. Today: why that collapse happens, why it's a vicious cycle once it starts, and the auxiliary loss that Switch Transformer and GShard both use to stop it.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why router collapse is self-reinforcing, state the Switch Transformer auxiliary loss loss_aux = α · N · Σ(f_i · P_i) and why its minimum is exactly α, and connect expert capacity + token dropping to why a fixed load-balancing loss alone isn't the whole story.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is "load balancing," and why does an MoE router need it?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Days 1–2's MoE fundamentals (experts, router, top-k, sparse activation) and the idea of active vs. total parameters. No prior load-balancing math needed.

Quick recap: what does the router actually do?

Each Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer has a small router network. For every token, the router outputs a score for each expert, turns those scores into probabilities with a softmax, and then sends the token to the top-k highest-scoring experts (Switch Transformer uses k=1, GShard uses k=2). Only the chosen experts do any work on that token — this is what keeps MoE's per-token compute cheap even though the total parameter count across all experts is huge.

What is "load balancing," and why is it a problem at all?

Load balancing means making sure tokens get spread out roughly evenly across all the available experts, instead of piling up on just a few. You might assume a trained router would naturally learn to do this — after all, spreading load seems like the "smart" thing to do. It does not happen by default. Left to train with only the language-modeling loss, routers tend to fall into routing collapse: a small number of experts start getting picked slightly more often (maybe just by random initialization), those experts then get more gradient updates and become slightly better at handling whatever tokens they see, which makes the router even more likely to pick them next time. Within a few thousand steps, most of the expert capacity can end up unused while 1 or 2 experts absorb the majority of tokens.

So what's today's catch?

The fix is not "hope harder." Fedus et al.'s Switch Transformer paper (2021) and Lepikhin et al.'s GShard paper (2020) both add a second loss term — an auxiliary load-balancing loss — that is added to the ordinary language-modeling loss during training. It directly penalizes uneven routing, so the optimizer is pushed to spread tokens across experts even though nothing about next-token prediction alone would ask for that. There's a second, complementary piece too: even with the auxiliary loss nudging things toward balance, real batches are never perfectly even, so systems also give each expert a hard capacity limit and simply drop tokens that arrive after an expert is full. We'll build both pieces today.

📚
Primary sources (optional): Fedus et al. — Switch Transformer (arXiv:2101.03961) · Lepikhin et al. — GShard (arXiv:2006.16668)
2 · INTUITIONThe restaurant with one popular waiter✓

Picture a restaurant with 8 waiters and a host who seats every new table.

🙋‍♂️🍽️
The collapse: one waiter gets all the tables

Early on, one waiter happens to get a couple of easy tables and does well. The host starts noticing he's "good," and sends him more tables. He gets even more practice, gets even better and faster, and the host sends him still more. Meanwhile the other 7 waiters stand around getting no practice at all — they never improve, because they never get customers.

🎫🙋‍♀️🙋‍♂️🙋‍♀️
The fix: a ticket-based cap per waiter

The manager hands out a limited number of table-tickets to each waiter per shift, and also scores the host on how evenly tickets got used — not just on how happy customers were. Now the host is pushed to spread new tables around, and every waiter gets enough practice to actually get good.

In one line: a router that is only rewarded for good predictions will happily send everything to its early favorites — you have to reward it separately for spreading tokens out, and also hard-cap how much any one expert can take.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: the "manager's score" (the auxiliary loss) isn't a separate rule bolted on after training — it's summed directly into the same loss the router's weights get gradients from, every single step. And the "ticket cap" (expert capacity) isn't a fairness policy — it's a hard memory/compute limit: an expert's per-batch buffer physically cannot hold more than its capacity, so anything past that limit doesn't wait in line, it gets dropped and skips that layer entirely.

4 · HOW & WHYThe auxiliary loss, and why its minimum is exactly α✓

The rules

  • For a batch of T tokens routed across N experts, define two per-expert quantities: f_i = the fraction of tokens actually dispatched to expert i (a hard count, via argmax/top-k), and P_i = the mean router probability mass expert i received across the batch (a soft, differentiable average of the softmax output).
  • The Switch Transformer auxiliary loss is loss_aux = α · N · Σ_i (f_i · P_i), summed over all N experts, with α a small fixed hyperparameter (Switch Transformer uses α = 0.01).
  • f_i alone isn't differentiable (argmax has no useful gradient), so the loss multiplies it by the differentiable P_i — this is exactly how the loss can push gradients back into the router's weights even though the actual token-to-expert assignment is a hard, non-differentiable choice.
  • Both f_i and P_i are probability-like: Σ_i f_i = 1 and Σ_i P_i = 1. Perfectly uniform routing means f_i = P_i = 1/N for every expert.
✅
loss_aux = α · N · Σ_i (f_i · P_i), minimized at uniform routing where it equals exactly α
Plug in f_i = P_i = 1/N: the sum has N terms, each of size (1/N)·(1/N), so the total is N · (1/N²) = 1/N. Multiply by α · N: α · N · (1/N) = α. That's the constant floor — loss_aux can never go below α, and it only reaches α when routing is perfectly even. Any skew away from uniform strictly increases it above α.

Why it matters

  • It fights a genuinely self-reinforcing failure — without it, a slight early imbalance compounds: more tokens → more gradient signal → better expert → even more tokens. The auxiliary loss adds constant pressure in the opposite direction, every step, so imbalance can't freely compound.
  • Capacity is the second, independent safety net — the auxiliary loss shapes training incentives, but any single batch can still be locally uneven by chance. Systems set expert_capacity = (T/N) · capacity_factor (a hard buffer size per expert) and drop tokens once an expert's buffer is full, tracking a drop_rate. A higher capacity_factor (e.g. 1.25 or 1.5 instead of 1.0) means bigger buffers and fewer dropped tokens, at the cost of more allocated (and often wasted) compute/memory per expert.
  • Production consequence — dropped tokens don't get an error; the model just skips that expert's transformation for them that layer (often via a residual/identity pass-through), silently degrading quality on whichever tokens got unlucky. Tuning α and capacity_factor together is a real trade-off: too little balancing pressure risks collapse, too much can slightly hurt the primary language-modeling loss, and too tight a capacity risks dropping a lot of tokens.
🔗
Causal chain: random early imbalance → favored experts get more gradient signal → they get better → router favors them even more → collapse, unless the auxiliary loss's constant pressure toward uniform f_i, P_i counteracts it, and a hard per-expert capacity limit catches whatever imbalance survives training.

⚠️
The gotcha: don't confuse the two mechanisms. The auxiliary loss is a training-time incentive that shapes the router's weights over many steps — it can't guarantee any single batch is perfectly balanced. Expert capacity is an inference/forward-pass-time hard limit that drops tokens no matter how well-trained the router is, whenever a batch happens to be locally uneven. A well-trained router (low loss_aux) still needs a capacity buffer greater than 1.0x to avoid meaningful drop rates in practice.
