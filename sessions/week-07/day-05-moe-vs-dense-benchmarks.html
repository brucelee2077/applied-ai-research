MoE vs. Dense
Benchmarks

A dense model's total parameters and active parameters are the same number. A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model splits them apart on purpose. Today: put real numbers on that split, and find out what it means for the compute bill and the memory bill.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: compute total vs. active parameters for a 64-expert top-1 MoE layer, show why MoE training FLOPs track active (not total) params, and correctly apply Chinchilla's ~20-tokens-per-parameter rule to an MoE model without the common total-params mistake.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is the active-vs-total parameter split?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Switch Transformer's sparse-routing idea and Chinchilla's scaling-law intuition from earlier this week. No prior benchmarking experience needed.

Dense: one number does both jobs

A dense transformer is the "normal" kind: every parameter in the model touches every token that passes through it. If the model has 70 billion parameters, processing one token means doing math with (activating) all 70 billion of them. There is only one number that matters: total parameters, and it is also the active parameters.

MoE: the same layer, arranged differently

A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model rearranges the feed-forward layers you already know. Instead of one feed-forward block per layer, you build several parallel copies of it — called experts — plus a tiny extra network called the router (or gate). The router looks at each token and sends it to a small subset of experts (often just 1 or 2 out of dozens). The other experts sit idle for that token.

This splits one number into two:

  • Total parameters — every weight that exists, summed across every expert. This is what you need enough GPU/TPU memory (HBM) to store.
  • Active parameters — the weights actually multiplied against a given token on one forward pass. This drives the FLOPs (floating-point operations) cost of that pass, which drives wall-clock speed.

Why bother splitting them?

MoE exists because of a specific bet: model quality scales mainly with total parameters (more distinct knowledge slots), while compute cost scales with active parameters (how much math per token). Decouple the two, and you get a model that "knows" as much as a much bigger dense model while costing as much to run as a much smaller one. The Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph, Shazeer, 2021) made this practical at scale: it simplified routing to top-1 (each token picks exactly one expert) and reported pretraining trillion-parameter sparse models with the same or lower compute as dense models an order of magnitude smaller — up to 7x pretraining speedups at matched compute.

Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) is the reference point for what "compute-optimal" means for a dense model: for a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should scale together, at roughly 20 training tokens per parameter (their headline model: 70B parameters, ~1.4 trillion tokens). That ratio is the dense yardstick we'll compare an MoE model's active parameters against.

2 · INTUITIONA hospital, not a lone GP✓

Think of a single general practitioner (GP) versus a large hospital.

🩺
Dense = one GP

The GP personally looks at every patient, no matter what they need — sprained ankle or heart arrhythmia. 100% of the GP's skill is on duty for every visit. Total skill used = total skill that exists.

🏥
MoE = a hospital with triage

64 specialists (experts) plus a triage nurse (the router). A patient sees exactly one or two specialists. The hospital's total knowledge is enormous, but the effort spent on any one patient is still just one or two specialists' worth.

In one line: the hospital "knows" vastly more collectively while the cost of treating any single patient stays small — that gap is exactly the total-vs-active parameter split.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real specialists have irregular caseloads, but in an MoE model the router must actively balance load across experts every batch. If triage keeps sending everyone to the same two specialists — a real failure mode called router collapse or load imbalance — the other 62 sit completely idle and their capacity is wasted. MoE papers spend real effort on auxiliary load-balancing losses to prevent this.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of the active/total split, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • Total params = every weight stored, summed across the dense parts plus all experts. This is your memory (HBM) bill.
  • Active params = the weights that actually multiply against one token: dense parts plus the top-k chosen experts (usually k=1 for Switch-style routing). This is your FLOPs bill.
  • Training FLOPs approximation: FLOPs ≈ 6 × N_active × D, where D is training tokens. The 6 comes from ~2 FLOPs/param/token for the forward pass, times 3 for forward+backward.
  • Chinchilla's ~20-tokens-per-parameter compute-optimal ratio was derived from dense models, where total = active. For MoE, apply that ratio to active params — never to total params.
✅
N_active = dense_part + k × ffn_params_per_expert
"Dense parts (attention/embedding) always activate, plus whichever k experts the router picked for this token." Everything else in the layer sits idle for that token.

Why it's a big deal

  • Decoupled scaling — quality tracks total params (more knowledge slots); compute tracks active params (FLOPs per token). Split them, and you buy capacity without buying compute.
  • Real speedups — Switch Transformer reported pretraining trillion-parameter sparse models at the same or lower compute as dense models 10x smaller.
  • Real cost — the "free" capacity isn't free in memory: total params still need HBM for weights and optimizer states, so an MoE model can need many times the memory footprint of a dense model with the same compute bill.
🔗
Causal chain: Switch Transformer's top-1 routing (earlier this week) → splits total from active params → compute (FLOPs) tracks active only → Chinchilla's tokens-per-param ratio must use active params too, or you'll badly misjudge how "trained" the model is.
