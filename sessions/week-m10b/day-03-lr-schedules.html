Module 10b · Scale · Day 3

Learning-Rate Schedules

The learning rate is the size of each training step. You might think you set it once and leave it. But the best runs change it over time: start tiny, ramp up, hold steady, then fade to almost zero at the end. A fresh model hates a big first step. Today you learn why warmup exists, two common schedule shapes, and why a bigger batch needs a bigger learning rate.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why warmup is needed, tell apart the cosine and WSD (warmup-stable-decay) schedules, sketch a warmup-then-decay curve from numbers, and state the learning-rate ↔ batch-size coupling rule.
1 · WHAT IS ITThe step size changes over the whole run✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Day 1 you know the learning rate scales the step size, and too big a step can spike. Today we plan how the learning rate should change from the first step to the last.

The learning rate is not one fixed number

A learning-rate schedule is a plan for how the step size moves across the whole run. The best runs do not use one value the whole time. They shape it: small at first, bigger in the middle, tiny at the end.

Why warmup — a cold model hates a big first step

At the start the weights are random, so the gradients are large and messy. A big step now would fling the weights somewhere terrible (a spike). Warmup starts the learning rate near zero and raises it slowly over the first few hundred or thousand steps. The model settles before it is pushed hard.

Two common shapes: cosine and WSD

After warmup the rate holds high, then decays. A cosine schedule curves smoothly down like the right half of a hill. A WSD schedule (warmup-stable-decay) instead holds the rate flat for most of the run, then drops it fast at the very end.

Why fade at the end — the marathon kick

Near the end you want small, careful steps to settle into a good minimum. A big step now would bounce you out of it. So both schedules bring the rate close to zero by the finish. And there is one more knob: batch size, which you must couple to the learning rate. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONPacing a marathon✓
🏃
The runner

A good marathon runner does not sprint from the gun. They ease in slowly to warm the muscles (warmup), then settle into a strong steady pace for most of the race (the stable middle), and save a final kick for the end — except in training the "kick" is the opposite: you slow to careful, tiny steps to land exactly on the finish line.

⏱️
Pace = step size

The pace is the learning rate. Too fast at the start and you pull a muscle (a spike). Too fast at the end and you overshoot the line. The schedule is the coach's pacing plan for the whole race.

In one line: a learning-rate schedule paces training like a marathon — ease in (warmup), hold a strong pace (stable), then wind down to careful small steps (decay) to settle on a good answer.

Cosine and WSD are two pacing plans. Cosine winds down gradually the whole second half. WSD holds the pace flat almost to the end, then drops it quickly — handy when you might extend the race or stop early.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a runner's real finish is a sprint, a big final effort. Training does the reverse at the end — it takes smaller and smaller steps. The "warm up, hold, wind down" shape matches; the direction of the final push does not.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYA warmup-then-decay curve, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

The schedule has two parts. During warmup, the learning rate climbs in a straight line from near zero up to the peak. After warmup, it curves back down toward zero following a cosine shape. At any step you just read the rate off this plan.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
if t < W: lr = lr_max × (t / W) # warmup: straight ramp up
if t ≥ W: lr = lr_max × 0.5 × (1 + cos(π × (t−W)/(T−W))) # cosine decay

Symbols: t = the current step. W = warmup length (how many steps to ramp). T = total steps in the run. lr_max = the peak learning rate. π ≈ 3.14159. During warmup, t/W goes from 0 to 1, so lr rises from 0 to lr_max. After warmup, the cosine term goes from 1 down to 0, so lr falls from lr_max to 0.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say lr_max = 0.001, warmup W = 100, total T = 500. Read the rate at three steps:

🔢
t = 50 (in warmup): lr = 0.001 × (50/100) = 0.0005 # halfway up the ramp
t = 100 (peak): lr = 0.001 × (100/100) = 0.001 # top of the ramp
t = 300 (decay): progress = (300−100)/(500−100) = 0.5
  lr = 0.001 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(π×0.5)) = 0.001 × 0.5 × (1+0) = 0.0005
t = 500 (end): lr = 0.001 × 0.5 × (1 + cos(π)) = 0.001 × 0.5 × 0 = 0.0

At step 50 the rate is halfway up. At step 100 it hits the peak. At the halfway point of decay (step 300) it is back to half the peak. By the end it reaches zero. That is the full warmup-then-cosine curve, read from the plan.

The LR ↔ batch-size coupling

A bigger batch gives a smoother, more trustworthy gradient. Because you trust it more, you can safely take a bigger step. A common rule: scale the learning rate up with the batch size.

🧮
lr_new = lr_base × (batch_new / batch_base) # linear scaling rule
# double the batch → double the learning rate (roughly)
⚠️
The linear rule works up to a point. Past a large batch, doubling the learning rate again starts to cause spikes. Warmup gets more important as batch and learning rate grow, because the risky first steps get even bigger.
🔗
The big picture: the schedule and the batch size are the two dials that set how hard you push at each moment. Set them well and you avoid most spikes without any recovery. Day 4 asks the deeper question: how do you pick lr_max and friends for a giant model without paying to train it many times?
