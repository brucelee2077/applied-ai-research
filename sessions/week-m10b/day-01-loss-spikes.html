Module 10b · Scale · Day 1

Loss Spikes

You start a big training run. The loss falls smoothly for 40,000 steps. Then, without warning, it jumps to the sky — the model seems to forget everything it learned. This is a loss spike, and every large model hits them. Today you learn what a spike is, the three usual causes, and the calm fix: roll back and skip. Panic makes it worse. A plan makes it routine.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: say what a loss spike is in one sentence, name its three common causes (a bad batch, too-high learning rate, and fp16 overflow), and describe the recovery — roll back to the last good checkpoint and skip the offending batch.
1 · WHAT IS ITThe loss jumps up out of nowhere✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know from Module 2 that training uses an optimizer and a learning rate to take small steps that lower the loss, and from Modules 9a/9b that numbers can be stored in low precision like fp16. Today we look at what happens when one of those steps goes wrong.

What "loss" is doing during training

The loss is one number that says how wrong the model is. During a healthy run it slides down smoothly, step after step. You watch its curve like a heartbeat monitor. Down and steady is good.

The scary moment: a spike

A loss spike is a sudden sharp jump in that curve. One step the loss is 2.1, the next it is 9.0 or NaN. The model took one bad step and hurt itself. On a run that costs millions of dollars, this is the moment engineers dread.

Why one bad step can ruin things

Each step nudges the model's weights a little. If a step is far too big, or built from broken numbers, it can push the weights into a bad place. From there the loss shoots up. The bigger and longer the run, the more chances for this to happen — so spikes are not rare, they are expected.

The one thing to remember today

A spike is not the end of the run. There is a calm, standard fix: go back to the last saved good version of the model and skip the step that caused the trouble. You do not throw the whole run away. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONA car skid on a wet road✓
🚗
The skid

You are driving steadily. You hit a wet patch and the car slides sideways for a moment. That is the spike. A good driver does not panic and slam the brakes. They ease off, steer gently, and get back on track. A panicked driver over-corrects and crashes.

🎯
The gentle fix

Training the same way: when the loss skids up, you do not throw out the whole trip. You back up to the last safe spot on the road (the last good checkpoint) and drive past the slippery patch (skip the bad batch) more carefully.

In one line: a loss spike is a skid — recognize it early, stay calm, roll back to safe ground, and steer past the thing that caused it.

The wet patch has three usual forms: a strange piece of data (a bad batch), driving too fast (a learning rate that is too high), or a broken speedometer (low-precision numbers overflowing). You will meet all three next.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a car skid happens to the whole car at once. A loss spike often comes from one specific step, so you can name it, roll back to just before it, and skip exactly that batch — something a real driver cannot do with the road.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYWhy one bad step blows up the loss✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Each training step changes a weight by moving it a small amount in the direction that lowers the loss. The size of that move is the learning rate times the gradient. If the gradient is suddenly huge, or the learning rate is too big, the move is enormous and it throws the weight far from any good value.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
w_new = w_old − lr × g
# w = a weight, lr = learning rate, g = gradient (slope of loss)

Symbols: w_old = the weight before the step. lr = the learning rate, a small fixed number like 0.001. g = the gradient, how steeply the loss changes. Multiply lr × g to get the move, and subtract it. Small lr × g = a gentle, safe step. Huge lr × g = a wild jump.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say a weight is 0.5 and the learning rate is 0.01. Compare a normal gradient with a spiking one:

🔢
normal: w_new = 0.5 − 0.01 × 3 = 0.5 − 0.03 = 0.47 # tiny, safe
spiking: w_new = 0.5 − 0.01 × 90000 = 0.5 − 900 = −899.5 # way off the map

The normal step barely moves the weight. But when a bad batch or an overflow makes the gradient 90000, the same learning rate throws the weight to −899.5. Every weight that got such a shove is now garbage, and the loss leaps up. That is the spike, in one line of arithmetic.

The three usual causes

  • A bad batch: one batch holds strange or broken data (a corrupt example, a huge outlier). It makes a giant gradient g.
  • Learning rate too high: even a normal gradient becomes a wild step if lr is too big. This is why warmup and schedules (Day 3) exist.
  • fp16 overflow: in low precision, a number bigger than about 65,504 becomes NaN or infinity. That poisons the gradient and the step explodes.
🔗
The big picture: the fix follows straight from the formula. If one step's lr × g did the damage, undo it: reload the last good checkpoint (get w_old back) and skip the batch that made g huge. Days 2 and 3 add guardrails so fewer steps ever go this wrong.
