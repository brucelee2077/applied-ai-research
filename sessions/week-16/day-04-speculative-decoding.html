Phase 4 · Week 16 · Day 4 (Thu)

Speculative Decoding
Draft, Then Verify in Parallel

Generating text one token at a time is slow — not because the math is hard, but because every step has to wait for the last one to finish, and each wait means streaming billions of weights from memory. Speculative decoding lets a small, cheap "draft" model guess several tokens ahead, so the big model can check them all in a single parallel pass instead of one slow step at a time.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound, walk through the draft → verify → accept/reject-and-resample loop exactly, compute acceptance probabilities and expected speedup by hand, and simulate the whole algorithm in code to prove it changes nothing about the output distribution.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is speculative decoding, and why does it exist?✓
🧭
What this assumes: basic Python/NumPy, and a rough sense that big language models generate text token by token. No prior decoding-systems knowledge needed — we define every term as it appears.

The problem: generating text is stubbornly sequential

A large language model (LLM) generates text one token (roughly, one word-piece) at a time. To predict token number t+1, the model needs tokens 1 through t already decided. Then it must wait for that new token before it can even start predicting t+2. This is called autoregressive generation — each step depends on the one before it, so you cannot skip ahead.

Each one of those steps is a full forward pass through every layer of the model — for a large model, that can mean streaming tens of billions of parameters. Here's the part almost nobody guesses correctly: on modern GPUs and TPUs, a single decode step is usually memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. That means most of the time is spent moving the model's weights and its cached memory of past tokens (the KV cache, "key-value cache") from HBM (High Bandwidth Memory — the accelerator's main memory) into the fast on-chip memory where the actual math happens. The multiplications themselves are almost free by comparison; the wait is for data to arrive.

The insight that unlocks the whole trick

If a decode step is bottlenecked by streaming weights, not by doing the math, then something surprising follows: running that same step on 5 candidate token positions costs almost the same wall-clock time as running it on just 1 position. You pay the weight-streaming cost once either way, and the extra multiplications for the other 4 positions are nearly free. Speculative decoding exploits exactly this asymmetry.

The recipe: a small, fast draft model guesses several tokens ahead, cheaply and sequentially (it's small, so each of its steps is fast). Then the large target model — the one whose output you actually want — checks all of those guesses in one single forward pass, because a transformer can compute its output for every position in a sequence simultaneously in one pass. This is exactly what makes training parallel across a whole sequence; decoding is normally the odd one out because it's usually done one token at a time. Speculative decoding brings that same parallelism to decoding, just for verification instead of generation.

How this connects to what you've already studied

This closes out the Phase 4 arc. Pallas kernels attacked the bandwidth bottleneck by writing faster memory-access patterns. Quantization attacked it by shrinking the weights so there's less to stream. GQA/MQA (grouped/multi-query attention) attacked it by shrinking the KV cache. Speculative decoding attacks the same bottleneck from a completely different angle: instead of moving less data, it makes each expensive data movement produce more than one token's worth of progress.

So what's today's catch?

The catch is: how do you combine a "sloppy but fast" guesser with a "correct but slow" checker, and still guarantee the final text is exactly what the big model would have produced on its own — not an approximation? That guarantee comes from a precise acceptance rule, which is today's main mechanism to learn.

2 · INTUITIONThe executive and the fast-drafting assistant✓

Picture a busy executive (the target model) who must approve every sentence of a report before it ships. Each time she reads one sentence and signs off, there's a long walk back to her office to fetch the next one — that walk is the round-trip to HBM to fetch weights and the KV cache.

🚶‍♀️📄
One sentence at a time

She reads, signs, walks, reads, signs, walks — for every single sentence. Correct, but painfully slow, because the "walk" (memory fetch) dominates the time, not the "reading" (the actual compute).

🏃‍♂️📑➡️👩‍💼
Hire a fast drafting assistant

A junior assistant (the draft model) writes the next five sentences in one go, in the style she'd want. She skims all five in a single pass and, for each one, decides: keep it, or stop here and write her own version instead — discarding everything the assistant wrote after that point.

In one line: a cheap assistant proposes several steps ahead; the expensive decision-maker checks them all in one pass instead of one at a time.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: the executive's "read five sentences in one pass" feels like fast human skimming, but for a GPU it's parallel purely because of memory-bandwidth economics — streaming the weights once and reusing them for 5 positions is nearly as cheap as streaming them for 1, which has no clean human analogue (a person reading 5 sentences really does take about 5x longer than reading 1). Also, real assistants learn from rejected drafts — the draft model here gets no such feedback. It's a fixed, separate model with no learning signal from what gets rejected.

4 · HOW & WHYThe exact acceptance rule, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • The draft model M_q autoregressively samples K candidate tokens (usually K=4–8) from its own distribution q, one cheap step at a time.
  • The target model M_p runs one forward pass over the prompt plus all K drafted tokens, producing its true distribution p at every one of those positions simultaneously.
  • For each drafted token x, left to right: accept it with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)). If the target model liked x at least as much as the draft model did, acceptance is guaranteed.
  • On rejection at some position, do not just discard and retry — resample a replacement from the residual distribution norm(max(0, p(x) − q(x))), then stop; every draft token after that point is discarded too.
  • If all K tokens are accepted, sample one bonus token from the target's own distribution at the final position — it's "free" because the verification pass already computed it.
✅
a_i = min(1, p_i(x) / q_i(x))
"Accept the draft token with probability equal to how much more (or less) the target model liked it than the draft model did — capped at 1." This single rule is what makes the output distribution exact.

Why it's a superpower

  • Exact, not approximate — the modified rejection sampling scheme (the paper's real contribution) guarantees the final token stream is distributed exactly as if you sampled from the target model alone, token by token. No retraining, no architecture change, no quality loss.
  • Fast because of bandwidth economics — one big forward pass typically yields more than one accepted token, so you need far fewer expensive target-model passes per generated token.
  • Composable with earlier Phase 4 material — an int8/int4-quantized copy of the target model is a popular draft model choice: it shares the target's "style" closely (higher acceptance) while being cheaper to run per token.
🔗
Causal chain: decode steps are bandwidth-bound (Pallas/quantization/GQA territory) → verifying K positions in one pass costs almost the same as verifying 1 → a cheap draft model can propose K guesses → an exact accept/resample rule lets the target model check them without changing what it would have said → fewer expensive passes per token, same output.

7 · PRODUCESimulate the accept/reject loop and prove it's exact✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): study how an auxiliary, highly compressed draft model can rapidly generate speculative tokens for a larger model to verify in parallel — the concrete, checkable artifact is a small reproducible simulation of the speculative decoding accept/reject loop (not a full LLM integration, which is out of scope for one study day).

Acceptance criteria

  • Toy categorical target distribution p and toy categorical draft distribution q over a small vocabulary (50–100 symbols), where q is deliberately a coarser/cheaper approximation of p (e.g. p is a mixture of two modes, q is a single unimodal approximation).
  • A draft_and_verify(p, q, K) function implementing the walkthrough exactly: draft K tokens from q, compute per-position acceptance min(1, p/q), accept/reject/resample from the residual, return accepted length and tokens.
  • Distributional correctness: run ≥100,000 rounds of the speculative sampler and of plain sampling from p; a KL-divergence or chi-squared check between the two empirical histograms must print pass/fail against a stated threshold (e.g. KL < 0.01).
  • Speedup formula validation: sweep K in {1,2,4,8}, measure acceptance rate α empirically, and print expected accepted-tokens-per-round both empirically and via (1-α^(K+1))/(1-α), confirming agreement within ~5%.
  • Script runs end-to-end with python3 experiments/week16_jax/speculative_decoding_sim.py and exits 0, printing both validation results.
⚠️
Common misunderstanding: the speedup does not come from trusting a "close enough" draft model — that would be an approximation/quality trade-off. The modified rejection sampling scheme makes the final output distribution mathematically exact no matter how bad the draft model is. A worse draft only lowers α (and the speedup) — it never changes correctness. Also watch the bonus-token bookkeeping in step 12 of the walkthrough: you get K+1 tokens per round when everything is accepted, not just K.
