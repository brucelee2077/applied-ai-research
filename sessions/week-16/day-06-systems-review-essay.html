The Hardware Lottery
Systems Review Essay

Four weeks of kernels, quantization, and decoding tricks all trace back to one number: how many FLOPs you do per byte you move. Today you write the essay that proves it — tying roofline analysis, FlashAttention, ThunderKittens, and Pope et al.'s inference scaling into a single falsifiable argument.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: define arithmetic intensity and the ridge point precisely, explain why FlashAttention and decode sit on opposite sides of the roofline, connect ThunderKittens' tile abstraction to the same memory-hierarchy constraint, and draft a comprehensive essay naming which architectures won or lost the hardware lottery.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is "the hardware lottery," and what is arithmetic intensity?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Weeks 13–16 — you've seen roofline plots, FlashAttention, ThunderKittens, and Ring Attention. No new mechanism today. Today's job is naming the one variable that explains all of them.

The problem: why did some ideas win and others lose?

Over the last four weeks you learned a pile of separate-seeming tricks: tile Q/K/V into SRAM, write kernels with a tile-shaped DSL, shrink the KV cache, batch decode requests. Each one felt like its own clever idea. Today's essay asks a harder question: is there one underlying reason all of these tricks exist, and one reason certain "smarter" ideas never got adopted at all?

The answer has a name: the hardware lottery, a term coined by researcher Sara Hooker in 2021. It says an algorithm's success depends heavily on how well it fits the hardware and software stack available at the time — almost independent of how elegant the idea is on paper.

The shared variable: arithmetic intensity

Arithmetic intensity is the ratio of math done per byte moved from memory:

📦
Intensity = Total FLOPs / Total Bytes moved from HBM
FLOPs = floating-point operations (the "math"). Bytes = data read from or written to memory. Every paper on this week's reading list is really one more data point on the same plot of this one ratio.

The two memories that matter

  • HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) — the off-chip memory stacked next to the GPU/TPU chip. Huge bandwidth (~3 TB/s on an H100) but still 10–100x slower to reach than on-chip memory. Every byte moved here costs real time and energy.
  • SRAM — a tiny on-chip scratchpad (e.g. 192KB "shared memory" per H100 streaming multiprocessor, or a TPU's VMEM). Only megabytes total, but ~10–20x faster than HBM. The entire game of kernel-writing is keeping data in SRAM as long as possible.

So what's today's "concept"?

There's no new mechanism to learn. Today's deliverable is an essay that treats arithmetic intensity as the thread connecting a single SM's memory hierarchy (ThunderKittens), a single attention layer (FlashAttention), a whole chip's roofline ceiling (the Scaling Book), and a whole inference cluster (Pope et al.).

📚
Official sources (use these, no others): Scaling Book — Roofline · FlashAttention (arXiv:2205.14135) · ThunderKittens (arXiv:2410.20399) · Pope et al. (arXiv:2211.05102)
2 · INTUITIONThe highway system a city gets stuck with✓

Imagine two people planning a city's roads. One is an urban designer who cares only about pure travel-time-per-trip, in the abstract — the mathematically "best" network might be an all-tunnels city with a subway stop on every block. The other is a civil engineer who has to build with whatever concrete, cranes, and land are actually affordable this decade.

🚇
the "best" design

An all-tunnels subway everywhere is optimal for trip time on paper. It never gets built — tunneling that dense wasn't affordable when the city was founded.

🛣️
what actually gets built

The city sprawls along whatever a few big surface highways could reach. Every neighborhood's shape — where businesses cluster, whether you need a car — gets bent by that early highway layout, not by "what's truly best."

In one line: decades later, all "clever" urban planning is really just working around the highways that already got built.

The hardware lottery is the same story. HBM bandwidth and SRAM size are the highways that got built — because DRAM economics and chip packaging made bandwidth the scarce, expensive thing. Every "clever" architecture — grouped-query attention, low-rank KV caches, quantized weights — is an engineer finding a route that respects the existing highway, not free invention from a blank page.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: highways are static infrastructure that outlasts any single trip. The "highway" in ML hardware actively co-evolves with the algorithms — chip vendors add features (TMA, sparsity support, lower-precision tensor cores) because certain algorithms became popular. It's a highway system that redesigns itself in response to traffic, which real highways don't do on a multi-year timescale.

4 · HOW & WHYThe roofline model, and why it explains four weeks of tricks✓

The roofline model

  • A plot with arithmetic intensity (FLOPs/byte) on the x-axis and achieved FLOPs/s on the y-axis.
  • Two ceilings exist: a slanted memory roofline (achieved FLOPs/s = intensity × HBM bandwidth) and a flat compute roofline (the chip's peak FLOPs/s).
  • Whichever ceiling you hit first decides if you're compute-bound or memory-bound.
  • The ridge point is the intensity where the two ceilings cross: Ridge = Peak FLOPs/s / HBM Bandwidth. Below it, a bigger chip does nothing — you need more bandwidth, not more FLOPs.
✅
Achieved FLOPs/s = min(Peak FLOPs/s, Intensity × HBM Bandwidth)
On an H100 (~989 TFLOP/s dense fp16 — not the ~1979 headline figure, which includes a 2x structured-sparsity multiplier that dense attention matmuls don't get — and ~3.35 TB/s HBM): ridge ≈ 989e12 / 3.35e12 ≈ 295 FLOPs/byte. Below 295, you're memory-bound no matter how fast the chip's math is.

Why it's the throughline across Weeks 13–16

  • ThunderKittens fights this constraint inside one SM: register tiling, bank-conflict-free SRAM layout, and TMA-pipelined copies all exist to make sure compute doesn't stall waiting on the memory hierarchy.
  • FlashAttention fights it inside one attention layer: tiling + online softmax keep the N×N score matrix out of HBM entirely, moving attention from memory-bound to compute-bound with zero change to the math.
  • Pope et al. apply the exact same logic across a whole serving cluster: batch size, KV-cache size, and interconnect bandwidth decide whether prefill or decode is compute- or communication-bound.
🔗
Causal chain: roofline (the diagnostic tool) → FlashAttention (a single-kernel fix inside the memory hierarchy) → ThunderKittens (a language/tooling fix for authoring such kernels reliably) → Pope et al. (the same physics, one level up, applied to a whole cluster). One mental model, four papers as evidence — that's the essay's real deliverable.

  • No invented URLs or claims beyond what the four cited sources plus standard roofline/CUDA memory-hierarchy facts support.
⚠️
The trap to avoid: "memory-bound" is not a synonym for "bad," and being memory-bound is not a flaw to eliminate at all costs. Intensity is a property of an operation given a hardware target, not an intrinsic property of an algorithm — the same attention math is memory-bound on one chip and compute-bound on another. And decode is structurally memory-bound; no trick removes that. The real levers are fewer bytes moved (quantization, GQA/MQA) or more useful work per byte reloaded (batching, speculative decoding) — not chasing higher FLOPs as if intensity were a score to maximize.
