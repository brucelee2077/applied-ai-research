Module 05a · Represent · Day 4

The Full Transformer BlockAssembling Attention, Norm, and Feed-Forward

You know those plastic building bricks that all snap together the same way? Every brick has the same little bumps on top and the same holes underneath, so you can press one onto another, then another, and build a tower as tall as you like — and every brick is doing the same simple thing. Today you build the transformer's brick: one small, self-contained unit called a block. Over the last three days you made all the parts on your workbench — attention (words sharing), the feed-forward layer (each word thinking on its own), plus two safety rails (the residual shortcut and layer norm). Today you snap them together into ONE reusable block — and then you discover the quiet magic: stack that same block a few dozen times and you have the same block structure that sits inside GPT and every large language model. Think it's just "gluing the parts"? Hold that thought — by the end you'll see why the order you snap them in decides whether a deep tower trains beautifully or falls over.

🎯
Together we'll assemble this block one snap at a time — first the two working parts (share, then think), then the two rails wrapped around each part, then the honest history of why those rails had to exist, then the one surprising choice (norm before or after?) that lets a tower go deep, then how we stack the block into a real transformer, and finally the two honest things a single block can never do. Every failure we meet is a little puzzle with a neat fix, and every formula is said out loud in plain words first.
1Share, then thinkTwo working parts

Let's start by laying out what we already have on the workbench. Over the last three days you built two working parts. Before we wrap or arrange anything, let's remember what each one actually does — because that's the beating heart of every block.

Think of a class working on a group project. First everyone gathers in a circle and shares what they found — you hear a bit of everyone's research, and everyone hears a bit of yours. That's the sharing round. But sharing isn't the same as finishing. So next, each student goes back to their own desk, alone, and actually works through what they just heard into a tidier idea. That's the solo-thinking round. A transformer block runs these exact two rounds, in this exact order: first the words share, then each word thinks on its own. Where the picture breaks down: real students take turns talking, but in a block all the words share at the same instant, and all of them think at the same instant — it only happens in two steps, not two slow rounds.

What the group-project picture gets right: gather-and-share first, then go off and process it on your own — that two-step rhythm is exactly a transformer block. Where it breaks down: students share out loud one at a time, but attention lets every word pull from every other word at the same instant in a single mathematical step.

The two parts, named

Let's name them gently, using the words you already know:

  1. The sharing round is self-attention — the sub-layer you built earlier in this module. It mixes information between words.
  2. The thinking round is the feed-forward layer (FFN) — yesterday's workshop. It transforms each word on its own.

A word we'll use a lot today: each of these two parts is a sub-layer — one working step inside the bigger block.

💡
Why the order matters: mix, THEN transform. Attention gathers context, so by the time a word reaches the feed-forward step it has something worth thinking about. Do it the other way and each word would "think" before it had heard anything — like writing your essay before the group meeting. Share first, then think. That's why every block puts attention before the feed-forward layer.

Victory lap: you can already say the whole block in one breath — first the words share (attention), then each word thinks alone (feed-forward). Next we wrap a safety rail around each of these two parts.

2A shortcut and a re-leveller around every sub-layerWrap each part

We have our two working parts. But you don't just stack raw parts — you wrap each one with two little helpers first. You already built both helpers in the last two days; now you see where they clip on. This wrapping is what turns two fragile parts into a rugged, stackable block.

Think of shipping a fragile vase across the country. You don't just drop the bare vase in a truck. First you do two things. (1) You keep the original vase safe — if the fancy new gift-wrapping tears, the vase itself is still fine underneath. (2) You add bubble wrap so the vase arrives at a sensible, steady condition, not rattled to pieces. A transformer wraps each sub-layer the same way: a shortcut that keeps the original signal safe even if the sub-layer's work goes wrong, and a re-leveller that keeps the numbers at a sensible, steady size. Where the picture breaks down: bubble wrap only protects — it doesn't change the vase — but the re-leveller (layer norm) actively rescales the numbers, so it's more like re-packing than just padding.

What the vase picture gets right: you protect the original and keep things at a steady, sane condition — two separate jobs, both needed for the fragile thing to survive the trip. Where it breaks down: a vase is protected passively, but layer norm does active work every step, recomputing a steady scale from the numbers themselves.

Rail 1 · the shortcut (residual)

The first helper is the residual connection — a skip connection. Instead of forcing the signal to squeeze through the sub-layer and hope nothing is lost, you let the original signal flow straight past on an express lane, and just add the sub-layer's change on top. Said in one plain line: output = x + SubLayer(x). If the sub-layer's work is weak, no harm done — the full-strength original is still there, added right back in.

Rail 2 · the re-leveller (layer norm)

The second helper is layer normalization (layer norm) — you met it two days ago. It re-levels each word's numbers to a steady scale, so nothing balloons or shrinks to nothing as the signal travels. It's the bubble wrap that keeps every part receiving numbers at a sensible size.

Now let's see why the shortcut earns its keep, in one before→after picture. On the left: no shortcut — the signal must pass through the part, and if the part weakens it, the signal fades. On the right: add the shortcut — the original flows straight up the express lane, so it arrives full-strength no matter what the part does.

So each sub-layer gets wrapped: a shortcut so the signal survives, and a re-leveller so it stays a sensible size. Do this to both the attention sub-layer and the feed-forward sub-layer, and you have a rugged block ready to stack.

⚙️
Optional (skippable) — the wrapped sub-layer in symbols. If x is a word's vector and SubLayer is either attention or the FFN, one wrapped sub-layer is y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)) — take the input x, run the part, add the input back (the shortcut), then re-level (layer norm). A full block is just this done twice: once with attention as the part, then once with the FFN as the part, feeding the first result into the second. You don't need the symbols — the vase picture says it all — but they're here if you like seeing the shapes line up.

Victory lap: you can now describe a wrapped sub-layer: run the part, add the original back (shortcut), re-level (layer norm). Next: why these rails weren't always there — and the disaster that happened without them.

3The tower that kept falling overWhy the rails exist

You might wonder: why bother with the shortcut and the re-leveller at all? Why not just stack the raw parts? The honest answer is a great story — engineers tried the raw stack first, and it kept falling over. Seeing why it fell is the best way to understand why every block carries these two rails.

Think of whispering a secret down a long line of people (the game "telephone"). The first person whispers clearly. But each person only hears a slightly quieter, slightly fuzzier version. By the tenth person the message is a faint mumble; by the fiftieth it's gone entirely — or someone panics and shouts, and it turns to noise. The longer the line, the worse it gets. A plain deep stack of parts, with no shortcut, is exactly that line: each part passes its signal to the next, and across dozens of them the numbers shrink toward nothing (fade to a mumble) or blow up too big (turn to a shout). Where the picture breaks down: in telephone the words get garbled, but here it's the size of the numbers that breaks — the message could be perfectly fine if only its scale stayed sensible.

What the telephone picture gets right: pass a signal through many hands with no protection and it degrades a little each time, worse the longer the line — exactly like activations and gradients through a deep raw stack. Where it breaks down: telephone loses meaning, but a deep net loses scale — and scale is fixable with the two rails.

Puzzle 1 — the fading gradient, and its fix

When a network learns, a learning signal (the gradient) has to travel back down through every part. In a raw deep stack, each part multiplies that signal by some factor. Multiply by a number smaller than 1 over and over — 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 … — and it races toward zero: the vanishing gradient. Multiply by something bigger than 1 and it explodes to infinity: the exploding gradient. Either way, the deep layers can't learn. The fix is the shortcut (residual): because the signal can flow straight up the express lane, the gradient gets a clean path straight back down — it skips past each part instead of being multiplied down by it.

Watch the fade happen, then watch the shortcut stop it — one before→after picture:

Puzzle 2 — the drifting scale, and its fix

There's a second, sneakier trouble. Even if the signal doesn't vanish, each part shifts the numbers a little — nudging their average up or their spread wider. Stack fifty parts and the numbers a late layer sees can be a wildly different size from what the early layers saw. That's feature-scale drift. The fix is the re-leveller (layer norm): wrap each part with a layer norm and it re-levels the numbers to a steady scale every step, so every part always receives numbers at a sensible size — no drift.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent until it isn't): a raw deep stack doesn't announce that it's broken — it just trains slower and slower, then stops improving, because its early layers get almost no signal (vanishing) and its numbers wander off-scale (drift). The two rails — residual to stop the fade, layer norm to stop the drift — are what quietly made very deep networks trainable in the first place. This is why you'll never see a serious transformer block without both.

Victory lap: you now know the honest history — the raw tower kept falling over, and the two rails are exactly the fixes for its two failures (fading gradients → residual, drifting scale → layer norm). Next: a surprising choice about where to put the re-leveller that decides how deep you can go.

4The one choice that lets a tower go deepBefore or after?

Here's the surprising part I promised you in the opening. We agreed each part gets a shortcut and a re-leveller. But there's a choice nobody warns you about: do you re-level after you add the shortcut, or before? It sounds like a tiny detail — a coin flip. It turns out to decide whether your tower can be 6 blocks tall or 60. This little choice is one of the most satisfying "aha" moments in the whole transformer.

Think of washing muddy vegetables before cooking. You could cook first and wash the plate after — or wash the veg before it goes in the pot. Both "clean" things, but the order completely changes the result: wash-then-cook gives you a clean meal every time, while cook-then-wash lets the mud build up in the pot batch after batch until the whole dinner is ruined. Putting the re-leveller (layer norm) before the part is like washing the veg first: everything downstream stays clean and steady. Putting it after lets a little mess through each block, and in a deep tower that mess piles up. Where the picture breaks down: mud is dirt you remove, but layer norm doesn't remove information — it just rescales it — so "clean" here means "at a steady, sane size," not "stuff thrown away."

What the cooking picture gets right: doing the same two actions in a different order gives a very different result, and the "clean first" order is the one that stays reliable batch after batch (block after block). Where it breaks down: cooking mud is gross but not mathematically precise — the real reason Post-LN struggles is that the numbers right before its final norm grow large in a deep stack, which we'll name below.

The two arrangements, named

  • Post-LN (the original 2017 design): add-then-norm. You run the part, add the shortcut, and re-level after. In one line: y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)).
  • Pre-LN (the modern default): norm-then-add. You re-level first, run the part on the cleaned input, then add the shortcut. In one line: y = x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x)).

The difference is just where the norm sits — but watch what it does to the express lane in this before→after picture. In Post-LN, the shortcut passes through the norm, so the clean express lane gets disturbed at every block; in Pre-LN, the shortcut runs straight from bottom to top untouched, giving the gradient a perfectly clean highway even in a very deep tower.

Puzzle 3 — why deep Post-LN diverges, and its fix

Here's the third failure of the day. In a deep Post-LN tower, the number coming out of the shortcut-add — right before the final norm — grows large as the stack gets deeper. Early in training, that big value can make the whole tower diverge (the loss shoots to infinity and training dies). The fix is Pre-LN: move the norm before the part, keep the express lane clean, and the deep tower trains stably from step one. (Teams also use a gentle learning-rate warm-up to tame early Post-LN training — but that's a training-schedule trick you'll meet on the training day, not part of the block itself.)

⚙️
Optional (skippable) — the two formulas side by side. Post-LN: y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)). Pre-LN: y = x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x)). Same three ingredients — x, the sub-layer, and the norm — in a different order. Notice in Pre-LN the raw x appears outside the norm, so the residual lane is never rescaled; that's the whole reason it trains deep so cleanly. Skip this if the cooking picture already did it for you.

Victory lap: you can now answer a real staff-level question — "Post-LN or Pre-LN, and why?" — with: Pre-LN norms before the sub-layer, so the residual lane stays untouched and deep stacks train stably; Post-LN was the 2017 original but tends to diverge deep without careful warm-up. That's a genuine interview beat, and it's yours.

5The whole block, assembledSnap it together

Now the payoff moment. We have every piece: two parts (attention, then feed-forward), and around each part a shortcut and a re-leveller in the Pre-LN order. Let's snap them together and look at the whole block in one picture — the thing you've been building toward for three days.

Think of a sandwich made to a fixed recipe. It's always the same layers, in the same order: bread, filling one, bread, filling two — every sandwich the shop makes is identical, which is exactly why they can make them fast and stack them in the display case. A transformer block is that fixed recipe: attention-with-its-rails, then feed-forward-with-its-rails, every single time, so you can build one and then reuse the identical recipe as many times as you want. Where the picture breaks down: a sandwich's fillings are fixed, but each block learns its own weights — the recipe (the shape) is identical, but what each block actually does with the ingredients is learned and different.

What the sandwich picture gets right: a fixed, repeatable recipe of layers in a set order, which is exactly what lets you mass-produce and stack it. Where it breaks down: every sandwich is truly identical, but blocks share only their shape — each one learns its own weights and specializes in different work.

Read the block out loud

Trace the picture from the bottom, and say it in plain words:

  1. Words come in. Re-level them (norm), let them share (attention), then add the originals back (shortcut). That's the first wrapped sub-layer.
  2. Take that result. Re-level again (norm), let each word think on its own (feed-forward), then add it back (shortcut). That's the second wrapped sub-layer.
  3. Out comes a refined set of words — same width as they came in, so the next block can take them straight away.

That "same width in, same width out" is the secret to stacking: because a block hands back exactly the shape it received, you can feed its output straight into another identical block.

🎤
Once it clicks, here's how you'd say it (in an interview): "A transformer block is two wrapped sub-layers: self-attention then a position-wise feed-forward network. Each sub-layer has a residual connection and a layer norm — modern models use the Pre-LN arrangement, x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x)), so the residual lane stays clean and deep stacks train stably. Attention mixes across tokens, the FFN transforms each token independently, and because the block preserves the input width you can stack N identical blocks." Notice you can already say every word of that.

Victory lap: you just assembled the complete block — the single most-repeated unit in all of modern AI. Next, the quiet magic: what happens when you stack it.

6One block, stacked into a towerStack the block

Here's the moment the whole module has been pointing at. You have one block. The quiet magic of the transformer is almost embarrassingly simple: you just stack the same block, over and over. That's it. That stack is the whole body of a transformer — the same block structure that sits inside GPT and every large language model.

Think of floors in a tall building. Each floor is built to the same blueprint — same height, same layout, same connectors top and bottom — so once you've designed one floor, you just repeat it to make the building as tall as you like. And each floor refines what the one below did: the ground floor handles arrivals, the next sorts them, the next processes them, and so on, until the top floor delivers the finished result. A transformer stacks identical blocks the same way: each block takes the words the block below refined, and refines them a little more. Where the picture breaks down: building floors are truly identical, but each transformer block learns its own weights, so an early block might handle simple grammar while a deep block handles meaning — same blueprint, different learned job.

What the building picture gets right: design one floor to a repeatable blueprint and you can stack it into any height, each floor refining the last — exactly how a transformer reuses one block design. Where it breaks down: real floors are identical, but each block learns different weights, specializing at different jobs up the tower.

Why stacking "just works"

The reason you can stack blocks so freely is the thing you noticed last concept: a block hands back the same width it received. So the output of block 1 is a perfectly valid input for block 2, which is a valid input for block 3, forever. Let's make that concrete — predict first: if one block keeps a word 4 numbers wide, how wide is that word after passing through 6 stacked blocks? Then run it.

width = 4; blocks = 6; [width for _ in range(blocks)]; print("after", blocks, "blocks, width =", width)
after 6 blocks, width = 4
Same width, block after block. Each block hands back the width it received, so a 4-wide word passing through 6 stacked blocks comes out 4 wide. Because the shapes always line up, you can stack as many as you like — 6, 12, 96. That's the whole trick behind building a deep transformer.

Real models pick a number of blocks and stick with it: the original transformer used 6; GPT-style models use dozens. But the design never changes — it's the same block structure you just built, repeated. (GPT adds one more ingredient on top of this structure — a causal mask so each word can only look backward — which you'll meet next lesson; the wiring you built today is the shared body underneath.) You now understand the body of a large language model.

Victory lap: you can explain the entire body of a transformer in one sentence — it's one block, stacked N times, each refining the words a little more. Next, the honest limits: two things a single block simply cannot do.

7Two things one block can't doThe honest limits

Before we wrap up, two honest caveats — the kind a good engineer always keeps in mind. Your block is powerful, but there are exactly two things a single block fundamentally cannot do. Knowing them is the sign you truly understand what a block is (and isn't).

Limit 1 · one block is one hop of thinking

Think of a relay race. One runner can only carry the baton one leg of the track — no matter how fast they are, they can't run the whole race alone. To cross the finish line you need a team of runners, each handing off to the next. A transformer block is one runner: it does exactly one round of mix-then-transform — share once, think once — and then hands off. If a question needs several hops of reasoning ("the author of the book that won the prize that…"), one block can't chain them; you need a stack of blocks, each doing one more hop. Where it breaks down: relay runners hand off a physical baton on a track, but blocks hand off a whole set of word-vectors, and each can reconsider all the words, not just carry one thing forward.

Limit 2 · a block can't tell word order by itself

Think of a bag of scrabble tiles. Tip the bag out and you have all the letters, but the bag itself has no idea what order they were in — "DOG" and "GOD" are the same bag of tiles. A transformer block is like that bag: on its own it treats the words as an unordered set, so "the cat sat" and "sat the cat" would look identical to it. Engineers have a fancy word for this: the block is permutation-equivariant — shuffle the inputs and it just shuffles the outputs, blind to order. Since word order obviously matters in language, we fix this by feeding the block external position information (which you met earlier in this module) so each word carries a tag saying where it sits. Where it breaks down: scrabble tiles carry no order at all, but a real transformer is given position tags on its inputs — so it only forgets order if you forget to add them.

⚠️
The two honest limits, in one line each: (1) depth of reasoning — one block does a single mix-then-transform; multi-step reasoning needs many stacked blocks. (2) order-blindness — a block is permutation-equivariant, so it needs position information supplied from outside or it treats a sentence like a shuffled bag of words. Neither is a bug in the block; they're the reason a transformer stacks blocks and adds positions.

Victory lap: you now know not just how to build a block, but exactly where its powers stop — and why the full transformer stacks blocks (for depth) and adds positions (for order). That honesty is what separates "I've seen the diagram" from "I understand it."
