@@@ hero
@lede You know those plastic building bricks that all snap together the same way? Every brick has the same little bumps on top and the same holes underneath, so you can press one onto another, then another, and build a tower as tall as you like — and every brick is doing the *same simple thing*. Today you build the transformer's brick: one small, self-contained unit called a **block**. Over the last three days you made all the parts on your workbench — attention (words sharing), the feed-forward layer (each word thinking on its own), plus two safety rails (the residual shortcut and layer norm). Today you snap them together into ONE reusable **block** — and then you discover the quiet magic: stack that same block a few dozen times and you have the same block *structure* that sits inside GPT and every large language model. Think it's just "gluing the parts"? Hold that thought — by the end you'll see why the *order* you snap them in decides whether a deep tower trains beautifully or falls over.
@goal Together we'll assemble this block one snap at a time — first the two working parts (share, then think), then the two rails wrapped around each part, then the honest history of why those rails had to exist, then the one surprising choice (norm *before* or *after*?) that lets a tower go deep, then how we stack the block into a real transformer, and finally the two honest things a single block can never do. Every failure we meet is a little puzzle with a neat fix, and every formula is said out loud in plain words first.

@@@ concept id=c1 tag="Two working parts" title="Share, then think" gotit="Got the two parts"
Let's start by laying out what we already have on the workbench. Over the last three days you built two working parts. Before we wrap or arrange anything, let's remember what each one actually *does* — because that's the beating heart of every block.

Think of a **class working on a group project**. First everyone gathers in a circle and *shares* what they found — you hear a bit of everyone's research, and everyone hears a bit of yours. That's the sharing round. But sharing isn't the same as *finishing*. So next, each student goes back to their own desk, alone, and actually *works through* what they just heard into a tidier idea. That's the solo-thinking round. A transformer block runs these exact two rounds, in this exact order: first the words share, then each word thinks on its own. **Where the picture breaks down:** real students take turns talking, but in a block *all* the words share at the same instant, and *all* of them think at the same instant — it only happens in two steps, not two slow rounds.

**What the group-project picture gets right:** gather-and-share first, then go off and process it on your own — that two-step rhythm is exactly a transformer block. **Where it breaks down:** students share out loud one at a time, but attention lets every word pull from every other word at the same instant in a single mathematical step.

#### The two parts, named
Let's name them gently, using the words you already know:

1. The sharing round is **[[self-attention||The step where every word looks at every other word in the sentence and pulls in what matters. It MIXES information across positions — but only as a weighted blend.]]** — the sub-layer you built earlier in this module. It *mixes* information *between* words.
2. The thinking round is the **[[feed-forward layer||The little per-word workshop you built last lesson: it widens each word's vector, bends it, and shrinks it back — a small network run on each word on its own. It TRANSFORMS each word, but never mixes across words.]]** (FFN) — yesterday's workshop. It *transforms* each word *on its own*.

A word we'll use a lot today: each of these two parts is a **[[sub-layer||One of the two working steps inside a transformer block: the attention sub-layer, or the feed-forward sub-layer. "Sub" because it lives inside the bigger block.]]** — one working step inside the bigger block.

!!! c-info 💡
<b>Why the order matters: mix, THEN transform.</b> Attention gathers context, so by the time a word reaches the feed-forward step it *has* something worth thinking about. Do it the other way and each word would "think" before it had heard anything — like writing your essay before the group meeting. Share first, then think. That's why every block puts attention *before* the feed-forward layer.
!!!

**Victory lap:** you can already say the whole block in one breath — *first the words share (attention), then each word thinks alone (feed-forward)*. Next we wrap a safety rail around each of these two parts.

@@@ concept id=c2 tag="Wrap each part" title="A shortcut and a re-leveller around every sub-layer" gotit="Got the wrapper"
We have our two working parts. But you don't just stack raw parts — you *wrap* each one with two little helpers first. You already built both helpers in the last two days; now you see where they clip on. This wrapping is what turns two fragile parts into a rugged, stackable **block**.

Think of **shipping a fragile vase across the country**. You don't just drop the bare vase in a truck. First you do two things. (1) You keep the *original* vase safe — if the fancy new gift-wrapping tears, the vase itself is still fine underneath. (2) You add bubble wrap so the vase arrives at a *sensible, steady* condition, not rattled to pieces. A transformer wraps each sub-layer the same way: a **shortcut** that keeps the original signal safe even if the sub-layer's work goes wrong, and a **re-leveller** that keeps the numbers at a sensible, steady size. **Where the picture breaks down:** bubble wrap only protects — it doesn't change the vase — but the re-leveller (layer norm) actively *rescales* the numbers, so it's more like re-packing than just padding.

**What the vase picture gets right:** you protect the original *and* keep things at a steady, sane condition — two separate jobs, both needed for the fragile thing to survive the trip. **Where it breaks down:** a vase is protected passively, but layer norm does active work every step, recomputing a steady scale from the numbers themselves.

#### Rail 1 · the shortcut (residual)
The first helper is the **[[residual connection||A skip connection: you add a sub-layer's input straight back onto its output, so output = x + SubLayer(x). The original signal always survives, and gradients get a clean path straight through.]]** — a *skip connection*. Instead of forcing the signal to squeeze *through* the sub-layer and hope nothing is lost, you let the original signal flow *straight past* on an express lane, and just **add** the sub-layer's change on top. Said in one plain line: `output = x + SubLayer(x)`. If the sub-layer's work is weak, no harm done — the full-strength original is still there, added right back in.

#### Rail 2 · the re-leveller (layer norm)
The second helper is **[[layer normalization||Layer norm: it rescales each word's feature vector to a steady mean and spread before (or after) a sub-layer, so the numbers never drift too big or too small as blocks pile up.]]** (layer norm) — you met it two days ago. It re-levels each word's numbers to a steady scale, so nothing balloons or shrinks to nothing as the signal travels. It's the bubble wrap that keeps every part receiving numbers at a sensible size.

Now let's *see* why the shortcut earns its keep, in one before→after picture. On the left: no shortcut — the signal must pass *through* the part, and if the part weakens it, the signal fades. On the right: add the shortcut — the original flows straight up the express lane, so it arrives full-strength no matter what the part does.

So each sub-layer gets wrapped: a shortcut so the signal survives, and a re-leveller so it stays a sensible size. Do this to *both* the attention sub-layer and the feed-forward sub-layer, and you have a rugged block ready to stack.

!!! c-info ⚙️
<b>Optional (skippable) — the wrapped sub-layer in symbols.</b> If `x` is a word's vector and `SubLayer` is either attention or the FFN, one wrapped sub-layer is `y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x))` — take the input `x`, run the part, add the input back (the shortcut), then re-level (layer norm). A full block is just this done twice: once with attention as the part, then once with the FFN as the part, feeding the first result into the second. You don't need the symbols — the vase picture says it all — but they're here if you like seeing the shapes line up.

**Victory lap:** you can now describe a *wrapped* sub-layer: run the part, add the original back (shortcut), re-level (layer norm). Next: why these rails weren't always there — and the disaster that happened without them.

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="Why the rails exist" title="The tower that kept falling over" gotit="Got the history"
You might wonder: why bother with the shortcut and the re-leveller at all? Why not just stack the raw parts? The honest answer is a great story — engineers *tried* the raw stack first, and it kept falling over. Seeing *why* it fell is the best way to understand why every block carries these two rails.

Think of **whispering a secret down a long line of people** (the game "telephone"). The first person whispers clearly. But each person only hears a slightly quieter, slightly fuzzier version. By the tenth person the message is a faint mumble; by the fiftieth it's gone entirely — or someone panics and shouts, and it turns to noise. The longer the line, the worse it gets. A plain deep stack of parts, with no shortcut, is exactly that line: each part passes its signal to the next, and across dozens of them the numbers **shrink toward nothing** (fade to a mumble) or **blow up too big** (turn to a shout). **Where the picture breaks down:** in telephone the *words* get garbled, but here it's the *size* of the numbers that breaks — the message could be perfectly fine if only its scale stayed sensible.

**What the telephone picture gets right:** pass a signal through many hands with no protection and it degrades a little each time, worse the longer the line — exactly like activations and gradients through a deep raw stack. **Where it breaks down:** telephone loses *meaning*, but a deep net loses *scale* — and scale is fixable with the two rails.

#### Puzzle 1 — the fading gradient, and its fix
When a network learns, a *learning signal* (the gradient) has to travel back down through every part. In a raw deep stack, each part multiplies that signal by some factor. Multiply by a number smaller than 1 over and over — `0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 …` — and it races toward zero: the **[[vanishing gradient||When the learning signal is multiplied down toward zero as it passes back through many layers, so the early layers barely learn at all.]]**. Multiply by something bigger than 1 and it explodes to infinity: the **exploding gradient**. Either way, the deep layers can't learn. **The fix is the shortcut (residual):** because the signal can flow straight up the express lane, the gradient gets a clean path straight back down — it *skips past* each part instead of being multiplied down by it.

#### Puzzle 2 — the drifting scale, and its fix
There's a second, sneakier trouble. Even if the signal doesn't vanish, each part *shifts* the numbers a little — nudging their average up or their spread wider. Stack fifty parts and the numbers a late layer sees can be a wildly different size from what the early layers saw. That's **[[feature-scale drift||When each layer shifts the size and spread of the numbers, so deep layers receive activations at scales the network was never tuned for — making training wobbly.]]**. **The fix is the re-leveller (layer norm):** wrap each part with a layer norm and it re-levels the numbers to a steady scale every step, so every part always receives numbers at a sensible size — no drift.

!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Failure mode (silent until it isn't):</b> a raw deep stack doesn't announce that it's broken — it just trains slower and slower, then stops improving, because its early layers get almost no signal (vanishing) and its numbers wander off-scale (drift). The two rails — <b>residual</b> to stop the fade, <b>layer norm</b> to stop the drift — are what quietly made very deep networks trainable in the first place. This is why you'll never see a serious transformer block *without* both.
!!!

**Victory lap:** you now know the honest history — *the raw tower kept falling over*, and the two rails are exactly the fixes for its two failures (fading gradients → residual, drifting scale → layer norm). Next: a surprising choice about *where* to put the re-leveller that decides how deep you can go.

@@@ concept id=c4 tag="Before or after?" title="The one choice that lets a tower go deep" gotit="Got Pre-LN"
Here's the surprising part I promised you in the opening. We agreed each part gets a shortcut *and* a re-leveller. But there's a choice nobody warns you about: do you re-level **after** you add the shortcut, or **before**? It sounds like a tiny detail — a coin flip. It turns out to decide whether your tower can be 6 blocks tall or 60. This little choice is one of the most satisfying "aha" moments in the whole transformer.

Think of **washing muddy vegetables before cooking**. You could cook first and wash the plate after — or wash the veg *before* it goes in the pot. Both "clean" things, but the *order* completely changes the result: wash-then-cook gives you a clean meal every time, while cook-then-wash lets the mud build up in the pot batch after batch until the whole dinner is ruined. Putting the re-leveller (layer norm) **before** the part is like washing the veg first: everything downstream stays clean and steady. Putting it **after** lets a little mess through each block, and in a deep tower that mess piles up. **Where the picture breaks down:** mud is dirt you remove, but layer norm doesn't remove information — it just rescales it — so "clean" here means "at a steady, sane size," not "stuff thrown away."

**What the cooking picture gets right:** doing the same two actions in a different order gives a very different result, and the "clean first" order is the one that stays reliable batch after batch (block after block). **Where it breaks down:** cooking mud is gross but not mathematically precise — the real reason Post-LN struggles is that the numbers *right before* its final norm grow large in a deep stack, which we'll name below.

#### The two arrangements, named
- **[[Post-LN||Add-then-norm: y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)). The original 2017 transformer arrangement — re-level AFTER adding the shortcut.]]** (the original 2017 design): *add-then-norm.* You run the part, add the shortcut, and re-level *after*. In one line: `y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x))`.
- **[[Pre-LN||Norm-then-add: y = x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x)). The modern default — re-level BEFORE the part, so the shortcut lane is never touched by norm and deep towers train stably.]]** (the modern default): *norm-then-add.* You re-level *first*, run the part on the cleaned input, then add the shortcut. In one line: `y = x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x))`.

The difference is just *where the norm sits* — but watch what it does to the express lane in this before→after picture. In Post-LN, the shortcut passes *through* the norm, so the clean express lane gets disturbed at every block; in Pre-LN, the shortcut runs straight from bottom to top *untouched*, giving the gradient a perfectly clean highway even in a very deep tower.

#### Puzzle 3 — why deep Post-LN diverges, and its fix
Here's the third failure of the day. In a deep **Post-LN** tower, the number coming out of the shortcut-add — right *before* the final norm — grows large as the stack gets deeper. Early in training, that big value can make the whole tower **diverge** (the loss shoots to infinity and training dies). **The fix is Pre-LN:** move the norm *before* the part, keep the express lane clean, and the deep tower trains stably from step one. (Teams also use a gentle *learning-rate warm-up* to tame early Post-LN training — but that's a training-schedule trick you'll meet on the training day, not part of the block itself.)

!!! c-info ⚙️
<b>Optional (skippable) — the two formulas side by side.</b> Post-LN: `y = LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x))`. Pre-LN: `y = x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x))`. Same three ingredients — `x`, the sub-layer, and the norm — in a different order. Notice in Pre-LN the raw `x` appears *outside* the norm, so the residual lane is never rescaled; that's the whole reason it trains deep so cleanly. Skip this if the cooking picture already did it for you.
!!!

#### Read the block out loud
Trace the picture from the bottom, and say it in plain words:
1. Words come in. Re-level them (norm), let them **share** (attention), then **add** the originals back (shortcut). That's the first wrapped sub-layer.
2. Take that result. Re-level again (norm), let each word **think** on its own (feed-forward), then **add** it back (shortcut). That's the second wrapped sub-layer.
3. Out comes a refined set of words — same width as they came in, so the next block can take them straight away.

That "same width in, same width out" is the secret to stacking: because a block hands back exactly the shape it received, you can feed its output straight into another identical block.

The reason you *can* stack blocks so freely is the thing you noticed last concept: **a block hands back the same width it received.** So the output of block 1 is a perfectly valid input for block 2, which is a valid input for block 3, forever. Let's make that concrete — predict first: if one block keeps a word 4 numbers wide, how wide is that word after passing through 6 stacked blocks? Then run it.

Real models pick a number of blocks and stick with it: the original transformer used 6; GPT-style models use dozens. But the *design* never changes — it's the same block *structure* you just built, repeated. @@@ concept id=c7 tag="The honest limits" title="Two things one block can't do" gotit="Got the limits"
Before we wrap up, two honest caveats — the kind a good engineer always keeps in mind. Your block is powerful, but there are exactly two things a *single* block fundamentally cannot do. Knowing them is the sign you truly understand what a block is (and isn't).

#### Limit 1 · one block is one hop of thinking
Think of a **relay race**. One runner can only carry the baton *one* leg of the track — no matter how fast they are, they can't run the whole race alone. To cross the finish line you need a *team* of runners, each handing off to the next. A transformer block is one runner: it does exactly *one* round of *mix-then-transform* — share once, think once — and then hands off. If a question needs several hops of reasoning ("the author of the book that won the prize that…"), one block can't chain them; you need a *stack* of blocks, each doing one more hop. **Where it breaks down:** relay runners hand off a physical baton on a track, but blocks hand off a whole set of word-vectors, and each can reconsider *all* the words, not just carry one thing forward.

#### Limit 2 · a block can't tell word order by itself
Think of a **bag of scrabble tiles**. Tip the bag out and you have all the letters, but the bag itself has no idea what *order* they were in — "DOG" and "GOD" are the same bag of tiles. A transformer block is like that bag: on its own it treats the words as an unordered set, so "the cat sat" and "sat the cat" would look identical to it. Engineers have a fancy word for this: the block is **[[permutation-equivariant||Shuffle the input words and a bare block just shuffles its outputs the same way — it has no built-in idea of which word came first. Order has to be added from outside.]]** — shuffle the inputs and it just shuffles the outputs, blind to order. Since word order obviously matters in language, we fix this by feeding the block *external* **position information** (which you met earlier in this module) so each word carries a tag saying *where* it sits. **Where it breaks down:** scrabble tiles carry no order at all, but a real transformer *is* given position tags on its inputs — so it only forgets order if you *forget* to add them.

**The day as signposts, in order** — each one only makes sense because of the one before it:
- **1 · Two working parts.** A block runs two rounds: **attention** (words *share*, mixing across positions) then the **feed-forward** layer (each word *thinks* on its own, transforming). Mix, then transform.
- **2 · Wrap each part.** Around *each* part go two rails: a **residual** shortcut (`output = x + SubLayer(x)`) so the signal survives, and **layer norm** so the scale stays steady.
- **3 · Why the rails exist.** A raw deep stack fails two ways — the learning signal **vanishes/explodes** (fix: residual) and the feature **scale drifts** (fix: layer norm).
- **4 · Before or after?** Put the norm **before** the part — **Pre-LN** (`x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x))`) — so the residual lane stays clean and deep towers train stably; **Post-LN** (the 2017 original, `LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x))`) tends to diverge deep.
- **5 · Snap it together.** The full block = attention-with-rails, then feed-forward-with-rails, preserving the input width.
- **6 · Stack the block.** Because width in = width out, you stack **N identical blocks** into a deep tower — the same block structure inside every LLM — each refining the words a little more.
- **The limits.** One block = one hop of reasoning (multi-hop needs **depth**), and a block is **permutation-equivariant** (needs **position information** or it's blind to word order).

#### Cheat-sheet · the full block at a glance
%%% table
:: Piece :: In plain words :: Why it matters
self-attention sub-layer :: words share — a weighted blend across positions :: gathers context so each word has something to think about
feed-forward sub-layer :: each word thinks on its own — widen, bend, shrink :: transforms per word; where most of the per-token work happens
residual (shortcut) :: add the input back: `x + SubLayer(x)` :: gives the signal & gradient a clean highway; stops vanishing/exploding
layer norm (re-leveller) :: rescale each word's numbers to a steady size :: stops feature-scale drift as blocks pile up
Pre-LN vs Post-LN :: norm-then-add vs add-then-norm :: Pre-LN keeps the residual lane clean → deep towers train stably
stacking N blocks :: repeat the identical block up a tower :: same shape in/out lets you go deep; that stack is the body of a transformer
%%%

#### Cheat-sheet · the words you met today
%%% jargon
transformer block | one repeatable unit: attention sub-layer, then feed-forward sub-layer, each wrapped with a shortcut and a norm
sub-layer | one working step inside a block (attention, or feed-forward)
residual connection | a shortcut that adds a sub-layer's input back onto its output: output = x + SubLayer(x)
layer normalization | re-levels each word's numbers to a steady scale so they don't drift as blocks stack
vanishing / exploding gradient | the learning signal shrinking to zero (or blowing up) through many raw layers; fixed by the residual
feature-scale drift | each layer shifting the size of the numbers; fixed by layer norm
Post-LN | add-then-norm: LayerNorm(x + SubLayer(x)) — the 2017 original, wobbles when very deep
Pre-LN | norm-then-add: x + SubLayer(LayerNorm(x)) — the modern default, trains deep stacks stably
permutation-equivariant | a bare block treats words as an unordered set; it needs external position information to know order
%%%
