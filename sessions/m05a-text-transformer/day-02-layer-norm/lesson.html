Module 5a · Represent · Day 2

Keeping the
Numbers Calm

Yesterday you added the input back with a residual connection. But adding things over and over can make the numbers grow huge — or drift tiny — and wild numbers make training shaky. Today you learn layer normalization: a small step that rescales each token's numbers to a tidy mean of 0 and spread of 1, so every layer sees numbers in the same friendly range.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain what layer normalization does, write the "subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation" formula, and say why calm numbers keep training stable.
1 · WHAT IS ITRescale each token to mean 0, spread 1✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know a token is turned into a list of numbers called an embedding (M3), and you have met residual connections (Day 1). Today's step sits right beside them in the block.

The problem: numbers drift

Inside a transformer, every token is a list of numbers. As those numbers pass through layers and get added by residual connections, some grow very large and some shrink very small. When the numbers in a batch have wildly different sizes, the gradients bounce around and training becomes unstable — slow, or it breaks.

The fix: normalize each token

Layer normalization (often written LayerNorm) rescales each token's list of numbers so it has a tidy shape:

  • the average (the mean) becomes 0
  • the spread (the standard deviation) becomes 1
🧮
before: [5, 500, 5000] → after: [-0.81, -0.59, 1.41]

The key word: "layer"

LayerNorm normalizes across the features of one token at a time — its own little list of numbers — not across the whole batch. Each token is tidied up on its own. This is what makes it work well for text, where sequences have different lengths. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONGrading fairly across different classes✓
📝
Two classes, two scales

One teacher grades out of 10, another out of 1000. A 7/10 and a 700/1000 are the same result, but the raw numbers look nothing alike. You cannot compare them directly.

⚖️
Rescale to a shared scale

Normalizing puts everyone on the same footing: shift so the average is 0, stretch so the spread is 1. Now a score means the same thing no matter which class it came from.

In one line: layer normalization is fair grading for numbers — it puts every token's values onto one common, calm scale before the next layer looks at them.

🎮
Before: [5, 500, 5000] — impossible to work with. After normalizing: [-0.81, -0.59, 1.41] — tidy, centered, and easy for the next layer to handle.
⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: fair grading is a one-time human choice, but layer normalization runs at every layer, every step, automatically. It also keeps two small learnable knobs (called gain and bias) so the model can undo the rescaling if a layer truly needs bigger or shifted numbers.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYThe formula, and pre-LN vs post-LN✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Take one token's list of numbers. Find its average. Subtract that average from every number, so the new average is 0. Then find how spread out the numbers are, and divide every number by that spread, so the new spread is 1. Two little steps: center, then scale.

Step 2 — the formula, symbol by symbol

🧮
y = (x − μ) / (σ + ε)
x = the token's list of numbers
μ = the mean of x (its average)
σ = the standard deviation of x (its spread)
ε = a tiny number so we never divide by zero
y = the normalized output — mean 0, std 1

In real models there are also two learnable knobs, γ (gain) and β (bias): the final output is γ·y + β. They let the model rescale or shift the numbers again if a layer genuinely needs to. To keep today simple, focus on the center-and-scale part.

Step 3 — a worked example with small numbers

Take x = [2, 4, 6].

  • Mean: μ = (2+4+6)/3 = 4.
  • Center: x − μ = [−2, 0, 2].
  • Spread: σ = 1.633 (how far the numbers sit from 4, on average).
  • Scale: [−2, 0, 2] / 1.633 ≈ [−1.22, 0, 1.22]. New mean 0, new std 1. Done.

Where LayerNorm sits — pre-LN vs post-LN (awareness)

There are two common places to put it inside a block, each paired with a residual connection:

  • Post-LN (the original 2017 design): do the sublayer, add the residual, then normalize — LayerNorm(x + sublayer(x)).
  • Pre-LN (used by most modern LLMs like GPT): normalize first, then do the sublayer and add — x + sublayer(LayerNorm(x)).
💡
Why most big models chose pre-LN: normalizing before the sublayer keeps the residual highway (Day 1) totally clean, so gradients flow even better in very deep stacks. Pre-LN is easier to train without warm-up tricks. You will use pre-LN when you assemble the block on Day 4.
