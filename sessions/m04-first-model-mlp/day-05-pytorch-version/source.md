@@@ concept id=c1 tag="The box" title="nn.Module: the box that holds your model" gotit="Got nn.Module"
Before we translate anything, let's meet the container everything lives in. Everything you built this week was the same practice — guess, get corrected, nudge — and today's job is only to repackage it. In PyTorch you don't leave your model as a loose pile of variables. You wrap it in a box called an [[nn.Module||PyTorch's base class for a model. You subclass it, store your layers as attributes, and PyTorch then knows how to find every weight inside.]]. You *subclass* it — make your own model by extending PyTorch's `nn.Module` — and inside it you store your layers as attributes (like `self.fc1 = ...`). That storing is the whole point: once a layer is an attribute, PyTorch can *find* it, and find its weights.

First, plain words on every PyTorch term today, so nothing sneaks up on you:

%%% jargon
nn.Module | the base class you extend to make a model; it holds your layers and knows how to find their weights
nn.Linear | one dense layer — it IS your first-layer math, with its weight matrix and bias created and stored for you
forward | the method where you write the forward pass, and only the forward pass
tensor | PyTorch's array — a numpy array that also remembers how it was computed
autograd | PyTorch's automatic backward pass; it records the forward math and computes every gradient for you
loss.backward() | the one call that runs autograd and fills every weight's gradient
optimizer | an object from torch.optim that holds the weights and applies the update
zero_grad() | clear the stored gradients before each batch — the one genuinely new habit
%%%

Now the picture. Think of your Module as a labeled toolbox: each layer is a tool clipped into a named slot inside it.

**Think of a school backpack with labeled pockets.** You clip your calculator into one pocket, your pens into another. Because each thing has its own named pocket, when a teacher says "empty your bag," everything comes out — nothing is lost in the lining. `nn.Module` is that backpack: put each layer in a named slot (`self.fc1`, `self.fc2`) and PyTorch can later empty the bag and hand you every weight.

**What the picture gets right:** naming and storing each item is exactly what lets you collect them all later without missing any. **Where it breaks down:** a backpack only holds things. Your Module also *runs* — it defines how data flows through the layers (the `forward` method, coming next). A backpack never processes what's inside it.

#### Why the box matters: model.parameters()
Here's the deep reason to store layers as attributes. In a moment you'll hand *every weight and bias* to the thing that updates them (the optimizer). You won't list them by hand. You call [[model.parameters()||An iterator that walks the whole Module and yields every weight and bias tensor inside it — exactly the list the optimizer needs.]] — and because each layer sat in a named slot, PyTorch walks the box and yields every weight for you. That one call is the whole payoff of using a Module. That's your first win: the container. Everything from here clips *into* this box.

@@@ concept id=c2 tag="The layers" title="nn.Linear + forward: your first-layer math, handed to you" gotit="Got the layers"
Now we fill the box. Remember your numpy MLP: you had a weight matrix `W1` and bias `b1`, and the layer's job was `x @ W1 + b1`. The weights you used to create and shape by hand are now created and shaped for you.

And the shape flow is *identical* to your from-scratch version: 784 pixels in, first layer sends it to 128 hidden numbers, ReLU bends it, second layer sends it to 10 class scores. Same MLP, same shapes — just PyTorch words.

**Think of buying a pre-built flat-pack bookshelf instead of cutting the wood yourself.** Last week you cut every plank and drilled every hole — you *know* the shelf inside out. `nn.Linear` is the flat-pack: planks pre-cut, holes pre-drilled to the right sizes. **What the picture gets right:** the finished thing is identical; the tool removes tedious construction, not the design. **Where it breaks down:** a flat-pack shelf is fixed once built, but `nn.Linear`'s weights keep *changing* every training step as the model learns — pre-built, not frozen.

!!! c-info 🔎
<b>One honest detail so nothing surprises you in the Produce step.</b> Your numpy <code>W1</code> was shaped <code>(784, 128)</code> and you computed <code>x @ W1 + b1</code>. PyTorch stores the same weights <i>transposed</i>: <code>model.fc1.weight.shape</code> is <code>(128, 784)</code> — that is <code>(out, in)</code> — and under the hood computes <code>x @ W.T + b</code>. It's the exact same linear map, just laid out the other way around. So when you print the shape and see <code>(128, 784)</code>, that is correct, not a bug.
!!!

#### The forward method: you write only the forward pass
Inside your Module you write *one* method, called [[forward||The method on your Module where you write the forward pass — how input flows through the layers to the output. You never write the backward pass; PyTorch does that.]]. In it you describe how data flows forward — and *that is all you write*. There is no `backward` method for you to fill in:

%%% formula
expr: def forward(self, x): return self.fc2(relu(self.fc1(x)))
note: pass x through the first layer, bend with ReLU, pass through the second layer. This is your Day-1 forward line, verbatim — the ONLY pass you write. No softmax here; the loss folds that in (concept 5).
%%%

Read that against your numpy code and it's the same line. The thing to *feel* is the asymmetry: you write the forward pass, and you never write the backward pass again. That missing backward method isn't an oversight — it's the entire reason PyTorch exists, and the next three concepts unpack *how* PyTorch writes it for you.

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="The tensor" title="The tensor: a numpy array that remembers how it was made" gotit="Got the tensor"
So how can PyTorch write your backward pass when you only gave it the forward pass? The secret starts with the data. In numpy your data lived in arrays. In PyTorch it lives in a [[tensor||PyTorch's array. Same shapes and same math as a numpy array, plus a hidden diary: if it has requires_grad turned on, it records every operation that produced it.]] — a numpy array with a hidden diary. When a tensor has [[requires_grad||A flag on a tensor. When True, every operation using that tensor is recorded in the autograd graph, so a gradient can later be computed for it. Weights get it automatically.]] turned on (weights get this automatically), PyTorch quietly writes down *every operation* that touches it. Multiply it, add to it, pass it through ReLU — each step gets logged.

That running log is the [[autograd graph||The record PyTorch builds during the forward pass: every operation, in order, connected input-to-output. Reading it backward yields the gradients — the hand-derived chain rule from Day 2, done automatically.]]. As your `forward` runs, PyTorch silently builds a chain: input → matmul → add bias → ReLU → matmul → add bias → loss. It's just *remembering* the recipe, step by step, so it can retrace it later.

**Think of a chef who films every step while cooking.** A normal cook just makes the meal (that's numpy — computes the answer and forgets how). A chef with a camera rolling makes the *same* meal, but now there's a recording of every step in order. If the dish comes out too salty, they play the tape backward to see exactly where the salt went in. The tensor's diary is that camera: it doesn't change the cooking, it just records it so mistakes can be traced.

**What the picture gets right:** recording costs a little extra, but it's what makes it possible to trace any result back to its causes afterward. **Where it breaks down:** a chef watches the tape and *reasons* about it. Autograd doesn't reason — it applies a fixed, exact rule (the chain rule from Day 2) at each recorded step. No judgment, just calculus replayed in reverse.

#### Why this is the whole trick
On Day 2 you spent hours deriving the backward pass — working out `dW2`, gating it through ReLU, working out `dW1`. That derivation *is* "read the forward recipe in reverse and apply the chain rule at each step." PyTorch records the recipe automatically as the forward runs, so the reverse pass is now something a machine can do for you. You didn't lose the backward pass — you taught it to a robot once (on Day 2), and now the robot does it every time. The next concept is you pressing the button that runs it.

@@@ concept id=c4 tag="One button" title="loss.backward(): your entire Day-2 backward pass, in one call" gotit="Got backward()"
Here's the button. Once the forward pass has run and you have a loss, you call [[loss.backward()||One call that runs autograd: it walks the recorded graph backward from the loss, applies the chain rule at every step, and fills each weight's .grad with its gradient. It replaces your entire hand-derived Day-2 backward pass.]]. That single line walks the autograd graph backward — from the loss, back through every recorded operation, all the way to the weights — and *fills in every weight's gradient*. Each weight tensor gets a `.grad` attribute holding "how much this weight should change to lower the loss." That is *exactly* the numbers you computed by hand on Day 2, now produced by one call.

**Think of a receipt that itemizes exactly how much each ingredient cost.** Working out each cost by hand — reading labels, multiplying quantities — takes forever (that was Day 2). But the store already tracked every scan, so it prints one itemized receipt instantly. `loss.backward()` is that print button: the graph already tracked every operation, so one press itemizes the "cost" (gradient) of every weight.

**What the picture gets right:** because the steps were tracked as they happened, the full breakdown comes out in one action instead of being recomputed by hand. **Where it breaks down:** a receipt is just a readout of fixed prices. `loss.backward()` actively *computes* fresh numbers using calculus each time — and those numbers change every batch, because the loss and weights change every batch.

#### The two-way trip
Every batch makes a *round trip* through the graph:

- **Forward** (you write it): data flows in, the graph is recorded, out comes the loss.
- **Backward** (`loss.backward()` writes it): retrace the recorded graph in reverse, and every weight's `.grad` gets filled.

!!! c-info 🔗
<b>Where do the gradients live?</b> After <code>loss.backward()</code>, the gradient for each weight sits in that weight's own <code>.grad</code> attribute — e.g. <code>model.fc1.weight.grad</code>. The optimizer (next) reads exactly those <code>.grad</code> values to know how to nudge each weight. So backward <i>fills</i> the grads; the optimizer <i>uses</i> them.
!!!

Feel the size of this win: your entire Day-2 backward pass — the hardest code you wrote all week — is now `loss.backward()`. You didn't cheat; you understand every step it takes. You just stopped typing them out.

@@@ concept id=c5 tag="The loss, ready-made" title="A built-in loss — and the roster it points to" gotit="Got the loss"
For `loss.backward()` to work, you first need a loss — one number for how wrong the guess was, exactly like Day 4. Here too PyTorch hands you a ready-made one. You don't hand-write cross-entropy anymore; you use the built-in [[nn.CrossEntropyLoss||PyTorch's built-in classification loss. It takes the model's raw scores and the true labels and returns one number. It folds softmax inside itself, so your forward pass should output raw scores, not probabilities.]] (for classification, our digit job) or [[nn.MSELoss||PyTorch's built-in mean-squared-error loss, used for regression — predicting a number rather than a class.]] (for predicting a number). You create it once as a `criterion`, call it on `(scores, true_labels)`, and it returns one loss tensor — already wired into the autograd graph, ready for `.backward()`.

**Think of switching from a hand-drawn scorecard to an arena scoreboard machine.** On Day 4 you wrote the scoring rule yourself, step by step. `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` is the scoreboard: same rule, same score, but a trusted, tested machine — and it even quietly does the tricky part (turning raw scores into probabilities with softmax) more safely than a beginner would.

This loss is a *live* tensor plugged into the autograd graph — its whole reason for existing is to be run backward, not just displayed.

!!! c-info 🔗

#### The loss points backward — but toward what? model.parameters()
`loss.backward()` fills each weight's `.grad`. But who *collects* those weights so something can update them? That's [[model.parameters()||An iterator that yields every weight and bias tensor stored inside your Module. You hand it to the optimizer so the optimizer knows exactly which tensors to update.]] from concept 1. Because you stored your layers in the Module's named slots, `model.parameters()` walks the box and hands over every weight and bias — precisely the list you'll give the optimizer next.

So the chain so far: the built-in loss gives one number on the graph → `backward()` fills every `.grad` → `model.parameters()` is the roster of exactly those weights. One piece left: the thing that reads the grads and takes the step.

@@@ concept id=c6 tag="The updater" title="The optimizer: your weight-update line, as an object" gotit="Got the optimizer"
On Day 3 your update was a line you wrote by hand: `W = W - learning_rate * dW`, once per weight, every batch. PyTorch packages that exact rule into an object called an [[optimizer||An object from torch.optim that holds your model's parameters and knows the update rule. You build it once with model.parameters() and a learning rate; it applies the update when you call step().]], living in `torch.optim`. You build it *once*, handing it two things: the roster of weights (`model.parameters()`) and the learning rate. From then on it holds those weights and knows how to update them.

The simplest optimizer is [[SGD||Stochastic Gradient Descent — the optimizer whose rule is exactly your Day-3 line: weight = weight − learning_rate × gradient.]]: `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)`. Its update rule *is* your Day-3 line, verbatim — `weight = weight − lr × gradient` — just applied by the object instead of by you.

**Think of hiring a bookkeeper and handing them your ledger once.** On Day 3 you did the books yourself — every entry, every subtraction, by hand. The optimizer is a bookkeeper: you hand them the ledger (your weights) and the rule (subtract `lr × grad`) one time, and after that you just say "post the updates" and they do every entry. Same arithmetic, done by someone you briefed once.

**What the picture gets right:** you set it up once with the right ledger and rule, then delegate the repetitive updates — the arithmetic is identical to doing it yourself. **Where it breaks down:** a human bookkeeper might use judgment or catch odd entries. The optimizer applies its rule mechanically every time — it won't notice if something upstream (like a forgotten `zero_grad`, coming next) fed it bad gradients.

#### One step at a time: optimizer.step()
The moment the optimizer actually *acts* is when you call [[optimizer.step()||The call that applies one update to ALL parameters at once: for every weight, weight = weight − lr × weight.grad. It reads the .grad that loss.backward() just filled.]]. That one call sweeps over *every* weight the optimizer holds and applies `weight = weight − lr × weight.grad` to all of them at once, reading the `.grad` values `loss.backward()` just filled. One line, and your whole model takes a single gradient-descent step. So: `backward()` fills the grads, `step()` spends them. The next concept is the one habit that keeps those two in sync.

@@@ concept id=c7 tag="The new habit" title="optimizer.zero_grad(): the one habit numpy never needed" gotit="Got zero_grad"
Here is the single genuinely *new* thing in the whole lesson — the habit with no match in your numpy code, and the one that trips up almost every beginner. Before each batch's backward pass, you must call [[optimizer.zero_grad()||Clears every weight's .grad back to zero. Required before each backward pass because PyTorch ADDS new gradients onto the old ones instead of replacing them.]] to wipe the stored gradients to zero. Why? Because when you call `loss.backward()`, PyTorch does *not* replace each `.grad` — it **adds** the new gradient onto whatever was already there. Gradients **accumulate** by default.

Forget to zero them, and batch 2's gradients pile on top of batch 1's, batch 3 adds onto that, and your `.grad` values swell every step. The optimizer then takes wildly oversized steps, and the loss spikes, blows up to NaN, or refuses to fall — with *no error message at all*.

**Think of a whiteboard you use to total up one shopping trip's cost.** Wipe the board clean before each trip and the total is right every time. Forget to wipe it, and this week's groceries get added on top of last week's numbers still sitting there — the "total" grows and grows, meaningless. `zero_grad()` is wiping the board before you add up this batch.

**What the picture gets right:** you have to clear the running total before a fresh count, or old numbers silently contaminate the new one. **Where it breaks down:** a leftover whiteboard total is obvious — you'd *see* stale numbers. The gradient version is invisible: nothing looks wrong on screen, the code runs, and only the loss curve quietly reveals the damage.

!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>The classic silent bug: forgetting <code>optimizer.zero_grad()</code>.</b> Cause: PyTorch accumulates gradients across <code>backward()</code> calls by default. Leave it out and every batch's gradient piles onto the last — step sizes balloon, and the loss spikes to NaN or won't fall, with <i>no error thrown</i>. Remedy: call <code>optimizer.zero_grad()</code> at the top of every batch, before <code>backward()</code>. The senior habit: when a freshly-ported loop diverges for no clear reason, check the loop <i>order</i> first — <code>zero_grad → forward → loss → backward → step</code>.
!!!

This bug can't happen in numpy: there you computed a fresh `dW` each batch, with nothing to accumulate. PyTorch's accumulate-by-default is the price of autograd's flexibility — and `zero_grad()` is the one-line habit that pays it. That's the last new piece. Now let's snap all five together.

#### The order is the whole game
The five lines aren't interchangeable — the *order* is load-bearing:

**Think of two calculators from different brands.** Type the same sum into both. If both show the same answer down to the last digit, they compute the same way — you don't need to open them up. Feeding the same input through your numpy model and your PyTorch model and getting the same answer is exactly that check: matching outputs are evidence they're the same machine inside.

**What the picture gets right:** matching answers on the same input is strong evidence of the same underlying computation. **Where it breaks down:** two calculators matching on *one* sum doesn't fully prove identical internals — one might round differently on a harder input. So you don't trust a single match: you check *several* inputs, and better still you compare the *weights* themselves. One agreement is a hint; many agreements plus matched weights is proof.

#### The check, step by step
Here is the actual equivalence check, built up in three moves:

1. **In words.** "Same model" means: give both models the same weights and the same input, and they must produce the same output — and trained the same way, they must follow the same learning curve.
2. **The concrete check.** Copy your numpy weights into the PyTorch model (remembering the transpose from concept 2: numpy `W1` is `(784,128)`, PyTorch wants `(128,784)`). Feed one image through both. Compare the 10 output scores.
3. **A worked comparison.** Say numpy outputs scores `[..., 8.3, ...]` with the max at index 7, and PyTorch outputs `[..., 8.3000001, ...]`, max also at index 7. Both predict "7", and the numbers match to ~6 decimal places. The tiny wiggle is just floating-point order-of-operations, not a different algorithm. Repeat on ten images: all ten agree. Then train both the same way — the loss curves fall together to the same ~96%. That agreement is the proof no new idea sneaked in; only new *words* for ideas you already own.

#### The capability limit: PyTorch changes the labor, not the power
Sit with the honest bookend to the week. PyTorch removes *manual labor*. It does **not** change what your MLP can learn. Same architecture → same expressiveness → same failure modes. If your numpy model overfit, the PyTorch one overfits the same way. If a pattern was too hard for a 784→128→10 MLP in numpy, it's exactly as hard in PyTorch. The framework is faster to write and runs on a GPU — but it gives your model zero extra *ability*. That's precisely *why* the equivalence check belongs here: a tool that only removes typing cannot, by definition, make your model smarter.

#### Why a frontier lab cares
This equivalence is why the whole field runs on these abstractions. Every real model — from a 10-line MLP to the largest language models — is built from `nn.Module`, autograd, and an optimizer. Nobody hand-derives backprop for a billion parameters. But *everybody who debugs* one leans on knowing what `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()` actually do underneath — which is the week you just spent. The framework is the productivity layer; your hand-built understanding is the debugging layer. Staff engineers are hired for the second: they drop below the abstraction, reason about the actual gradients, and see *why* a silent run won't learn. You now have both layers.
