Module 5 · Train · Day 5

The Same Modelin PyTorch

For four days you did everything by hand: you wired the forward pass, you derived the backward pass one gradient at a time, you built the practice loop, you added dropout. It worked — but it was a lot of careful bookkeeping. So here is your reward. Today you rebuild the exact same model in a tool called PyTorch, and watch thirty lines of gradient math shrink to about five. And here's the promise: you are not learning a new idea today. Every PyTorch piece simply replaces one thing you already built by hand. Same practice loop. Same accuracy. A fraction of the code.

🎯
Here's what we'll figure out together, one small idea at a time: - nn.Module — the box that holds your model and lets PyTorch find its weights; - nn.Linear and the forward method — your first-layer math and your forward pass, handed to you; - the tensor and autograd — a numpy array that also remembers how it was computed, so loss.backward() replaces your whole hand-derived backward pass; - the optimizer — step() replaces your update line, and zero_grad() is the one new habit; - and the five-line loop that maps one-to-one onto the loop you already built — proving this is the same model, not a new one.
1nn.Module: the box that holds your modelThe box

Before we translate anything, let's meet the container everything lives in. Everything you built this week was the same practice — guess, get corrected, nudge — and today's job is only to repackage it. In PyTorch you don't leave your model as a loose pile of variables. You wrap it in a box called an nn.Module. You subclass it — make your own model by extending PyTorch's nn.Module — and inside it you store your layers as attributes (like self.fc1 = ...). That storing is the whole point: once a layer is an attribute, PyTorch can find it, and find its weights.

First, plain words on every PyTorch term today, so nothing sneaks up on you:

WordPlain-English meaning — before we use it
nn.Modulethe base class you extend to make a model; it holds your layers and knows how to find their weights
nn.Linearone dense layer — it IS your first-layer math, with its weight matrix and bias created and stored for you
forwardthe method where you write the forward pass, and only the forward pass
tensorPyTorch's array — a numpy array that also remembers how it was computed
autogradPyTorch's automatic backward pass; it records the forward math and computes every gradient for you
loss.backward()the one call that runs autograd and fills every weight's gradient
optimizeran object from torch.optim that holds the weights and applies the update
zero_grad()clear the stored gradients before each batch — the one genuinely new habit

Now the picture. Think of your Module as a labeled toolbox: each layer is a tool clipped into a named slot inside it.

Think of a school backpack with labeled pockets. You clip your calculator into one pocket, your pens into another. Because each thing has its own named pocket, when a teacher says "empty your bag," everything comes out — nothing is lost in the lining. nn.Module is that backpack: put each layer in a named slot (self.fc1, self.fc2) and PyTorch can later empty the bag and hand you every weight.

What the picture gets right: naming and storing each item is exactly what lets you collect them all later without missing any. Where it breaks down: a backpack only holds things. Your Module also runs — it defines how data flows through the layers (the forward method, coming next). A backpack never processes what's inside it.

Why the box matters: model.parameters()

Here's the deep reason to store layers as attributes. In a moment you'll hand every weight and bias to the thing that updates them (the optimizer). You won't list them by hand. You call model.parameters() — and because each layer sat in a named slot, PyTorch walks the box and yields every weight for you. That one call is the whole payoff of using a Module. That's your first win: the container. Everything from here clips into this box.

2nn.Linear + forward: your first-layer math, handed to youThe layers

Now we fill the box. Remember your numpy MLP: you had a weight matrix W1 and bias b1, and the layer's job was x @ W1 + b1. In PyTorch that entire thing — the matrix, the bias, and the multiply-and-add — is one ready-made object called nn.Linear. Write nn.Linear(784, 128) and PyTorch creates a weight matrix connecting 784 inputs to 128 outputs and a length-128 bias, initializes them with sensible random values for you, and hands back something that turns 784 numbers into 128. The weights you used to create and shape by hand are now created and shaped for you.

And the shape flow is identical to your from-scratch version: 784 pixels in, first layer sends it to 128 hidden numbers, ReLU bends it, second layer sends it to 10 class scores. Same MLP, same shapes — just PyTorch words.

Think of buying a pre-built flat-pack bookshelf instead of cutting the wood yourself. Last week you cut every plank and drilled every hole — you know the shelf inside out. nn.Linear is the flat-pack: planks pre-cut, holes pre-drilled to the right sizes. Same shelf. You just skip the sawing.

What the picture gets right: the finished thing is identical; the tool removes tedious construction, not the design. Where it breaks down: a flat-pack shelf is fixed once built, but nn.Linear's weights keep changing every training step as the model learns — pre-built, not frozen.

🔎
One honest detail so nothing surprises you in the Produce step. Your numpy W1 was shaped (784, 128) and you computed x @ W1 + b1. PyTorch stores the same weights transposed: model.fc1.weight.shape is (128, 784) — that is (out, in) — and under the hood computes x @ W.T + b. It's the exact same linear map, just laid out the other way around. So when you print the shape and see (128, 784), that is correct, not a bug.

The forward method: you write only the forward pass

Inside your Module you write one method, called forward. In it you describe how data flows forward — and that is all you write. There is no backward method for you to fill in:

🧮
def forward(self, x): return self.fc2(relu(self.fc1(x)))
pass x through the first layer, bend with ReLU, pass through the second layer. This is your Day-1 forward line, verbatim — the ONLY pass you write. No softmax here; the loss folds that in (concept 5).

Read that against your numpy code and it's the same line. The thing to feel is the asymmetry: you write the forward pass, and you never write the backward pass again. That missing backward method isn't an oversight — it's the entire reason PyTorch exists, and the next three concepts unpack how PyTorch writes it for you.

3The tensor: a numpy array that remembers how it was madeThe tensor

So how can PyTorch write your backward pass when you only gave it the forward pass? The secret starts with the data. In numpy your data lived in arrays. In PyTorch it lives in a tensor — a numpy array with a hidden diary. When a tensor has requires_grad turned on (weights get this automatically), PyTorch quietly writes down every operation that touches it. Multiply it, add to it, pass it through ReLU — each step gets logged.

That running log is the autograd graph. As your forward runs, PyTorch silently builds a chain: input → matmul → add bias → ReLU → matmul → add bias → loss. It's just remembering the recipe, step by step, so it can retrace it later.

Think of a chef who films every step while cooking. A normal cook just makes the meal (that's numpy — computes the answer and forgets how). A chef with a camera rolling makes the same meal, but now there's a recording of every step in order. If the dish comes out too salty, they play the tape backward to see exactly where the salt went in. The tensor's diary is that camera: it doesn't change the cooking, it just records it so mistakes can be traced.

What the picture gets right: recording costs a little extra, but it's what makes it possible to trace any result back to its causes afterward. Where it breaks down: a chef watches the tape and reasons about it. Autograd doesn't reason — it applies a fixed, exact rule (the chain rule from Day 2) at each recorded step. No judgment, just calculus replayed in reverse.

Why this is the whole trick

On Day 2 you spent hours deriving the backward pass — working out dW2, gating it through ReLU, working out dW1. That derivation is "read the forward recipe in reverse and apply the chain rule at each step." PyTorch records the recipe automatically as the forward runs, so the reverse pass is now something a machine can do for you. You didn't lose the backward pass — you taught it to a robot once (on Day 2), and now the robot does it every time. The next concept is you pressing the button that runs it.

4loss.backward(): your entire Day-2 backward pass, in one callOne button

Here's the button. Once the forward pass has run and you have a loss, you call loss.backward(). That single line walks the autograd graph backward — from the loss, back through every recorded operation, all the way to the weights — and fills in every weight's gradient. Each weight tensor gets a .grad attribute holding "how much this weight should change to lower the loss." That is exactly the numbers you computed by hand on Day 2, now produced by one call.

Think of a receipt that itemizes exactly how much each ingredient cost. Working out each cost by hand — reading labels, multiplying quantities — takes forever (that was Day 2). But the store already tracked every scan, so it prints one itemized receipt instantly. loss.backward() is that print button: the graph already tracked every operation, so one press itemizes the "cost" (gradient) of every weight.

What the picture gets right: because the steps were tracked as they happened, the full breakdown comes out in one action instead of being recomputed by hand. Where it breaks down: a receipt is just a readout of fixed prices. loss.backward() actively computes fresh numbers using calculus each time — and those numbers change every batch, because the loss and weights change every batch.

The two-way trip

Every batch makes a round trip through the graph:

  • Forward (you write it): data flows in, the graph is recorded, out comes the loss.
  • Backward (loss.backward() writes it): retrace the recorded graph in reverse, and every weight's .grad gets filled.
🔗
Where do the gradients live? After loss.backward(), the gradient for each weight sits in that weight's own .grad attribute — e.g. model.fc1.weight.grad. The optimizer (next) reads exactly those .grad values to know how to nudge each weight. So backward fills the grads; the optimizer uses them.

Feel the size of this win: your entire Day-2 backward pass — the hardest code you wrote all week — is now loss.backward(). You didn't cheat; you understand every step it takes. You just stopped typing them out.

5A built-in loss — and the roster it points toThe loss, ready-made

For loss.backward() to work, you first need a loss — one number for how wrong the guess was, exactly like Day 4. Here too PyTorch hands you a ready-made one. You don't hand-write cross-entropy anymore; you use the built-in nn.CrossEntropyLoss (for classification, our digit job) or nn.MSELoss (for predicting a number). You create it once as a criterion, call it on (scores, true_labels), and it returns one loss tensor — already wired into the autograd graph, ready for .backward().

Think of switching from a hand-drawn scorecard to an arena scoreboard machine. On Day 4 you wrote the scoring rule yourself, step by step. nn.CrossEntropyLoss is the scoreboard: same rule, same score, but a trusted, tested machine — and it even quietly does the tricky part (turning raw scores into probabilities with softmax) more safely than a beginner would.

What the picture gets right: the scoring rule is unchanged; a tested machine computes it for you and handles a fiddly detail (softmax) more reliably. Where it breaks down: a scoreboard shows a dead number you just read. This loss is a live tensor plugged into the autograd graph — its whole reason for existing is to be run backward, not just displayed.

🔗
Why no softmax in your forward pass? On earlier days you turned raw scores into probabilities with softmax before scoring. nn.CrossEntropyLoss folds softmax inside itself, more stably. So your forward should return the raw scores (concept 2). Applying softmax yourself AND letting the loss do it too is a classic double-softmax slip that quietly weakens learning.

The loss points backward — but toward what? model.parameters()

loss.backward() fills each weight's .grad. But who collects those weights so something can update them? That's model.parameters() from concept 1. Because you stored your layers in the Module's named slots, model.parameters() walks the box and hands over every weight and bias — precisely the list you'll give the optimizer next.

So the chain so far: the built-in loss gives one number on the graph → backward() fills every .grad → model.parameters() is the roster of exactly those weights. One piece left: the thing that reads the grads and takes the step.

6The optimizer: your weight-update line, as an objectThe updater

On Day 3 your update was a line you wrote by hand: W = W - learning_rate dW, once per weight, every batch. PyTorch packages that exact rule into an object called an optimizer, living in torch.optim. You build it once*, handing it two things: the roster of weights (model.parameters()) and the learning rate. From then on it holds those weights and knows how to update them.

The simplest optimizer is SGD: torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1). Its update rule is your Day-3 line, verbatim — weight = weight − lr × gradient — just applied by the object instead of by you.

Think of hiring a bookkeeper and handing them your ledger once. On Day 3 you did the books yourself — every entry, every subtraction, by hand. The optimizer is a bookkeeper: you hand them the ledger (your weights) and the rule (subtract lr × grad) one time, and after that you just say "post the updates" and they do every entry. Same arithmetic, done by someone you briefed once.

What the picture gets right: you set it up once with the right ledger and rule, then delegate the repetitive updates — the arithmetic is identical to doing it yourself. Where it breaks down: a human bookkeeper might use judgment or catch odd entries. The optimizer applies its rule mechanically every time — it won't notice if something upstream (like a forgotten zero_grad, coming next) fed it bad gradients.

One step at a time: optimizer.step()

The moment the optimizer actually acts is when you call optimizer.step(). That one call sweeps over every weight the optimizer holds and applies weight = weight − lr × weight.grad to all of them at once, reading the .grad values loss.backward() just filled. One line, and your whole model takes a single gradient-descent step. So: backward() fills the grads, step() spends them. The next concept is the one habit that keeps those two in sync.

7optimizer.zero_grad(): the one habit numpy never neededThe new habit

Here is the single genuinely new thing in the whole lesson — the habit with no match in your numpy code, and the one that trips up almost every beginner. Before each batch's backward pass, you must call optimizer.zero_grad() to wipe the stored gradients to zero. Why? Because when you call loss.backward(), PyTorch does not replace each .grad — it adds the new gradient onto whatever was already there. Gradients accumulate by default.

Forget to zero them, and batch 2's gradients pile on top of batch 1's, batch 3 adds onto that, and your .grad values swell every step. The optimizer then takes wildly oversized steps, and the loss spikes, blows up to NaN, or refuses to fall — with no error message at all.

Think of a whiteboard you use to total up one shopping trip's cost. Wipe the board clean before each trip and the total is right every time. Forget to wipe it, and this week's groceries get added on top of last week's numbers still sitting there — the "total" grows and grows, meaningless. zero_grad() is wiping the board before you add up this batch.

What the picture gets right: you have to clear the running total before a fresh count, or old numbers silently contaminate the new one. Where it breaks down: a leftover whiteboard total is obvious — you'd see stale numbers. The gradient version is invisible: nothing looks wrong on screen, the code runs, and only the loss curve quietly reveals the damage.

⚠️
The classic silent bug: forgetting optimizer.zero_grad(). Cause: PyTorch accumulates gradients across backward() calls by default. Leave it out and every batch's gradient piles onto the last — step sizes balloon, and the loss spikes to NaN or won't fall, with no error thrown. Remedy: call optimizer.zero_grad() at the top of every batch, before backward(). The senior habit: when a freshly-ported loop diverges for no clear reason, check the loop order first — zero_grad → forward → loss → backward → step.

This bug can't happen in numpy: there you computed a fresh dW each batch, with nothing to accumulate. PyTorch's accumulate-by-default is the price of autograd's flexibility — and zero_grad() is the one-line habit that pays it. That's the last new piece. Now let's snap all five together.

8The whole loop: five lines that map one-to-one onto yoursThe five-line loop

Now the payoff — the moment all the pieces click into a loop you already know by heart. The entire PyTorch training loop, per batch, is five lines, and each maps exactly onto a step you built across Days 2–4. Here's the shape:

Think of a five-step morning routine you run every day without thinking: alarm off, wash, dress, eat, leave. Each PyTorch line is one step of that routine — zero_grad (alarm off / clear yesterday), model(x) (wash), criterion (dress), backward() (eat), step() (leave). You don't re-decide the routine each morning; you just run it. The five-line loop is your training routine, run once per batch.

What the picture gets right: the routine only feels automatic because you did the slow, deliberate version first — each line is a pointer to understanding you already have. Where it breaks down — and this is the important half: skip or reorder a morning step and you're merely late. Skip or reorder a loop step and it fails silently — drop zero_grad() and gradients corrupt; run step() before backward() and you spend empty grads; and nothing warns you. A morning routine forgives a wrong order; this loop does not.

The order is the whole game

The five lines aren't interchangeable — the order is load-bearing:

🪜
Math Ladder — the five-line loop, and why the order is fixed
1 · In words: for each batch — clear old gradients, run forward to get scores, score them into a loss, run backward to fill every .grad, then step the optimizer to nudge the weights.
2 · The formula: optimizer.zero_grad() → scores = model(x) → loss = criterion(scores, y) → loss.backward() → optimizer.step()
3 · Tiny numbers: your numpy loop was ~30 lines of dW math per batch; this is 5 lines and reaches the same test accuracy. Line-for-line: zero_grad = your reset, model(x) = Day-1 forward, criterion = Day-4 loss, backward() = all of Day-2, step() = Day-3 update.
4 · Sanity check: zero_grad must come BEFORE backward (or grads accumulate); backward must come BEFORE step (or step spends stale/empty grads). Swap or drop one and the loop silently misbehaves — no crash, just a loss that won't fall.

Let it land: there is nothing new in this loop. It is the exact same practice you built by hand — guess, get corrected, nudge — with the correction bookkeeping handed to autograd and the optimizer. Five lines, all of which you could expand back into numpy from memory.

9The equivalence check: proving it's the same model, not a new oneSame model

We just rebuilt the same brain a brand-new way. But how do we prove it's the same brain, and not a clever lookalike that happens to look right? All day the promise has been "PyTorch is a re-expression, not a new algorithm." A promise you can't check is just hope. So let's turn "I hope it's the same" into "I proved it's the same" — because proving it is the habit that makes the whole week pay off.

Think of two calculators from different brands. Type the same sum into both. If both show the same answer down to the last digit, they compute the same way — you don't need to open them up. Feeding the same input through your numpy model and your PyTorch model and getting the same answer is exactly that check: matching outputs are evidence they're the same machine inside.

What the picture gets right: matching answers on the same input is strong evidence of the same underlying computation. Where it breaks down: two calculators matching on one sum doesn't fully prove identical internals — one might round differently on a harder input. So you don't trust a single match: you check several inputs, and better still you compare the weights themselves. One agreement is a hint; many agreements plus matched weights is proof.

The check, step by step

Here is the actual equivalence check, built up in three moves:

  1. In words. "Same model" means: give both models the same weights and the same input, and they must produce the same output — and trained the same way, they must follow the same learning curve.
  2. The concrete check. Copy your numpy weights into the PyTorch model (remembering the transpose from concept 2: numpy W1 is (784,128), PyTorch wants (128,784)). Feed one image through both. Compare the 10 output scores.
  3. A worked comparison. Say numpy outputs scores [..., 8.3, ...] with the max at index 7, and PyTorch outputs [..., 8.3000001, ...], max also at index 7. Both predict "7", and the numbers match to ~6 decimal places. The tiny wiggle is just floating-point order-of-operations, not a different algorithm. Repeat on ten images: all ten agree. Then train both the same way — the loss curves fall together to the same ~96%. That agreement is the proof no new idea sneaked in; only new words for ideas you already own.

The capability limit: PyTorch changes the labor, not the power

Sit with the honest bookend to the week. PyTorch removes manual labor. It does not change what your MLP can learn. Same architecture → same expressiveness → same failure modes. If your numpy model overfit, the PyTorch one overfits the same way. If a pattern was too hard for a 784→128→10 MLP in numpy, it's exactly as hard in PyTorch. The framework is faster to write and runs on a GPU — but it gives your model zero extra ability. That's precisely why the equivalence check belongs here: a tool that only removes typing cannot, by definition, make your model smarter.

Why a frontier lab cares

This equivalence is why the whole field runs on these abstractions. Every real model — from a 10-line MLP to the largest language models — is built from nn.Module, autograd, and an optimizer. Nobody hand-derives backprop for a billion parameters. But everybody who debugs one leans on knowing what loss.backward() and optimizer.step() actually do underneath — which is the week you just spent. The framework is the productivity layer; your hand-built understanding is the debugging layer. Staff engineers are hired for the second: they drop below the abstraction, reason about the actual gradients, and see why a silent run won't learn. You now have both layers.
