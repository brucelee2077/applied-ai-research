@@@ concept id=c1 tag="Why it exists" title="Why a framework exists at all: the pain it removes" gotit="Got why"
Start with the honest question you earned by doing it the hard way. This week you practiced the same loop again and again — the model guesses, gets corrected, and nudges its weights. The *guess* (the forward pass) was short and easy to write. The *correction* (the backward pass) was the monster: on Day 2 you spent hours deriving `dW2`, gating it through ReLU, deriving `dW1`, checking every shape. Now imagine you change the model — add a layer, swap ReLU for something else, branch the network in two. Every one of those changes means you re-derive the *entire* backward pass by hand, from scratch, and one wrong sign or transposed matrix trains a model that silently learns nothing.

That is the pain a [[deep-learning framework||A library like PyTorch whose core job is to compute the backward pass automatically, so you never hand-derive gradients for a new model shape again.]] exists to remove. As networks grow, hand-writing the backward pass for every new architecture becomes error-prone and does not scale — that is the whole reason the tool was built.

**Think of doing your taxes by hand every single year, with the forms changing each time.** One year it's a simple form; then you buy a house and it's a harder form; then you start a side business and it's harder still. Doing it by hand, every new situation is a fresh chance to make an arithmetic slip that quietly costs you. A framework is like tax software: you describe your situation, and it does the error-prone arithmetic correctly every time, whatever shape your situation takes.

**What the analogy gets right:** the *hard, repetitive, easy-to-get-wrong* arithmetic is exactly what gets automated, and it stays correct across every new shape. **Where it breaks down:** tax software also gives you advice ("you qualify for this deduction"). The framework gives *zero* modeling advice — it only computes gradients for whatever model you describe. It never tells you your model is a bad idea (that's the capability limit at the end).

#### The practice never changed — only who does the bookkeeping
Hold onto the spine: the practice is still guess, get corrected, nudge. The framework does not change the practice at all. It just takes over the *correction bookkeeping* — the backward pass — so that changing your model no longer means re-deriving pages of calculus. That single job, done reliably, is why every lab in the world builds on a framework instead of raw numpy.

@@@ concept id=c2 tag="Autograd + the tape" title="Autograd: record the forward, replay it backward" gotit="Got autograd"
So how does the framework do your backward pass without you writing it? The trick has a name: [[autograd||Short for automatic differentiation. As the forward pass runs, PyTorch records every operation on tracked tensors into a graph, then walks that graph backward to compute every gradient — the by-hand backward pass, automated.]], short for *automatic differentiation*. The idea is almost too simple: **while the forward pass runs, PyTorch quietly writes down every operation you do to your tensors, in order.** That running list is the [[computation graph||Also called the tape: a record where each tensor operation is a node, connected input-to-output. It is built dynamically as the forward pass runs, so PyTorch can later read it backward.]] — sometimes called *the tape*. Then, to get gradients, it walks that record backward, applying the chain rule you learned on Day 2 at each node.

Two small flags make this work. A tensor flagged [[requires_grad||A flag on a tensor. When True, every operation using that tensor is recorded on the computation graph, so a gradient can later be computed for it. Your model's weights get this automatically.]]`=True` is *tracked* — its operations get recorded (your model's weights get this flag automatically). After the backward walk, each tracked tensor's gradient is stored in its [[.grad||The attribute where autograd stores a tensor's gradient after the backward walk — the exact dL/dW number you computed by hand on Day 2.]] attribute. That is the direct mapping from your from-scratch code: the `dW` you computed by hand *is* `W.grad` now.

**Think of a chef who films every step while cooking.** A normal cook just makes the meal and forgets how (that's plain numpy — it computes the answer and keeps no record). A chef with a camera rolling makes the *same* meal, but now there's a recording of every step in order. If the dish comes out too salty, they play the tape backward to find exactly where too much salt went in. The tape is autograd's computation graph: it doesn't change the cooking, it just records it so mistakes can be traced back to their cause.

**What the analogy gets right:** recording costs a little extra, but it's what makes it possible to trace any result back to its causes afterward — which is exactly what a gradient is. **Where it breaks down:** a chef *watches the tape and reasons* about it. Autograd does not reason — it mechanically applies one fixed, exact rule (the chain rule) at each recorded step. No judgment, just calculus replayed in reverse.

#### Define-by-run: why plain `if` and `for` just work
Here's a detail that quietly matters. The graph is built **dynamically, as the forward pass actually runs** — this is called [[define-by-run||PyTorch builds the graph while your Python code executes, line by line. Whatever operations actually run get recorded, so ordinary if-statements and for-loops need no special framework syntax.]]. PyTorch doesn't ask you to declare your model's shape ahead of time in a special language. Whatever operations your Python code *actually executes* — including inside an `if` branch or a `for` loop — are what get recorded. So you write ordinary Python: an `if` that picks a different path on some inputs, a `for` that repeats a block. No special framework syntax. The tape simply records whatever ran, and the backward walk retraces exactly that. This is *the* reason PyTorch feels like normal programming and not like learning a second, stranger language.

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="One button" title="loss.backward(): the whole backward chain in one call" gotit="Got backward()"
The tape is recorded. Now you press the button. Once the forward pass has run and you have a loss, you call [[loss.backward()||The single call that runs the backward walk: starting from the loss, it retraces the recorded graph in reverse, applies the chain rule at each node, and fills every tracked leaf tensor's .grad. It replaces your entire hand-written backward pass.]] — and that *one line* replaces the whole hand-written backward chain you wrote on Day 2. It starts at the loss, walks the computation graph in reverse through every recorded operation, applies the chain rule at each step, and fills each weight's `.grad` with "how much this weight should change to lower the loss." Those are the *exact* numbers you computed by hand — now produced by one call.

One behavior to name precisely, because it causes a famous bug later: `loss.backward()` does not *replace* the values in `.grad` — it **adds** to them. It *accumulates* the freshly computed gradients onto whatever was already sitting in each leaf tensor's `.grad`. On a clean, zeroed grad that just means "fill it in"; but it is why you must zero the grads each step (concept 7).

**Think of an itemized receipt from a store checkout.** Working out how much each item cost you by hand — reading labels, multiplying quantities — takes forever (that was Day 2). But the register already scanned every item as it went by, so it prints one itemized receipt instantly. `loss.backward()` is that print button: the tape already recorded every operation, so one press itemizes the "cost" — the gradient — of every weight at once.

**What the analogy gets right:** because each step was recorded as it happened, the full breakdown comes out in one action instead of being recomputed by hand. **Where it breaks down:** a receipt is a fixed readout of prices already set. `loss.backward()` *actively computes* fresh numbers with calculus every time — and those numbers change every batch, because the loss and the weights change every batch. It also *adds* to any total already there, which a receipt would never do.

#### Feel the size of this win
Your entire Day-2 backward pass — the hardest code you wrote all week, roughly thirty careful lines — is now the single line `loss.backward()`. You did not cheat and skip understanding it; you built it by hand first, so you know exactly what those thirty lines were. You just stopped typing them. Change your model tomorrow and you *still* just call `loss.backward()` — the framework re-derives the new backward pass from the new tape, for free.

- **1 · The tensor + autograd substrate** — the ground floor. A tensor is a numpy array that *also* records itself onto the tape (concept 2). This is the engine; the three layers above are conveniences built on it.
- **2 · [[nn.Module||PyTorch's model container. You store layers as attributes; it auto-registers their weights and biases, and .parameters() hands that whole collection to the optimizer.]]** — the model container. It does *parameter bookkeeping*: store your layers as attributes and it auto-registers every weight and bias, so you no longer track a hand-kept dict of arrays. Its payoff is `.parameters()` — one call that hands the whole collection to the optimizer.
- **3 · the loss function** — a ready-made object like `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` (classification) or `nn.MSELoss` (regression) that turns scores + labels into one number, already wired onto the tape. It replaces the loss you hand-wrote on Day 4.
- **4 · the [[optimizer||An object from torch.optim that stores your parameters and owns the update rule. optimizer.step() applies the rule to every parameter using the .grad autograd just filled.]]** — an object from `torch.optim` that owns the *update rule*. It stores your parameters and, on `step()`, applies "weight = weight − lr × grad" to all of them.

**Think of a professional kitchen with stations.** The prep counter (the tensor/autograd substrate) is where the actual food is. On top of it sit stations: a pantry that keeps every ingredient labeled and findable (`nn.Module`), a tasting station that scores each dish (the loss), and an expediter who decides the next move for every plate (the optimizer). You could cook on the bare counter alone — but the stations let you run the kitchen without losing track of anything.

**What the analogy gets right:** the layers are *separable roles* stacked on one foundation — each does one job, and you can reason about them one at a time. **Where it breaks down:** kitchen stations are staffed by people who improvise. These layers do not improvise at all; each applies a fixed rule. The optimizer never decides "this dish needs more salt" — it only applies the exact update you configured.

#### The optimizer is a swap-in part: SGD or Adam
Because the optimizer *owns* the update rule as a separate object, swapping the rule is a **one-line change**. The simplest is [[SGD||Stochastic Gradient Descent: the optimizer whose rule is your Day-3 line exactly — weight = weight − learning_rate × gradient.]] — `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)` — whose rule *is* your Day-3 line, verbatim. A smarter, very common one is [[Adam||A popular adaptive optimizer in torch.optim. Its internal update rule is more elaborate than SGD, but you swap it in by changing one line; its mechanics come in a later optimization module.]] — `torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.001)`. To switch from plain SGD to Adam, you change *that one line* and nothing else — the loop, the model, and the loss all stay identical. (What makes Adam smarter inside is a later optimization module; here the point is only that the optimizer is a clean, swappable slot.)

**Think of a five-step morning routine you run every day without thinking: alarm off, wash, dress, eat, leave.** Each loop line is one step — `zero_grad` (clear yesterday), `model(x)` (wash), `criterion` (dress), `backward()` (eat), `step()` (leave). **What the analogy gets right:** the routine feels automatic *because* you did the slow, deliberate version first — each line is a pointer to understanding you already have. **Where it breaks down — the important half:** skip a *morning* step and you're merely late. Skip or reorder a *loop* step and it fails silently — drop `zero_grad()` and gradients corrupt; run `step()` before `backward()` and you spend empty grads — with no warning. #### The order is load-bearing
%%% mathladder
title: the canonical loop, and why the order is fixed
words: for each batch — clear the old gradients, run forward to get scores, score them into a loss, run backward to fill every .grad, then step the optimizer to nudge the weights.
formula: optimizer.zero_grad() → scores = model(x) → loss = criterion(scores, y) → loss.backward() → optimizer.step()
numbers: your numpy loop was ~30 lines of dW math per batch; this canonical loop is 5 lines and reaches the same test accuracy. Line-for-line: zero_grad = your reset, model(x) = Day-1 forward, criterion = Day-4 loss, backward() = all of Day-2, step() = Day-3 update.

There is *nothing new* in this loop. It is the exact practice you built by hand — guess, get corrected, nudge — with the correction bookkeeping handed to autograd and the optimizer. The one line with no numpy ancestor is `zero_grad()`, and that is the whole subject of concept 7.

**Think of addressing a thousand envelopes.** One person with a pen (the CPU) writes them one at a time — steady, but slow for a big stack. For a tiny job the move can cost more than it saves, so the GPU only wins clearly on *large* matrix multiplies — which is exactly what real training is made of.

#### The pattern you'll actually write
In real code you pick the device once and move both the model and the data to it:

%%% formula
expr: device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu";  model = model.to(device);  x = x.to(device)

This one power — same code, far faster on a GPU — is what turns "train a small MLP on my laptop" into "train a model with billions of parameters." Nothing about the *math* changed; the framework just let the same tensor operations run on hardware built for them.

**Think of a rental car with four quirks the desk warns you about.** "The tank reads full even when it's not; the trunk pops if you double-press; the dome light drains the battery if left on; and the spare only fits the front." None is a defect — each is just a behavior you handle once you know it. These four bugs are that briefing: known behaviors of autograd, each with a habit that neutralizes it.

**What the analogy gets right:** these are *known, documented behaviors* with simple habits, not mysterious breakage — once briefed, you never get caught. **Where it breaks down:** a car quirk usually gives a visible sign (a light, a noise). Three of these four give *no sign at all* — the code runs, and only the loss curve or the memory graph quietly reveals the damage. That silence is exactly why you memorize them.

#### 1 · Forgetting `optimizer.zero_grad()` — the accumulate trap
!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Cause:</b> `loss.backward()` <i>adds</i> new gradients onto whatever is in each `.grad` — PyTorch accumulates gradients across backward calls by default (concept 3). Forget to clear them and batch 2's gradient piles on batch 1's, batch 3 on that; step sizes balloon and the loss spikes to NaN or refuses to fall — with <i>no error thrown</i>. <b>Remedy:</b> call <code>optimizer.zero_grad()</code> at the top of every training step, before <code>backward()</code>. This is the one line with no numpy ancestor — in numpy you computed a fresh `dW` each batch, so there was nothing to accumulate.
!!!

#### 2 · Calling `loss.backward()` twice — the freed graph
!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Cause:</b> to save memory, PyTorch <i>frees</i> the computation graph as soon as the first <code>backward()</code> finishes. Call <code>backward()</code> a second time on that same graph and you get a runtime error: the tape it needs is gone. <b>Remedy:</b> in the normal loop this never happens — you recompute the forward pass each step, which builds a <i>fresh</i> graph every time. Only if you deliberately need to back-propagate through the same graph twice do you pass <code>retain_graph=True</code> to the first call — and you use that flag sparingly, because keeping the graph around costs memory.
!!!

#### 3 · Evaluating without `torch.no_grad()` — the memory creep
!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Cause:</b> autograd keeps building the graph on <i>every</i> forward pass, even during evaluation where you never call <code>backward()</code>. So an eval loop silently records tapes and holds activations for a backward pass that never comes; GPU memory climbs run over run until a long evaluation crashes with out-of-memory. <b>Remedy:</b> wrap eval code in <code>with torch.no_grad():</code> so no graph is built (faster, and memory stays flat), and call <code>model.eval()</code> so layers like dropout switch to evaluation behavior.
!!!

#### 4 · Tensor and model on different devices — the mismatch
!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Cause:</b> an operation needs both of its inputs on the <i>same</i> device. If your model is on the GPU but a batch of data is still on the CPU (or vice versa), the op raises a runtime error about tensors on different devices. <b>Remedy:</b> keep <i>one</i> <code>device</code> variable (concept 6) and move <i>both</i> the model and every batch with <code>.to(device)</code>, so they always meet on the same hardware.
!!!

The pattern across all four: each is a direct consequence of autograd's design (accumulate, free the graph, track everything, live on a device), and each has a one-line habit that pays for the convenience. Knowing the *cause* is what lets you reach for the right fix instead of guessing.

#### The two capability limits — the sharpest point of the week
This is the most important idea to carry out of Module 5, so say it plainly, twice:

!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Capability limit 1 — no modeling judgment.</b> The framework does <i>not</i> choose your architecture, your loss, or your learning rate, and it will <i>not</i> diagnose why training failed. Autograd only computes correct gradients for whatever graph you build. Every real modeling decision — how many layers, which loss, what learning rate, why the loss plateaued — stays with <i>you</i>. !!!

!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>Capability limit 2 — autograd is not magic.</b> Automatic differentiation is <i>not</i> symbolic math that simplifies your model, and it is <i>not</i> an optimizer that fixes it. It will faithfully compute <i>correct</i> gradients for a <i>badly-designed</i> model — so a wrong model trains wrongly, smoothly, without a single complaint. The gradients being "correct" tells you nothing about whether the model is any good. No error will warn you; only your own judgment will.
!!!

#### Why a frontier lab cares — and why you built it by hand
Every real model — from a 10-line MLP to the largest language models — is stacked from exactly these four layers: the tensor/autograd substrate, `nn.Module`, a loss, and an optimizer. Nobody hand-derives backprop for a billion parameters. But *everybody who debugs* one leans on knowing what `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()` actually do underneath — which is the week you just spent. The framework is the *productivity* layer; your hand-built understanding is the *debugging* layer. Staff engineers are hired for the second: when a training run silently won't learn and autograd throws no error, they drop below the abstraction, reason about the actual gradients, and see *why*. You now hold both layers.
