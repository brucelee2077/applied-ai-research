Comprehensive Profiling
Reading a JAX/TPU Trace

Weeks 6-7 taught you the roofline: the best-case speed your hardware allows. But real programs fall short of that ceiling, and roofline math alone can't tell you why. Today you learn to capture a real timestamped recording of your program — a trace — and read it to find exactly where the wall-clock time actually goes.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: capture a JAX trace with jax.profiler.trace(...), open it in TensorBoard's trace viewer, spot an idle "bubble" on the accelerator track, and tell apart a host-to-device transfer bottleneck from an unwanted recompilation.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a profiler, and why do you need one?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Weeks 6-7's roofline analysis and TPU architecture — knowing what performance should be possible. No prior profiling experience needed.

The problem: roofline tells you the ceiling, not the reality

Roofline analysis gives you a number: "this program should take about 40ms per step." You run it, and it actually takes 90ms. Something is eating the other 50ms — but roofline math can't tell you what. Is the accelerator doing wasted work? Is it sitting idle? Is the compiler doing something dumb? You need to see what actually happened, not guess.

The answer: a profiler

A profiler is a tool that records, with real timestamps, exactly which piece of code or which hardware operation was running at every microsecond during your program's execution. Instead of guessing where time goes, you get a literal timeline.

JAX ships an XLA-integrated profiler. When you wrap a code region with jax.profiler.trace(log_dir), JAX asks the XLA runtime (the compiler that turns your JAX code into hardware instructions) to record every compiled operation that actually runs on the accelerator (TPU or GPU), plus what the host (the regular CPU machine feeding the accelerator) was doing at the same moments. It writes all of this to disk as a trace file.

Two programs running at once

A JAX program is really two programs running at the same time: your Python/host code (which traces, compiles, and dispatches work) and the compiled XLA code executing on the accelerator. These two sides talk to each other over a bus. Any moment the accelerator sits idle waiting on the host is called a bubble — and it is wasted money, because you pay for accelerator time whether it computes anything or not.

This week targets the two most common causes of bubbles:

  • Host-to-device (H2D) transfer bottlenecks — the accelerator finished its work and is waiting for the next batch of data to arrive, because the host was too slow preparing and copying it over.
  • Compiler inefficiencies — XLA either recompiled a function it shouldn't have (a jit retrace, usually a bug), or compiled your ops into a suboptimal sequence of small, unfused operations instead of one efficient fused kernel.

Two words you'll see in every trace

HLO (High Level Optimizer IR) is the intermediate form XLA compiles your JAX function into. The boxes you'll see on the accelerator track are HLO ops (things like fusion.3, all-reduce, copy-start) — not your original Python lines. Infeed is TPU vocabulary for the data-loading channel that streams batches from host memory into the TPU's on-chip buffers; an "infeed bottleneck" means the TPU's compute cores are idle waiting on this channel.

2 · INTUITIONA kitchen video review✓

Think of a restaurant kitchen line during dinner service.

🧑‍🍳
The chef = the accelerator

The chef on the line is your TPU/GPU — the expensive resource. Their hourly wage is your cloud bill, whether they're chopping or just standing there.

📋
The expediter = the host CPU

The expediter reads orders, plates garnishes, and slides finished dishes to the pass. This is the host feeding work to the chef.

A kitchen video review — the profiler trace — is a timestamped recording of every station: exactly when the chef started searing, when they finished, and every second they stood there with empty hands.

Watching the tape, you'd see two failure patterns:

  • The chef stands idle. They finish a dish, then wait 40 seconds because the expediter hasn't brought the next ticket and ingredients. That's a host-to-device transfer bottleneck (an infeed stall): compute idle, waiting on data from the slower, non-cooking side of the kitchen.
  • The chef fumbles. Instead of one clean motion (sear, flip, plate), they do five tiny disconnected motions with pauses between — pick up pan, set down, pick up spatula, set down — because the recipe card wasn't organized well. That's a compiler inefficiency: many small, unfused ops instead of one fused kernel.

In one line: the trace is a video of your program's kitchen — it shows you exactly when the expensive resource was working versus standing idle, and why.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real kitchen has one chef working sequentially, but a TPU pod has thousands of cores executing in lockstep across a mesh. The "waiting" you diagnose is often waiting on a collective communication step (like an AllReduce) between many chefs at once, not just one chef waiting on one expediter. There's no clean single-kitchen equivalent for that.

4 · HOW & WHYTensorBoard's trace viewer, and why it matters✓

What TensorBoard shows you

TensorBoard is a web dashboard (originally built for TensorFlow, now general-purpose) that reads logged data and renders it as interactive charts. Its Profiling Plugin reads JAX/TF/XLA trace files and renders a trace viewer: a zoomable, horizontal timeline (built on Google's Perfetto UI) where:

  • each horizontal row (a track) is a thread or hardware resource,
  • the x-axis is wall-clock time,
  • each colored box is one operation, sized to its duration.

The rules for reading it

  • The accelerator track (labeled by device id, e.g. /device:TPU:0) shows actual compiled HLO ops running on hardware.
  • The host tracks (Python/CPU threads) show dispatch, tracing, compilation, and input-pipeline activity.
  • A gap on the accelerator track with no boxes is a bubble — idle time you're paying for.
  • Line up the x-axis position of a bubble against the host tracks at that same moment — whatever is running there is what the accelerator is waiting for.
✅
Gap on accelerator track lines up with an input-pipeline/copy box on the host track → host-to-device bottleneck.
Repeated wide "XlaCompile" boxes across many steps on the host track → unwanted recompilation, not a hardware speed problem.

Why it matters

  • Diagnosis before treatment — without a trace, a 12ms gap could be misdiagnosed as "need a faster matmul kernel," wasting engineering time on the wrong fix, when it's actually a data-loading problem.
  • Real evidence — "TPU busy 76%" alone doesn't tell you why; the trace shows you the exact moment and the exact cause.
  • Cheap to capture, precise to read — a few lines of code produce a timeline detailed enough to see individual HLO ops.
🔗
Causal chain: roofline (Weeks 6-7) tells you the best-case step time → you measure the real step time and see a gap → profiling shows where in the timeline the gap lives → the trace viewer tells you why (data starvation vs. compiler issue) → you fix the right thing.

Acceptance criteria ("done" looks like)

  • experiments/week08_jax/profiling/train_loop.py runs end-to-end and produces trace directories under traces/ for a baseline run, both bug variants, and a fixed version.
  • experiments/week08_jax/profiling/findings.md contains: (a) a table of step-time and utilization numbers for at least 3 runs (buggy-recompile, buggy-infeed, fixed), (b) for each bug, one sentence naming the specific trace-viewer visual signature that diagnosed it (e.g. "repeated wide XlaCompile boxes on host track"), (c) confirmation that the fixed version's utilization number is measurably higher than at least one buggy version's.
  • All three profiling runs are viewable via tensorboard --logdir=experiments/week08_jax/profiling/traces/<run> without errors.
  • No claim in findings.md is asserted without a corresponding number or trace-viewer observation backing it.

The arc, step by step

  • Early: write a minimal but non-trivial JAX training loop (reuse the MLP/ViT from Weeks 1-5), instrumented with jax.profiler.trace(...) around a warmed-up steady-state window of at least 10 steps, saving traces to traces/baseline/.
  • Mid: deliberately introduce one bug from each category taught — an unwanted-recompilation bug via an unstable static arg, and a host-to-device bottleneck via an unprefetched/synchronous data-loading step. Capture a trace for each into traces/bug_recompile/ and traces/bug_infeed/, and write down in findings.md exactly which visual pattern in the trace viewer revealed each bug.
  • Late: fix both bugs, re-capture traces into traces/fixed/, and compute before/after step time and device-utilization (busy_time / total_step_time) for buggy vs. fixed, recorded in findings.md.
