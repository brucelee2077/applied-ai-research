Module 22b · Generate · Day 1

The Forward Process:
Turning a Picture into Static

A diffusion model makes pictures by learning to remove noise. But before you can teach a network to remove noise, you need a way to add it — cleanly and in tiny steps. Today you meet the forward process: a fixed recipe that sprinkles a little random noise onto a picture, again and again, until the picture is pure static. It sounds like breaking things on purpose. It is. And it is the whole foundation of image generation.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain the forward process as adding a little Gaussian noise at each of T steps, write the closed form x_t = √ᾱ_t · x0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) · ε, and say why you can jump straight to any noise level in one shot.
1 · WHAT IS ITA fixed recipe that slowly wrecks a picture✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know that an image is a grid of numbers (pixels), and from Module 22a you know a Gaussian is the bell-shaped random noise. Today you learn how to break a picture on purpose, one small step at a time.

The strange plan

You want a machine to make new pictures. The trick behind diffusion is backwards from what you'd guess. First you take a real picture and slowly destroy it into random noise. Then you train a network to undo that destruction. If a network can walk noise back to a picture, you can hand it fresh random noise and it will paint you something new.

The forward process

The forward process is the destroying half. You start with a clean picture. You add a little Gaussian noise. Then a little more. Then more again. After many steps — say T = 1000 — nothing is left but static. There is no learning here. It is a fixed recipe.

How much noise, and when

How much noise you add at each step is set ahead of time by a noise schedule. Early steps add barely any noise. Later steps add a lot. The step number is called the timestep t: t = 0 is the clean picture, t = T is pure noise.

The one shortcut that makes it all work

You never really add noise 1000 times. There is a closed form: one equation that jumps straight to any timestep t in a single shot. That shortcut is what makes training fast. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONA drawing dissolving into TV static✓
📺
The dissolving drawing

Picture a clear drawing on an old TV screen. Now the signal starts to fail. A little grainy static creeps in. Then more. Each moment the drawing is a bit fainter and the static a bit louder. Wait long enough and the drawing is gone — just pure random speckle.

⏱️
One knob: time

There is really one knob here: how long you wait, the timestep t. At t = 0 the drawing is perfect. Turn t up and the mix slides from "mostly drawing" toward "mostly static". At the last step it is all static, and every drawing ends up as the same kind of static.

In one line: the forward process is a drawing slowly dissolving into TV static, controlled by one time knob — and the recipe never changes and never learns.

Here is the useful part. Because the static comes from a simple bell-shaped random source (a Gaussian), a bit of math lets you skip ahead. You do not have to sit and watch every fade. You can set the knob straight to any moment and know exactly what the mix looks like. That skip-ahead is the closed form you will meet in Section 4.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a failing TV loses the drawing for good. Here the "static" is carefully chosen Gaussian noise, and the exact amount at each step is known. That is what lets a trained network run the fade backwards — something a broken TV could never do.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYThe closed form, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

To make the noisy picture at any step t, you take a fraction of the clean picture and add a fraction of pure random noise. Early on, most of the mix is the clean picture. Late on, most of the mix is noise. One number, ᾱ_t, decides the split. You do not step through every timestep — you plug in t and get the answer directly.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
x_t = √(ᾱ_t) · x0  +  √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε
# the closed form: jump straight to timestep t

Symbols: x0 = the clean picture. ε (epsilon) = pure Gaussian noise, same shape as the picture. ᾱ_t (alpha-bar at step t) = a number from the schedule, near 1 when t is small and near 0 when t is large. √(ᾱ_t) = how much of the clean picture you keep. √(1 − ᾱ_t) = how much noise you add. The two weights always satisfy (√ᾱ_t)² + (√(1−ᾱ_t))² = 1, so as one grows the other shrinks.

Step 3 — a worked example on one pixel

Say one pixel of the clean picture is x0 = 0.80, the noise for that pixel is ε = −1.0, and at this timestep ᾱ_t = 0.36:

🔢
√(ᾱ_t) = √0.36 = 0.6 # keep 60% of the pixel
√(1 − ᾱ_t) = √0.64 = 0.8 # add 80%-weight noise

x_t = 0.6 · 0.80 + 0.8 · (−1.0)
    = 0.48 + (−0.80)
x_t = −0.32 # the noisy pixel at step t

The clean value 0.80 pulled the result up; the noise −1.0 pulled it down harder, because at this timestep the noise weight (0.8) beats the signal weight (0.6). Do this for every pixel with its own noise sample and you get the whole noisy image x_t — in one shot, no loop.

Why the closed form matters

  • Training is fast: to train, you pick a random t and make x_t instantly. No walking through 1000 steps for every example.
  • The noise is known: you built x_t from a known ε, so you can later ask a network "which ε did I add?" — that is tomorrow's training target.
  • Any endpoint is one line: at large t, ᾱ_t → 0, so x_t is almost pure noise — the same starting point for making any new picture.
🔗
The big picture: the forward process is only half the story. It gives you (noisy picture, known noise) pairs for free. Tomorrow a network learns to reverse it: look at a noisy picture and undo one step. Run that reverse walk from pure noise and you generate a brand-new image.
