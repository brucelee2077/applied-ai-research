Module 22b · Generate · Day 2

The Reverse Process:Walking Static Back to a Picture

Yesterday you learned how to wreck a picture: add a little noise at each of T steps until it is pure static. Today comes the payoff. You teach a network to run that fade backwards — to look at a noisy picture and take away a little noise, one step at a time. Start from fresh random static, denoise it step after step, and a brand-new picture appears. This reverse walk is how a diffusion model actually generates images.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain the reverse process as a network removing a little noise at each step, describe how you generate by denoising from pure random noise, and say why you take many small steps instead of one big jump.
1A network that takes away a little noise, step by stepWhat is it
🧭
What this assumes: Day 1 — the forward process adds a little Gaussian noise at each of T steps, and the closed form x_t = √ᾱ_t · x0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) · ε jumps to any noise level. Today you learn to walk that fade backwards.

The reverse process

The forward process turned a picture into static. The reverse process undoes it. It starts from a noisy picture at step t and produces a slightly cleaner picture at step t−1. Do that from t = T all the way down to t = 0 and the static becomes a real picture.

What the network does

A neural network — the denoiser — looks at the noisy image and estimates what noise is in it. You subtract a little of that estimated noise, and you land one step closer to clean. The network does not repaint the whole picture at once. It nudges the image a small amount each step.

How you generate a new picture

To make something new, you do not start from a real photo. You start from pure random noise — the same static the forward process ended on. Then you run the denoiser step after step. Because the network learned to remove noise, each step reveals a bit more structure, and a fresh picture forms out of nothing.

Why many small steps

You could ask the network to jump from pure noise to a finished picture in one leap. It rarely works well — too much guessing at once. Taking many small denoising steps lets the network correct itself along the way, so the picture comes out sharp and believable. Keep going 👇

2Rewinding the static back into a drawingIntuition
⏪
Rewinding the tape

Yesterday you watched a drawing dissolve into static, going forward in time. Today you press rewind. The static slowly pulls itself back together: faint shapes appear, then edges sharpen, then a clear drawing returns. The reverse process is that rewind — but done by a network, not by playing back a saved tape.

🧹
Wiping fog off a mirror

Think of a foggy mirror. You wipe once and see a blurry shape. Wipe again, it gets clearer. Each wipe removes a little fog. The denoiser is the wipe: each step takes away a little noise, so the picture underneath shows through more and more.

In one line: the reverse process is a rewind — a network wipes away a little noise at each step, turning static back into a picture.

Here is the surprising part. The network never saw the original picture. It starts from fresh random static — noise it has never seen — and still produces something real. It can do this because during training it learned, over millions of examples, what "a little less noisy" should look like at every noise level. So it always knows which way to wipe.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: rewinding a tape replays exactly what was recorded. The network is not replaying anything — it is guessing the cleaner image from scratch. Give it different random static and it invents a different picture. That is why it can create things that never existed.

4One reverse step, worked out by handMechanism & why

Step 1 — in plain words

You have a noisy image at step t. First, the network looks at it and guesses the noise inside it. Second, you use that guess to estimate what the clean image probably looks like. Third, you do not jump all the way to clean — you take a small step back to step t−1, which is a little less noisy. Then you repeat with the new, cleaner image.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
est_noise = network(x_t, t)
x_{t-1} = (1/√α_t) · ( x_t − c_t · est_noise ) + noise_kick
# c_t is a small schedule number; α_t = 1 − β_t

Symbols: x_t = the noisy image at step t. network(x_t, t) = the denoiser's guess of the noise. α_t (alpha at step t) = 1 − β_t, a schedule number near 1. c_t = a small schedule factor that scales how much guessed noise to remove. noise_kick = a tiny bit of fresh random noise added back so the walk stays varied (it is set to zero on the very last step). The result x_{t-1} is the image at the next-cleaner step.

Step 3 — a worked example on one pixel

Say one pixel is x_t = −0.32, the network guesses the noise there is est_noise = −0.90, and the schedule gives 1/√α_t = 1.01 and c_t = 0.14:

🔢
remove = c_t · est_noise = 0.14 · (−0.90) = −0.126
x_t − remove = −0.32 − (−0.126) = −0.194
x_{t-1} = 1.01 · (−0.194) = −0.196 # + noise_kick (≈0 near the end)

Because the guessed noise was negative, subtracting it nudged the pixel up a little, toward its likely clean value. One pixel, one small step. Do this for every pixel and you get the whole image at step t−1 — a touch cleaner than x_t.

Why take many small steps

  • Each step is an easy guess: removing a little noise is a small, accurate ask. Removing all the noise at once is a wild guess.
  • Self-correction: if one step overshoots, the next step sees the new image and pulls back. Errors do not pile up.
  • Same network, reused: one denoiser handles every noise level, because you also feed it the timestep t so it knows how noisy the input is.
🔗
The big picture: generation is just this reverse step in a loop. Start at x_T = pure noise, call the denoiser, step to x_{T-1}, and keep going down to x_0. Out comes a new picture. Tomorrow you learn how the network was trained to guess that noise in the first place.
