Module 22b · Generate · Day 3

The Training Objective:
Guess the Noise You Added

Yesterday the denoiser magically guessed the noise in a picture. Today you learn how it got that good. The training goal is beautifully simple: take a clean picture, sprinkle a known amount of noise onto it, then ask the network "which noise did I just add?" Score its guess against the truth. Do this millions of times and the network learns to name the noise at every noise level. This one trick — predicting ε — is the entire training recipe.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain ε-prediction (the model predicts the noise that was added), write the loss as plain MSE between true and predicted noise, and walk through one full training step by hand.
1 · WHAT IS ITTrain the network to name the noise it was given✓
🧭
What this assumes: Day 1 — the forward closed form x_t = √ᾱ_t · x0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) · ε makes a noisy image from a known noise ε. Day 2 — the reverse process needs a network that guesses that noise. Today you learn how to train that network.

The clever training setup

Here is the trick. You make the training example yourself, so you already know the answer. Take a clean picture x0. Pick a random timestep t. Draw a random noise ε. Use the forward closed form to build the noisy image x_t. Now you know exactly which noise went in. The network's job is to look at x_t and guess that ε.

ε-prediction

This target — guessing the noise — is called ε-prediction (epsilon-prediction). The network does not try to output the clean picture. It outputs the noise. That turns out to be an easier, more stable thing to learn, and you can always recover the clean picture from the noise using the closed form.

The loss is just MSE

How do you score the guess? With mean squared error (MSE) between the true noise ε and the network's guess. Subtract, square, average. If the guess is perfect, the loss is zero. There is no fancy objective — it is the same simple squared-error you have seen before.

The schedule sets the difficulty

The random timestep t picks the noise level, using the β / ᾱ schedule from Day 1. Sometimes the network sees a barely-noised image (easy); sometimes near-pure static (hard). Training over all levels is why the same network can denoise every step later. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONGuessing exactly what static was sprinkled on✓
🧂
The salt game

Imagine a clean plate. You sprinkle a known pinch of salt on it, then hand it to a friend. Their job: tell you exactly where and how much salt you sprinkled. You already know the answer, so you can grade them precisely. Play this thousands of times and they get very good at spotting salt.

🎯
Score the guess

To grade, you compare their guessed salt map to the real one, pinch by pinch, and add up how far off they were. A big total means a bad guess. Zero means perfect. The friend just wants to shrink that total — that is the whole game.

In one line: training is the salt game — you add a known noise to a picture and the network guesses exactly what noise you added, scored by how far off it is.

Why guess the salt instead of the clean plate? Because you sprinkled the salt yourself, you know the perfect answer for free — no human labels needed. And once the friend can name the salt, removing it is easy: the clean plate is just "plate with salt, minus the salt". That is why ε-prediction, not picture-prediction, is the target.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real salt sits on top and is easy to see. Here the noise is blended into the pixels by math, and at high noise levels almost nothing of the picture is left — so guessing the exact noise is genuinely hard. That difficulty is why the network must be large and train for a long time.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYOne training step, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Pick a clean picture and a random step t. Draw a random noise ε. Build the noisy image with the Day 1 closed form. Feed the noisy image and t into the network; it outputs a guessed noise ε̂ (epsilon-hat). Measure how far ε̂ is from the true ε by squaring the differences and averaging. That number is the loss. Nudge the weights to make it smaller.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
x_t = √ᾱ_t · x0 + √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε  # build the example (Day 1)
ε̂ = network(x_t, t)  # the guess
loss = mean( (ε − ε̂)² )  # MSE, averaged over all pixels

Symbols: x0 = clean picture. ε = the true noise you drew. ε̂ = the network's guessed noise. ᾱ_t = the schedule number at step t. mean(·) = average over every pixel. (ε − ε̂)² = the squared miss at one pixel. The loss is just the average squared miss — plain MSE, nothing fancy.

Step 3 — a worked example on three pixels

Say for three pixels the true noise is ε = [0.5, −1.0, 0.2] and the network guesses ε̂ = [0.3, −0.6, 0.4]:

🔢
diffs = ε − ε̂ = [0.2, −0.4, −0.2]
squares = [0.04, 0.16, 0.04]
loss = mean = (0.04 + 0.16 + 0.04) / 3 = 0.24 / 3 = 0.08

# the gradient nudges weights so next guess is closer to ε

The loss 0.08 says the guess was a bit off, mostly on the middle pixel (missed by 0.4). Training uses the gradient of this loss to shift the weights so the next guess on a similar input is closer. Do this over millions of (picture, t, ε) triples and the network learns to name the noise at every level.

Why ε-prediction and MSE

  • Free labels: you made the noise, so the target is known with no human labeling. Endless training data.
  • Stable target: the noise ε is always a clean N(0,1) sample, so the network always predicts something with the same simple statistics, at every noise level.
  • Simple loss: MSE is easy to compute and differentiate, and it matches the Gaussian noise assumption — the math lines up.
🔗
The big picture: this is the entire diffusion training loop — build a noisy example, guess the noise, score with MSE, step. Once trained, the same network powers the reverse walk from Day 2. Tomorrow you learn how to sample smartly: fewer steps for speed, more for quality.
