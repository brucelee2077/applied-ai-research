Module 11 · Scale · Day 4

Probability
Primer

A model does not say "the answer is cat". It says "70% cat, 20% dog, 10% fox" — a spread of guesses over the choices. To train it, you need to measure how far its guesses are from the truth. That measure is cross-entropy, the very loss from M2. Today you finally see why it works: it is a distance between two distributions. You will also meet entropy (how surprising an outcome is) and sampling (drawing a real choice from the guesses).

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: read a probability distribution, explain entropy as average surprise, describe cross-entropy and KL divergence as a distance from the model's guesses to the truth, and connect it all back to the M2 loss.
1 · WHAT IS ITFour ideas that make the loss make sense✓
🧭
What this assumes: you have seen the cross-entropy loss used to train a classifier (M2) even if it felt like a magic formula. Today we unpack what it actually measures, using four small ideas.

1. A distribution — a spread of guesses

A probability distribution is just a list of chances over the choices, and the chances add up to 1. A model's answer is a distribution: "cat 0.7, dog 0.2, fox 0.1". The truth is also a distribution — usually a sharp one: "cat 1.0, dog 0.0, fox 0.0", because the real label is exactly cat.

2. Likelihood — how much the model backed the truth

The likelihood is the chance the model put on the correct answer. If the label is cat and the model said 0.7 for cat, the likelihood is 0.7. Good models give high likelihood to the truth. Training tries to push it up.

3. Entropy — how surprising an outcome is

Entropy measures surprise. A rare event is surprising; a sure thing is not. If your model is very confident (0.99 cat) its entropy is low — few surprises. If it is unsure (0.34 / 0.33 / 0.33) its entropy is high — every outcome is a bit of a shock.

4. Cross-entropy & KL — a distance to the truth

Cross-entropy is the average surprise you feel when the truth shows up but you were expecting the model's guesses. The extra surprise, beyond what was unavoidable, is the KL divergence — a kind of distance between the two distributions. Shrinking it pulls the model's guesses toward the truth. That is exactly what the M2 loss does. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONMeasuring how surprised you are✓
🌦️
Two weather apps

App A says "90% sunny". App B says "50% sunny, 50% rain". It turns out sunny. App A barely blinks — it expected this. App B is quite surprised — it was only half-sure. The one that expected the truth felt less surprise.

😲
Surprise is the score

Now the sky rains instead. App A, which said only 10% rain, is shocked. Its surprise is large. Cross-entropy is exactly this: the average surprise a model feels when the real outcomes arrive.

In one line: cross-entropy is the average "ouch, I did not expect that" a model feels across all the true answers — and training just tries to make that ouch as small as possible.

A model that puts high probability on the truth is rarely surprised, so its cross-entropy is low. A model that spreads its guesses thin, or bets on the wrong answer, gets shocked often, so its cross-entropy is high. The KL divergence is the part of that surprise the model could have avoided by matching the truth better.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: human surprise is a fuzzy feeling; cross-entropy is an exact number in "bits" or "nats" from the log of the probability. Also KL divergence is not a fair two-way distance — measuring from truth-to-model differs from model-to-truth. Treat it as "closeness", not a symmetric ruler.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYCross-entropy, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Surprise for one outcome is −log(probability). If you gave the true answer a high probability, the log is close to 0, so the surprise is small. If you gave it a tiny probability, the log is very negative, so the surprise is large. Cross-entropy averages this surprise over the truth. When the truth is one exact label, it simplifies to the surprise of that single class.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
cross-entropy = − Σ true[c] · log( model[c] )
# one-hot truth → this becomes just − log( model[correct] )

Symbols: c = each class (cat, dog, fox). true[c] = the truth's probability for class c (1 for the real label, 0 for the rest). model[c] = the probability the model gave class c. log = the natural logarithm. Σ = add over all classes. Because true is 0 everywhere except the real label, only that one term survives.

Step 3 — a worked example

Truth is cat. Compare a confident model and a shaky one.

🔢
confident: model[cat] = 0.7
  loss = −log(0.7) ≈ 0.36 # small surprise

shaky: model[cat] = 0.3
  loss = −log(0.3) ≈ 1.20 # big surprise

perfect: model[cat] = 1.0 → −log(1.0) = 0

Backing the truth with 0.7 costs only 0.36; backing it with just 0.3 costs 1.20 — over three times the surprise. A perfect 1.0 costs exactly 0. So minimizing cross-entropy pushes the model to raise the probability it puts on the true answer. The KL divergence is this cross-entropy minus the truth's own unavoidable entropy — the part you can actually remove by learning.

Why this is a big deal

  • It is the M2 loss: the "cross-entropy loss" you already trained with is exactly this average surprise. Now you know what the number means.
  • It rewards honest confidence: being sure and right is cheap; being sure and wrong is very costly (a tiny probability on the truth blows up the log).
  • Minimizing it = shrinking KL: lowering the loss pulls the model's distribution toward the true distribution, which is the whole goal of learning.
🔗
The big picture: every language model and classifier is trained by making cross-entropy small — that is, by being less and less surprised by real data. Sampling then goes the other way: given the trained distribution, you draw a concrete choice from it. These two ideas power both training and generation.
