Module 11 · Scale · Day 3

Error Bars &
the Noise Band

Yesterday you got a mean and a spread. Today you draw them. An error bar is a little line above and below each result that shows how far it might wobble — the "noise band". The single most useful rule in all of experiment reading lives here: if two results' bands overlap, you have not really shown one is better. A difference hiding inside the noise band is not a finding.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: draw an error bar from a mean and a spread, read whether two bars overlap, and correctly refuse to call a difference "real" when it sits inside the noise band.
1 · WHAT IS ITA line that shows how far a result can wobble✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know a mean is an average and a spread (std) is the wobble around it (Day 2). Today we turn those two numbers into a picture and use it to make a decision.

From two numbers to one picture

Yesterday you reported a result as 82.0 ± 0.3. That is a dot at 82.0 with a wobble of 0.3. An error bar draws exactly this: a dot for the mean, and a little line reaching up and down by the spread. Long bar means "very unsure"; short bar means "quite sure".

The noise band

The zone the bar covers — from mean minus spread to mean plus spread — is the noise band. Any number that falls inside this band is, honestly, the same as your result — the noise alone could have produced it. The band is where "we cannot tell the difference" lives.

The one rule that matters most

Put two results side by side with their error bars. If the bars overlap, you have not shown one is better than the other. The gap between them is smaller than the noise, so it could just be luck. Only when the bars clearly separate can you claim a real difference.

The takeaway in one line

A difference that fits inside the noise band is not a finding — it is noise wearing a costume. Draw the bars, check for overlap, and let the picture keep you honest. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONTwo runners whose time ranges overlap✓
🏃
Nobody runs the same time twice

A runner does not run the exact same time every race. Sometimes 10.1 seconds, sometimes 10.4. So her "true" speed is really a range, say 10.1 to 10.4. That range is her error bar.

🏅
When you cannot crown a winner

Runner B ranges 10.2 to 10.5. Her range and A's range overlap between 10.2 and 10.4. On any given day either could win. You have not truly ranked them — their bands overlap.

In one line: an error bar is the range a result lives in, and if two ranges overlap you cannot honestly say one beat the other.

Your models are the runners. The mean is the typical time, the spread is how much it varies race to race. If your new model's band overlaps the baseline's band, the "win" could be just a good race day — seed luck. Only clearly separated bands earn a real ranking.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: for runners the ranges come from real physical variation you can watch. For models the spread comes from random seeds and you must run several seeds to even see the band. Also, separated bands make a difference likely but never 100% certain — they lower the chance of being fooled, they do not remove it.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYThe overlap test, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Each result has a mean and a spread. Its band goes from mean minus spread to mean plus spread. To compare two results, look at the higher one's low edge and the lower one's high edge. If the lower result's top reaches above the higher result's bottom, the bands touch — they overlap — and you cannot claim a difference.

Step 2 — the formulas

🧮
band(x) = [ mean − spread , mean + spread ]
overlap if ( lowerA.mean + spreadA ) ≥ ( higherB.mean − spreadB )
# A is the lower result, B is the higher result

Symbols: mean = a result's average. spread = its std (the wobble). band = the range the bar covers. lowerA = the result with the smaller mean; higherB = the one with the bigger mean. If A's top edge is at or above B's bottom edge, the bands overlap.

Step 3 — a worked example

Two results: baseline = 82.0 ± 0.3, new idea = 82.3 ± 0.3.

🔢
baseline band = [82.0−0.3, 82.0+0.3] = [81.7, 82.3]
new idea band = [82.3−0.3, 82.3+0.3] = [82.0, 82.6]

baseline top 82.3 ≥ new idea bottom 82.0 ? YES
→ bands overlap → NOT a finding

The baseline's top (82.3) sits above the new idea's bottom (82.0), so the bands overlap in the range 82.0 to 82.3. The +0.3 gain is buried inside the noise. Now change the new idea to 83.4 ± 0.3: its bottom is 83.1, which is above the baseline's top of 82.3 — no overlap, so that gain is real.

Why this is a big deal

  • The picture cannot be faked: a table of numbers hides overlap; error bars show it instantly.
  • It stops false wins: most "+0.3% improvements" vanish once you draw the bars — they were inside the band all along.
  • Bigger spread needs bigger gains: a noisy setup (long bars) needs a large gain to separate; a steady setup (short bars) can prove a smaller one.
🔗
The big picture: the overlap test is the everyday version of a statistical significance test. It will not let you brag about a gain that is really noise. Combined with Day 1's clean ablations and Day 2's seed sweeps, you now have the full toolkit for a trustworthy result.
