Comparative BenchmarkingFlashAttention, ThunderKittens & Why Kernels Win

You've hand-written Pallas kernels and learned the ThunderKittens CUDA DSL. Today's new idea isn't a new algorithm — it's a new skill: reading the FlashAttention 1→2→3 lineage and the ThunderKittens paper as a chronological argument about hardware constraints, then proving that argument yourself with numbers on real hardware.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain why naive attention is memory-bound not compute-bound, trace what each FlashAttention generation fixed and why, explain ThunderKittens' "silicon constraints as the API" philosophy, and benchmark a from-scratch attention kernel against a naive implementation and a vendor library.
1What is IO-awareness, and why does it matter?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you've written a Pallas kernel and read about the ThunderKittens CUDA DSL in the last two days. You know what a GPU kernel is. No prior knowledge of FlashAttention needed.

The problem: attention looks compute-heavy, but it isn't the bottleneck

Standard attention computes a score matrix S = QK^T (query dot key, for every pair of positions), applies softmax, then multiplies by V. That looks like a lot of matrix multiplication — so you'd guess the GPU's math units are the bottleneck. They are not.

The real bottleneck is HBM (High Bandwidth Memory — the GPU's big but relatively slow "main memory," as opposed to the small, very fast on-chip scratchpad called SRAM). Naive attention writes the full N×N score matrix to HBM, reads it back to compute softmax, writes the softmax result back, and reads it again for the final multiply. For long sequences, that traffic — not the FLOP count — is what makes attention slow.

The answer: fuse the steps, never touch HBM in between

FlashAttention (2022) noticed this and fused QK^T → softmax → ×V into a single kernel using tiling: load small blocks of Q, K, V into SRAM, do all three steps right there on-chip, and only write the final output to HBM once. This is called IO-awareness — optimizing for memory traffic, not FLOP count.

📦
FlashAttention → FlashAttention-2 → FlashAttention-3: each generation squeezes more speed out of the same math (exact softmax attention) by matching the kernel more closely to how the GPU actually schedules work.
Then ThunderKittens asks a bigger question: instead of hand-tuning one kernel per GPU generation, can a small set of reusable abstractions make writing any fast kernel easy?

So what's today's catch?

Today is not about deriving new math. It's about reading a family of papers as a chronological argument about hardware constraints — then proving that argument yourself by benchmarking a from-scratch kernel against a naive one and a vendor library, and watching the memory-traffic gap appear in real numbers.

📚
Official docs and papers: FlashAttention paper · FlashAttention GitHub · FlashAttention-2 paper · FlashAttention-3 paper · FlashAttention-3 blog post · ThunderKittens paper · ThunderKittens GitHub
2The kitchen with a walk-in freezer and a cutting boardIntuition

Picture a professional kitchen with two zones: a giant walk-in freezer in the back (HBM — huge capacity, but every trip there and back costs real time), and a small set of prep bowls right on your cutting board (SRAM — tiny capacity, but instantly reachable).

🥶🚶
Naive attention = running to the freezer for every step

Get the tomatoes, chop, walk back. Get the onions, chop, walk back. Correct, but you spend most of your time walking, not cooking — the freezer trips (HBM round-trips) dominate.

🍱🔪
FlashAttention = one trip, one tray, cook it all at the board

Grab a full tray of ingredients (a tile) in one trip. Do every step for that tray using only the prep bowls (SRAM). Only walk back for the next tray. Online softmax is keeping a running "flavor total" in your head instead of laying out every tray at once.

In one line: the FlashAttention lineage is the story of a cook who stops making unnecessary trips to the freezer; ThunderKittens is the kitchen-designer who reshapes the counter and bowls so that the fast way and the natural way become the same thing.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real kitchen has one cook per station, but a GPU runs thousands of "cooks" (threads) truly in parallel across many stations (streaming multiprocessors). The hardest part of kernel engineering is choreographing all of them to hit the freezer and the cutting board at overlapping but non-colliding times — a synchronization problem this single-cook story doesn't capture at all.

4What each generation fixed, and why it mattersHow & why

The lineage, one fix at a time

  • FlashAttention (2022): fuses QK^T → softmax → ×V into one kernel using tiling (load Q/K/V blocks into SRAM) and online softmax (rescale running sums as new blocks arrive, so the full row of scores is never needed at once). Result: the exact same math, but O(N·d) HBM writes instead of O(N²).
  • FlashAttention-2 (2023): re-orders the loop nest to parallelize over sequence length (not just batch/heads) and cuts non-matmul FLOPs, roughly doubling GPU utilization by better matching how warps (groups of 32 threads the GPU schedules in lockstep) actually get scheduled.
  • FlashAttention-3 (2024): targets Hopper (H100) specifically. Exploits asynchrony between Tensor Cores and the TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator — copies HBM↔SRAM tiles without occupying a compute thread) via warp specialization (some warps only load, others only compute), plus FP8 quantization.
  • ThunderKittens (2024): asks whether tiling, online softmax, warp specialization, and async copies can be captured as reusable primitives instead of re-derived per kernel, per GPU generation.
✅
ThunderKittens' three abstraction levels mirror the GPU's actual memory/compute hierarchy: warp level — a 16×16 tile as the atomic data type (exactly what the Tensor Core's mma instruction wants); thread-block level — a template for overlapping async loads with compute across warps; grid level — hiding launch/teardown overhead across the whole grid.
The design philosophy is "silicon constraints as the API" — the abstraction mirrors what the hardware does, instead of presenting a general model that then has to be optimized away.

Why it's the core kernel-interview skill

  • IO-awareness generalizes — the same "count the HBM round-trips" argument explains why naive attention is slow, why FlashAttention-2 is faster than FlashAttention-1 at the same math, and why ThunderKittens ties CuBLAS on GEMM but wins big on newer ops.
  • The gap size is a signal — TK roughly ties mature, heavily hand-tuned baselines (GEMM, attention-inference) but wins 10-40% on attention-backward, 8x on state-space models, 14x on linear attention. Bigger gap = less prior hand-tuning investment in that baseline.
  • Theory meets the merge request — real inference engines like llama.cpp have to decide whether to adopt a faster kernel at all, weighing portability, maintenance burden, and measured speedup against integration risk (see the produce section).
🔗
Causal chain: attention is memory-bound (Section 1) → tiling + online softmax fixes it exactly once (FlashAttention) → loop order and hardware-specific asynchrony squeeze out more (FA-2, FA-3) → ThunderKittens generalizes the fix into reusable, hardware-aligned primitives → you benchmark to see the argument proven in wall-clock numbers.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

A benchmark is only evidence if it times the steady state — the way it quietly lies is by measuring the very first call.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): timing the cold / first call. The first time you invoke a kernel, the measured time includes things that never happen again: lazy CUDA context creation, JIT compilation, cuDNN/autotuner shape selection, and cold caches. Your timer runs fine and prints a number — it is just measuring startup, not the kernel. This makes a genuinely faster kernel look slower (or gives wildly high variance), and you ship the wrong conclusion with no error to hint at it. A senior engineer checks in code review that the harness does warmup iterations first, uses torch.cuda.synchronize() (or an equivalent device barrier) around the timed region so async launches are actually finished, and reports the median of many steady-state runs — not one cold call.
⚖️
Trade-off: fusing the whole op into one kernel buys HBM traffic but costs flexibility. FlashAttention wins by fusing QK^T → softmax → ×V so the N×N scores never touch HBM — that is the entire IO-awareness gain. The cost: the intermediate score matrix no longer exists to inspect, log, or reuse, the fused kernel is specialized to specific shapes/dtypes and much harder to write and maintain, and adopting it in a real engine adds portability and integration risk. In a design review you weigh the measured speedup against maintenance burden and how often the op's shape actually matches what the fused kernel was tuned for.

7Benchmark naive vs. fused vs. vendor attentionProduce

Today's task (from the curriculum): build a small reproducible benchmark suite comparing a from-scratch attention kernel against a naive unfused implementation and a vendor/library baseline — directly measuring the memory-traffic story behind the FlashAttention argument, scoped down to run without a CUDA/ThunderKittens toolchain (JAX Pallas stands in as the hardware-aligned kernel).

Acceptance criteria

  • bench_attention.py runs end-to-end with python3 sessions/m15b-native-kernels/day-05-comparative-benchmarking/experiment.py and exits 0.
  • It implements (1) naive unfused attention (materializes the full N×N score matrix), (2) a tiled/Pallas fused attention kernel with online softmax, (3) the built-in library attention as the vendor baseline.
  • Runs each across at least 3 sequence lengths (e.g. 512, 2048, 8192) and prints, for each of the 3 implementations × 3 lengths, a wall-clock time and an estimated bytes-moved figure (9 rows minimum).
  • The naive implementation's estimated bytes-moved must scale visibly closer to O(N²) than the fused/library implementations' O(N) scaling — the checkable numeric signature of IO-awareness.
