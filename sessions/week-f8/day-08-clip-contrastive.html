Module 6 · Represent · Day 8 (finale)

CLIP &
Contrastive Learning

The module finale. Yesterday you turned an image into an embedding. What if text could become an embedding in the same space? Then a photo and its caption would land close together, and you could match pictures to words directly. CLIP does exactly this: it pulls matching image-caption pairs together and pushes mismatched ones apart, until images and text share one map of meaning.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain contrastive image-text training (CLIP) — pull matching image/caption embeddings together, push mismatched ones apart — and what a shared embedding space unlocks, like zero-shot classification.
1 · WHAT IS ITOne shared space for images and text✓
🧭
What this assumes: the image embedding from Day 6 (an image becomes a fixed-length list of numbers), and the idea of comparing embeddings by distance. Today we add a second encoder — for text — that lands in the same space.

Two encoders, one space

CLIP has two parts. An image encoder turns a photo into an embedding. A text encoder turns a sentence into an embedding. Both outputs live in the same shared embedding space.

The training goal

CLIP learns with contrastive learning. Show it many image-caption pairs. For each real pair (a dog photo with "a photo of a dog"), pull the two embeddings together. For every wrong pair (that dog photo with "a photo of a car"), push them apart.

What you get

After training on hundreds of millions of pairs, a photo and its true caption sit close; a photo and a wrong caption sit far. Now you can compare any image to any text directly. This unlocks zero-shot classification — labeling images using only text descriptions of the classes. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONA memory game: match photos to captions✓
🃏
The matching game

Lay out 4 photos in a row and 4 captions in a column. Your job: draw a line from each photo to the caption that describes it. Get every match right, and you have understood both the pictures and the words.

🧲
Pull matches, push mismatches

CLIP plays this game with millions of cards. Each round it pulls the right photo-caption pairs closer in embedding space and pushes every wrong pairing apart. Over time, matching things become magnets and mismatches repel.

In one line: CLIP learns to win a giant matching game — line up each image with its caption — and in doing so builds one shared space where meaning is measured by closeness.

Because it learned from natural captions, not fixed labels, it can later match a photo to any phrase you write — even categories it never saw as training labels. That is where zero-shot comes from.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: in a card game you truly understand each picture. CLIP does not understand a dog — it only learned that dog photos and the word "dog" tend to produce nearby embeddings. It is pattern-matching at huge scale, not real understanding.

Tip: CLIP compares embeddings with cosine similarity — the angle between two vectors — not raw distance. Close angle = high match.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYThe similarity grid, worked out✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Take a batch of N image-caption pairs. Encode all N images and all N captions. Now score every image against every caption, filling an N×N grid. The correct match for each image is the caption on the diagonal. Training pushes the diagonal scores up and the off-diagonal scores down.

Step 2 — the similarity score

🧮
cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| · |b|)
# dot product / (length of a × length of b)

Symbols: cosine similarity cos(a,b) measures the angle between two embeddings. a · b = the dot product (multiply matching numbers, add them). |a| = the length of a. Result 1 = same direction (great match), 0 = unrelated.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say an image embedding and two caption embeddings (kept short for clarity):

🔢
img = [1, 0]
"a dog" = [0.8, 0.6]
"a car" = [0, 1]

cos(img, "a dog") = (1·0.8 + 0·0.6) / (1 · 1) = 0.8
cos(img, "a car") = (1·0 + 0·1) / (1 · 1) = 0.0

(Both caption vectors have length 1 here.) The image scores 0.8 with "a dog" and 0.0 with "a car". So the model reads this image as a dog — the higher score wins. That is the whole matching decision.

Why a shared space is powerful

  • Zero-shot classification: to label an image, encode the class names as text ("a photo of a cat", "a photo of a dog"), and pick the one with the highest cosine score. No training on those classes needed.
  • Text-to-image search: type a phrase, encode it, and find the images whose embeddings are closest — a search engine over pixels.
  • A bridge to bigger models: CLIP's shared space feeds image generators and vision-language chat models.
🔗
The big picture: "encode two things into one space, then compare" is the same idea you met on Day 6, now spanning images and text. It is the foundation under image search, captioning, and the vision half of multimodal AI.
