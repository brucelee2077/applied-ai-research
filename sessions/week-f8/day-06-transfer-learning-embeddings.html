Module 6 · Represent · Day 6

Transfer Learning
& Embeddings

Training a big CNN from scratch takes millions of images and days of compute. Almost nobody does that. Instead you borrow a network someone already trained, chop off its last decision layer, and keep the rest as a ready-made feature extractor. Feed it an image and read the layer just before the head — a short list of numbers that captures what the image looks like. That list is an embedding.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain transfer learning (reuse a pretrained CNN, chop the head), and describe how the penultimate layer becomes a fixed-length image embedding you can use for search, clustering, or a new classifier.
1 · WHAT IS ITBorrow a network, keep its features✓
🧭
What this assumes: the CNN blocks from Day 3 (conv → pool → … → flatten → dense) and the idea of embeddings from earlier modules. Today we reuse someone else's trained network.

Why not train from scratch

A strong image network learns from millions of pictures (like ImageNet) over days on many GPUs. Most projects have neither. So instead of training, you borrow.

Transfer learning

Transfer learning takes a network that is already trained and reuses it for your problem. The early layers already know edges, textures, and shapes — useful for almost any image task. You keep those and only change the very end.

Chop the head

The last layer — the head — is specific to the classes the network was trained on (say, 1000 ImageNet categories). You chop it off. What remains is a feature extractor.

The penultimate layer is an embedding

Just before the head sits the penultimate layer. Its output is a fixed-length list of numbers — an embedding — that captures what the image looks like. Similar images give similar embeddings. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONHiring an expert for their summary✓
🧑‍🔬
The expert already knows a lot

Imagine hiring a wildlife expert who has studied millions of animal photos for years. You do not want to teach them from zero. You just show them a new photo and ask for a short summary of what they see.

📝
You only need the summary

Their summary — "furry, four legs, pointed ears, small" — captures the important parts in a few words. You can compare summaries to find similar animals, or feed the summary to a tiny helper who decides the exact species. That summary is the embedding.

In one line: the pretrained network is the expert; the penultimate layer is its short written summary of the image; you build only the small final decision on top.

Because the expensive part (the expert's years of study) is already done, you get strong results from just a few hundred of your own examples.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a human expert can reason about a photo they have never seen a category for. The network's summary only reflects patterns it met during its original training. If your images look very different from that training data (say, medical scans vs. everyday photos), the summary may be a poor fit and you may need to retrain more of the network.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYFrom image to embedding, and comparing two✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Run the image through all the borrowed layers up to the penultimate one. That gives a fixed-length list of numbers — the embedding. Two similar images produce two similar lists. To measure "similar", you compare the two lists with a distance.

Step 2 — the distance formula

🧮
distance(a, b) = sqrt( Σ (a[i] − b[i])² )
# Euclidean distance; smaller = more similar

Symbols: a, b = two embeddings (lists of numbers); a[i], b[i] = the i-th number in each; Σ = add over every position. A small distance means the two images look alike to the network.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say two images give these tiny 3-number embeddings, and a third is different:

🔢
cat_A = [0.9, 0.1, 0.4]
cat_B = [0.8, 0.2, 0.5]
car = [0.1, 0.9, 0.3]

dist(cat_A, cat_B) = sqrt(.01+.01+.01) = sqrt(.03) ≈ 0.17
dist(cat_A, car) = sqrt(.64+.64+.01) = sqrt(1.29) ≈ 1.14

The two cats sit close (0.17) and the car sits far (1.14). Without ever training a cat-vs-car classifier, the borrowed embeddings already separate them. That is the power you are reusing.

Two ways to use it

  • Feature extraction: freeze the borrowed weights, compute embeddings once, and train only a tiny classifier on top. Fast, works with little data.
  • Fine-tuning: unfreeze the last few layers and keep training them on your images. Slower, needs more data, but can do better when your task is unusual.
  • Search & clustering: compare embeddings directly to find similar images or group them — no classifier at all.
🔗
The big picture: "turn an input into an embedding, then compare embeddings" is one of the most reused ideas in all of AI. Tomorrow's finale, CLIP, puts image and text embeddings into the same space so you can compare a photo to a sentence.
