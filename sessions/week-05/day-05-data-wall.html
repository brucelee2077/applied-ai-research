The Data Wall

All week you've learned that compute-optimal training needs model size and dataset size to grow together (Chinchilla). But the internet's supply of high-quality human text is not infinite. Today: when we run out, and what labs do about it.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why frontier training demand is on a collision course with the world's supply of human text, do the demand-vs-supply math yourself, and describe the two rescue plans — data repetition and synthetic data — with their own quantified limits.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is "the data wall"?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Chinchilla-style compute-optimal scaling from earlier this week (model size and dataset size grow together for a fixed compute budget). No prior knowledge of data supply/demand forecasting needed.

A quick reminder: why training needs more than a bigger model

Training tokens are the chunks of text — roughly word-pieces — that a model is shown while it learns, during a stage called pretraining. Earlier this week you learned compute-optimal scaling (the Chinchilla result): for a fixed compute budget, you should not just grow the model. You should grow model size (N) and dataset size (D) together, in a fixed ratio. That means every time frontier labs want a bigger, better model, they also need more unique tokens — not just more parameters.

The problem: tokens have to come from somewhere

For years, "more tokens" meant "scrape more of the public internet." That worked because the internet was, for practical purposes, bigger than any single training run's appetite. But two things are true at once: compute keeps compounding fast (roughly 4-5x per year), so token demand keeps growing fast too — while the pool of high-quality, human-written text on the internet grows only as fast as people keep writing new things online, which is much slower.

The data wall is the point where those two curves cross: the point where a frontier training run would need more unique, high-quality human-written text than exists on the public internet. Villalobos et al. built a supply-and-demand forecast for exactly this crossing point (arXiv 2211.04325).

So what's today's catch?

Hitting the wall does not mean progress stops. It means the free, easy lever — "just scrape more unique human text" — stops working. Labs then reach for two rescue plans, each covered later today: repeating data (showing the model the same text more than once) and synthetic data (text generated by a model instead of scraped from humans). Both have their own diminishing-returns curves, which we'll quantify.

📚
Papers for today (optional, cited throughout): Villalobos et al., "Will we run out of data?" (2211.04325) · Muennighoff et al., "Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models" (2305.16264) · Qin et al., "Scaling Laws of Synthetic Data for Language Models" (2503.19551)
2 · INTUITIONThe wild fish stock a growing city depends on✓

Think of high-quality human text like the world's supply of a wild-caught fish that a growing city depends on for food.

🐟🌊
Early years: just fish more

The city's population grows, so it fishes a bit more of the ocean each year. That's fine, because the ocean is, for practical purposes, bigger than any single year's appetite.

🏙️📈
Now: appetite compounds, the ocean doesn't

The city's population (LLM compute and its appetite for tokens) keeps compounding fast. The ocean's total stock (all the good text humans have ever written) grows only slowly, tied to how many people are online writing things.

In one line: at some point the city's yearly catch approaches the entire remaining stock of wild fish — that's the data wall, projected here for around 2028.

Once you hit that wall, you have two moves. Re-serve the fish you already caught — repeat training epochs. The same fish is still nutritious the second and third time, but by the tenth reheating everyone's sick of it and it stops providing much value. That maps directly to Muennighoff's finding: "4 epochs is fine, 40 epochs is worthless." Or build a fish farm — synthetic data, cultivating new fish from existing genetic stock rather than trawling the wild ocean.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: farmed fish (synthetic data) can in principle be engineered to be more nutritious per unit than wild fish, because a strong "chef" model curates and recombines the best parts of existing knowledge. Synthetic data is not simply a diluted copy of the original — that's exactly why the concept-recombination approach you'll see later beats naive resampling.

4 · HOW & WHYHow the wall was forecast, and why the two rescue plans work✓

How Villalobos et al. forecast the wall

  • Demand side: extrapolate the historical growth in training-dataset size across notable models (GPT-3 → PaLM → Falcon → DBRX → Llama 3).
  • Supply side: estimate the "stock" of public human text ever posted to the indexed and deep web — roughly 3,100 trillion tokens (90% CI: 1,900T–5,200T), growing as global internet population grows.
  • Crossing point: the year demand equals supply — median 2028, range 2026–2032 — at roughly 4×1014 (400 trillion) tokens, about 5×1028 FLOP of training compute.
✅
Sensitivity check: even doubling the internet's assumed growth rate (10% → 20%/year) only shifts the stock estimate by about 0.4 orders of magnitude — so the 2026–2032 window is fairly robust to that one assumption.

Why the two rescue plans work (and where they stop working)

  • Repetition (Muennighoff et al.): showing the model the same unique data U multiple times gives an effective unique-data amount D' that grows fast at first, then flattens. Up to ~4 epochs costs almost nothing versus fresh data; by ~40 epochs, one more pass is worth essentially zero.
  • Synthetic data (Qin et al., SynthLLM): extract high-level "concepts" from real documents with a graph algorithm, then recombine them across documents into new synthetic examples — injecting cross-document diversity instead of paraphrasing one document at a time. This makes synthetic data follow its own predictable scaling law rather than degrading ("model collapse") as you generate more of it.
🔗
Causal chain: compute-optimal scaling (this week) → dataset size must grow with model size → historical growth in dataset size projected forward → crosses the finite stock of human text around 2028 → the two levers left are reusing data smarter (repetition) or making new data (synthetic), each with its own diminishing-returns curve.

⚠️
Common gotcha: "we'll run out of data in 2028" does not mean training physically halts in 2028. It's a median forecast under current trends, with a 2026-2032 range that's sensitive to overtraining assumptions. And "running out of data" doesn't mean loss stops decreasing — it means the free, easy lever (scrape more unique human text) stops working, forcing a shift to levers with their own diminishing-returns curves. Distinguish "exhaustion of one input" from "exhaustion of all possible progress."
