Module 9b · Scale · Day 1

Float Formats:
fp32, fp16, bf16

A computer stores a number using a fixed budget of bits. Split that budget one way and you can reach very big and very small numbers. Split it another way and you get more exact digits instead. Today you learn how a floating-point number is built from three parts, and why one clever format called bf16 almost never breaks even though it holds half as many bits as normal.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: name the three parts of a float (sign, exponent, mantissa), say which part controls range and which controls precision, and explain why bf16 rarely overflows but is coarser than fp16.
1 · WHAT IS ITThree parts share a fixed bit budget✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know a model is a big pile of numbers (weights and gradients) that get updated during training. You do not need to know binary. Today we look at how one such number is stored, because the choice of storage decides how fast and how safe training is.

The problem: numbers cost memory

A large model has billions of numbers. Each number takes space. Storing every number with lots of digits is safe but slow and memory-hungry. Storing them with fewer digits is fast and small, but you might lose important information. Picking the storage format is a real trade-off.

How one number is stored: three parts

A floating-point number is built from three parts. First the sign says positive or negative. Second the exponent sets the scale, like which power of two the number sits near. Third the mantissa holds the exact digits inside that scale.

Two things you can buy with bits

The exponent buys dynamic range — how far you can reach toward huge and tiny values. The mantissa buys precision — how finely you can tell two close numbers apart. A fixed bit budget means giving more to one leaves less for the other.

The three formats you will meet

fp32 uses 32 bits (1 sign, 8 exponent, 23 mantissa): wide range, fine precision, but big and slow. fp16 uses 16 bits (1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 mantissa): small and fast, but its range is narrow. bf16 also uses 16 bits (1 sign, 8 exponent, 7 mantissa): same wide range as fp32, but coarser digits. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONWriting numbers in scientific notation✓
✏️
The scientific-notation card

You have a small card. On it you may write a number only as d.dd × 10^e — a few digits, then a power of ten. The power of ten is the exponent. The few digits are the mantissa. The card has a fixed size, so the two must share the space.

⚖️
The trade you must make

Spend more of the card on the power (the exponent) and you can write huge numbers like 3 × 10^30 and tiny ones like 2 × 10^-30. Spend more on the digits (the mantissa) and you write 3.14159 instead of just 3.1. You cannot have both on one small card.

In one line: the exponent is how many zeros you can reach (range), and the mantissa is how many exact digits you get to keep (precision). A 16-bit format is a smaller card than a 32-bit one, so something has to give.

fp16 spends its card mostly on digits: fine precision, but a small power-of-ten range, so it cannot write very big or very tiny numbers. bf16 spends the same as fp32 on the power-of-ten range but keeps fewer digits: it reaches just as far, but is rougher up close.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real floats use powers of two, not ten, and the digits are binary. Also, near zero the gaps between representable numbers get very fine, while far from zero they get very wide — the card metaphor hides that the spacing is not even.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYWhat each format can represent, worked out✓

Step 1 — in plain words

The exponent bits set how far you can reach. With 8 exponent bits you can reach numbers near 10^38 and near 10^-38. With only 5 exponent bits you reach just to about 65,504 at the top. The mantissa bits set the gap between numbers you can write. More mantissa bits means smaller gaps, so you can tell close numbers apart.

Step 2 — the formulas

🧮
value = (−1)^sign × (1 + mantissa) × 2^(exponent − bias)
# sign = 0 or 1 · mantissa is a fraction from the mantissa bits
# exponent from the exponent bits · bias re-centers it around 0

top_value ≈ 2^(max_exponent) # set by exponent-bit count
smallest_gap ≈ 2^(−mantissa_bits) # set by mantissa-bit count

Symbols: sign = 1 bit (plus or minus). exponent = the scale bits (range). mantissa = the digit bits (precision). bias = a fixed number subtracted so the exponent can go negative. More exponent bits push top_value higher; more mantissa bits make smallest_gap finer.

Step 3 — a worked comparison

Compare the three formats on the two things that matter: how far they reach, and how fine their gaps are.

🔢
format exp-bits mantissa top value gap near 1
fp32 8 23 ~3.4 × 10^38 ~1.2 × 10^-7
fp16 5 10 ~65,504 ~9.8 × 10^-4
bf16 8 7 ~3.4 × 10^38 ~7.8 × 10^-3

# fp16 tops out at 65,504 — a big activation overflows to inf
# bf16 reaches 10^38 like fp32, but its gap is 8× wider than fp16

fp16 and bf16 both use 16 bits, so they must lose something next to fp32. fp16 kept precision and paid with range: its top value is only 65,504. bf16 kept range and paid with precision: it reaches 10^38 like fp32, but its gap near 1 is about 0.008 — eight times coarser than fp16.

Why bf16 rarely overflows

  • Same exponent width as fp32: bf16 has 8 exponent bits, exactly like fp32, so any value fp32 can hold, bf16 can also reach without going to infinity.
  • fp16's narrow range is the danger: a large gradient or activation above 65,504 becomes inf in fp16 and training breaks. bf16 does not hit that wall.
  • bf16's cost is coarseness: fewer mantissa bits means bf16 rounds harder. For deep-learning training this is usually fine, because the results are averaged over many numbers.
🔗
The big picture: this one choice — spend bits on range or on precision — decides whether a 16-bit training run stays alive. bf16 became the default on modern accelerators precisely because matching fp32's range avoids the most painful failures.
