Module 9b · Scale · Day 2

Loss Scaling
& Master Weights

Yesterday you saw fp16 has a narrow range. That narrow range bites at the bottom too: many gradients are so tiny they round down to zero in fp16, and a zero gradient means that weight stops learning. Today you learn the two-part fix that saved fp16 training: keep a full-precision copy of the weights, and turn the loss "volume" up before the backward pass so small gradients survive.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why small fp16 gradients underflow to zero, describe fp32 master weights and loss scaling by a factor S, and walk through a gradient that dies without scaling but survives with it.
1 · WHAT IS ITTiny gradients vanish, so we amplify them✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Day 1 you know fp16 has a narrow range. From M2 you know the training loop: a forward pass makes a loss, a backward pass makes gradients, and the optimizer nudges each weight. Today we look at what happens to those gradients when they are stored in fp16.

The problem: gradients are often tiny

A gradient tells each weight how to change. Deep in a network these numbers are often very small — around 0.00001 or less. fp16's smallest normal number is about 0.00006. Anything smaller rounds to zero. This is called underflow.

Why underflow stalls training

A gradient of zero tells the optimizer "do not change this weight". If many gradients underflow to zero, most of the model stops learning. The loss flattens and training stalls — the run wastes time and money going nowhere.

Fix part 1: fp32 master weights

Keep one exact copy of every weight in fp32. This copy is the master weights. The forward and backward passes use a fast fp16 copy, but the actual update is applied to the fp32 master, so tiny updates are not lost to rounding.

Fix part 2: loss scaling

Before the backward pass, multiply the loss by a big number S. Because of the chain rule, every gradient also gets multiplied by S, lifting the tiny ones up out of the underflow zone. After the backward pass, divide the gradients by S to put them back to their true size. This is loss scaling. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONTurn the recording volume up, then back down✓
🎙️
Recording a whisper

You want to record a very quiet sound. Your microphone has a floor — sounds below it are recorded as pure silence. A whisper falls below the floor, so the recording is just zero. The whisper is lost, the same way a tiny gradient rounds to zero in fp16.

🔊
Turn the gain up first

So you turn the recording gain way up before you record. Now the whisper is loud enough to land above the floor and gets captured. Later, when you play it back, you turn the volume back down by the same amount, and the whisper sounds normal again.

In one line: loss scaling turns the "gain" up (multiply the loss by S) so tiny gradients are loud enough to survive fp16, then turns it back down (divide by S) so the real update size is restored.

The fp32 master weights are like keeping the original, un-compressed master tape. All the day-to-day work happens on the small fast copy, but the true, exact record lives in the fp32 master, so nothing important is quietly thrown away.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a microphone's gain is one fixed knob you set by hand. Loss scaling can be automatic — the factor S goes up when gradients look safe and drops the moment a gradient overflows to infinity. That self-adjusting version is called dynamic loss scaling.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYOne gradient, dying then rescued✓

Step 1 — in plain words

The backward pass makes a gradient for each weight. If a gradient is smaller than fp16's smallest storable number, it becomes zero — the weight will not move. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a factor S before the backward pass. By the chain rule, this multiplies every gradient by S too, so tiny gradients get pushed above the zero-threshold. After the backward pass you divide the gradients by S to get their true size back, then update the fp32 master weights.

Step 2 — the formulas

🧮
scaled_loss = loss × S # S is the loss-scale factor
grad_scaled = backward(scaled_loss) # = grad_true × S (chain rule)
grad_true = grad_scaled ÷ S # unscale before the update
master_fp32 = master_fp32 − lr × grad_true

Symbols: loss = the training loss. S = the scale factor (a big number like 1024). grad_scaled = the gradient while it is still S times too big. grad_true = the real gradient after dividing by S. lr = the learning rate. master_fp32 = the full-precision copy of the weight.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say a gradient's true value is 0.00003, and fp16's smallest normal value is about 0.00006. Use a scale factor S = 1024.

🔢
without scaling:
  grad_true = 0.00003 < 0.00006
  stored in fp16 → 0.0 # underflow, weight frozen

with scaling (S = 1024):
  grad_scaled = 0.00003 × 1024 = 0.0307 # well above 0.00006
  stored in fp16 → 0.0307 # survives!
  unscale: 0.0307 ÷ 1024 = 0.00003 # true value back

Without scaling, 0.00003 is below fp16's floor, so it becomes 0.0 and the weight is stuck. With S = 1024, the gradient becomes 0.0307 — far above the floor — so fp16 keeps it. Dividing by 1024 afterward returns the true 0.00003, and that update lands on the fp32 master weight.

Why this works and why dynamic S

  • The chain rule does the work: scaling the loss once scales every gradient by the same S, so you fix all of them with a single multiply.
  • fp32 master weights catch the tiny update: even after unscaling, the update may be small; applying it to the fp32 copy avoids losing it to rounding again.
  • Dynamic loss scaling: too-large S can push gradients past fp16's top (65,504) into inf. So S starts high, and if any gradient overflows, that step is skipped and S is halved; after many safe steps, S is doubled. It hunts for the biggest safe S automatically.
🔗
The big picture: fp16 needs this crutch because its range is narrow at both ends. bf16, with fp32-wide range, mostly does not need loss scaling at all. Tomorrow you assemble the full mixed-precision recipe that ties master weights, scaling, and format choice together.
