Module 9b · Scale · Day 4

fp8:
E4M3, E5M2 & Scaling

Half the bits again. fp8 uses just 8 bits per number, so it is even faster and smaller than bf16 — but there is almost no room left for digits. To use it safely you must pick the right 8-bit layout for each job and rescale numbers on the fly so they land in the tiny window fp8 can hold. Push too hard and activations overflow into NaN and the whole run dies. This is the module finale.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: compare fp8's two formats (E4M3 for precision, E5M2 for range), explain per-tensor / block / delayed scaling, and say why the forward pass is safer for fp8 than the backward pass.
1 · WHAT IS ITEight bits, two layouts, and a scale to survive✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Day 1 you know a float is sign, exponent, mantissa, and that exponent buys range while mantissa buys precision. From Day 3 you know mixed precision keeps some parts in fp32. Today we push down to just 8 bits and see how much more careful you must be.

The idea: even fewer bits

fp8 stores each number in 8 bits. That is half of bf16 and a quarter of fp32. It makes matmuls faster and cuts memory again. But with only 8 bits total, there is very little to split between range and precision.

Two layouts, because 8 bits is not enough for both

fp8 comes in two flavors. E4M3 spends 4 bits on exponent and 3 on mantissa — more precision, narrower range. E5M2 spends 5 on exponent and 2 on mantissa — wider range, coarser precision. You pick per job.

You must rescale numbers to fit

fp8's window is so small that raw values often fall outside it. So you multiply each tensor by a scale factor to bring its values into fp8's range, then divide back out after. Where that scale comes from names the method: per-tensor, block, or delayed scaling.

The danger: overflow to NaN

If a value is bigger than fp8's top, it overflows. Overflow can produce NaN (not a number). One NaN spreads through the next matmuls and the loss becomes NaN — training is dead. This is why fp8 needs careful scaling, and why it is used only where it is safe. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONShrinking a photo — fine until detail vanishes✓
🖼️
Shrinking the photo

You shrink a big photo to save space. Made a bit smaller, it still looks fine — you cannot tell the difference. This is fp8 on forgiving data: fewer bits, but the result still works because small details did not matter here.

🔳
Shrink too far

Keep shrinking and suddenly faces turn to blocky mush — the fine detail is gone for good. This is fp8 pushed onto values it cannot hold: the number rounds to something wrong, or overflows to a broken value, and you cannot get the detail back.

In one line: fp8 is a very small photo — great when the detail did not matter, ruinous when it did, so you must choose carefully where to use it.

Rescaling is like zooming the important part of the photo to fill the frame before you shrink it: you fit the values you care about into the small space fp8 can hold, then zoom back out afterward. Pick the wrong zoom and the picture clips off the edges — that clipping is overflow to NaN.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: shrinking a photo just blurs it, but an fp8 overflow does worse than blur — it can produce a NaN, a broken value that spreads and destroys the whole computation, not just one number.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYScaling values into fp8's tiny window✓

Step 1 — in plain words

fp8 can only hold values inside a small window (E4M3 tops out around 448). If a tensor's biggest value is above that, casting to fp8 overflows to a broken value. So before casting you find a scale factor that shrinks the tensor to fit just under the top, cast to fp8, do the matmul, then divide the result by that same scale to undo it. Choosing the scale from the tensor's own biggest value is the whole trick.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
scale = fp8_max ÷ max(abs(tensor)) # fit the biggest value just under the top
x_fp8 = cast_fp8( tensor × scale ) # now everything fits
out = matmul(x_fp8, w_fp8) # fast 8-bit matmul
out = out ÷ (scale_x × scale_w) # undo both scales

Symbols: fp8_max = the largest value the fp8 format can hold (about 448 for E4M3). max(abs(tensor)) = the biggest size in the tensor. scale = the multiplier that brings that biggest value to the top of the window. x_fp8, w_fp8 = the fp8 inputs. out = the matmul result, then unscaled.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say a tensor's biggest value is 1200.0, but E4M3 tops out at 448. Without scaling it overflows. With per-tensor scaling:

🔢
without scaling:
  cast_fp8(1200.0) > 448 → overflow → NaN

with per-tensor scaling:
  scale = 448 / 1200 = 0.373
  1200.0 × 0.373 = 448 → fits in fp8 ✓
  matmul in fp8, then out ÷ 0.373 # true scale restored

Without scaling, 1200 is above 448 and casting to fp8 overflows, which can turn into a NaN that spreads. With a scale of 0.373, the biggest value maps exactly to 448 — the top of the window — so nothing overflows, and dividing the output by the scale afterward restores the true numbers.

The three scaling styles, and where fp8 is safe

  • Per-tensor scaling: one scale for the whole tensor — cheap, but one huge outlier forces a tiny scale that crushes everything else.
  • Block scaling: a separate scale for each small block, so one outlier only affects its own block — safer, slightly more bookkeeping.
  • Delayed scaling: reuse a scale measured on recent steps instead of recomputing it every step — faster, at the risk of being a little stale if the values shift.
  • Forward safer than backward: weights and activations in the forward pass are fairly well-behaved and use E4M3 (precision). Gradients in the backward pass have a much wider spread and use E5M2 (range) — and are the most likely place for an overflow, so fp8 there is riskier.
🔗
The big picture: fp8 is the sharpest point of the whole range-vs-precision trade. It gives another big speed and memory win, but only with careful per-tensor or block scaling and the right format per pass. Get it wrong and a single overflow becomes a NaN that kills the run. That closes Module 9b — you now understand the full precision ladder from fp32 down to fp8.
