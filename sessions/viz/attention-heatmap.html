Attention · Represent

Who listens to whom?

When a transformer reads a sentence, every word looks at every other word and decides how much each one matters. The result is a grid of numbers called an attention matrix. This page lets you feel it: hover any square, or use the arrow keys on the word list, to see who each word is really listening to.

The attention matrix for one sentence

How to read it: each row is one word doing the looking (the query). Each column is a word being looked at (the key). A darker blue square = more attention. Hover a square, or click / arrow-key a word on the left, to light up that word's row.

What to notice

  • Look at the row for ‘it’ — most of its attention points at ‘cat’. That is how the model figures out what the word “it” refers to. This is the whole magic of attention in one square.
  • Every row lights up a Σ = 1.00 badge. The attention a word spends is a fixed 100% budget spread across all words — it can shift where it looks, but the total is always one.
  • The strongest square in each row gets a ring. That is the word this word listens to most.
One trap to avoid
The matrix is not symmetric. “it → cat” being strong does not mean “cat → it” is strong. Row i, column j asks “how much does word i look at word j?” — swapping them is a different question. Read rows, not diagonals.
Quick check (3 questions)
1. In this grid, what does a single row represent?
One word's attention over all words — how much that query word looks at every other word. The row always sums to 1.
2. The row for “it” is darkest at the “cat” column. Why does that matter?
It shows the model has learned that “it” refers to “cat”. Attention is how a transformer resolves references and links related words.
3. If “it → cat” is 0.7, is “cat → it” also 0.7?
Not necessarily. The matrix is not symmetric — each row is computed from its own query, so the two directions are independent.
