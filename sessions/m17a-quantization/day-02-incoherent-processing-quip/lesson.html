Quantization · Week 15 · Day 2

Incoherent Processing(QuIP)

Squeezing an LLM weight down to 2 bits sounds impossible — a menu of just 4 numbers per weight. The trick that makes it work: before you round, you spin the weight matrix with a rotation that hides the giant outliers. Today: what "incoherence" means and how rotating first rescues 2-bit rounding.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain why outlier weights break low-bit quantization, define an incoherent matrix, describe how an orthogonal rotation (random or Hadamard) smooths outliers losslessly, and say why the Hadamard version is the one modern methods use.
1What is quantization, and what does "incoherent" mean?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you know a matrix is just a grid of numbers, and that a model's weights are millions of such numbers. No prior quantization knowledge needed. We start from zero.

First: what is quantization?

An LLM stores each weight as a 16-bit floating-point number. A "bit" is one 0-or-1 digit, so 16 bits per weight is a lot of memory across billions of weights. Quantization means: replace each weight with a value picked from a small menu of allowed numbers, then store only which menu item you chose.

If the menu has 4 items, you need only 2 bits per weight — 2 bits can label 4 things (00, 01, 10, 11). That is 8× less memory than 16 bits. The cost is error: the stored value is not exactly the original, and if the error is big enough it wrecks the model.

Why is 2-bit so hard? Outliers.

Real weight matrices have outliers: most weights are tiny (like 0.01) but a few are huge (like 4.5). A 4-item menu now has to stretch from −4.5 to +4.5, so the gaps between menu items are wide (say 3 apart). Every tiny weight then snaps to the wrong place with a big error. One giant number ruins the grid for everyone.

The new word: incoherent

A matrix is incoherent when its energy is spread out evenly — no single entry is a spike sticking far above the rest, like a well-shuffled deck. Coherent (the bad case) means the energy is concentrated in a few entries or directions. Formally, a matrix is μ-incoherent if its largest entry is small relative to its overall size, so a lower μ means smoother.

Incoherence processing: rotate, then round

Incoherence processing is the operation that makes a matrix incoherent before you quantize it. Take an orthogonal matrix Q — a square matrix whose rows are perpendicular unit vectors, so it only rotates/reflects space and never stretches it (Q · Qᵀ = I, the identity). Multiply your weight matrix W by one orthogonal matrix on the left and another on the right: W' = U · W · V. Rotation preserves length, so no information is lost, but it smears each big outlier across many entries. The outliers vanish into the crowd, the numbers become even in size, and a tight 4-item menu now fits them. At inference you undo the rotation by multiplying back — and that folds cheaply into the surrounding matrix multiplies, so it is nearly free.

📦
Two flavors: QuIP uses a random orthogonal matrix (great theory, but slow and memory-heavy — a dense n×n matrix). QuIP# / QTIP use the Hadamard transform — a structured orthogonal matrix of only +1/−1 entries that rotates just as well but runs in n·log n time with almost no extra memory. That speed is why the Hadamard version won.
📚
Official papers (optional): QuIP (arXiv:2307.13304) · QuIP# (arXiv:2402.04396) · QTIP (arXiv:2406.11235)
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: "旋转矩阵怎么可能帮上量化的忙" 最反直觉——它不改变信息，却把 outlier 摊平了。In plain terms: people expect quantization to be about the rounding, so a rotation seems irrelevant. The insight is that an orthogonal rotation is lossless (you can undo it exactly) yet it smears each spike across many entries, so a coarse 4-level menu suddenly fits — you fix the data's shape, not the grid.
2Packing a suitcase with a reversible blenderIntuition

Imagine packing a suitcase where every compartment must be the same size. Your clothes are the weights. If one giant winter coat sits in the suitcase, you must size every compartment to fit that coat — so all your t-shirts rattle around in oversized compartments and get crushed unevenly. That is coherent, outlier-dominated data, and a cheap coarse suitcase (a 2-bit menu) does badly.

🧥👕👕
Coherent = one giant coat

One 5 kg coat + twenty 0.1 kg shirts. Every compartment must be coat-sized, so the shirts are lost in space. Coarse packing wastes almost everything.

🌀🧺
Incoherence = a reversible blend

Run everything through a blender that redistributes the fabric evenly: now twenty-one items each ≈ 0.3 kg. Every compartment is the same modest size, everything packs snugly, and a cheap coarse suitcase suddenly works.

In one line: spread the outlier's weight evenly across everything (that is the rotation), then a coarse menu fits perfectly — and because the blend is a rotation, you can un-blend to recover the coat and shirts exactly.

直觉 / Intuition: 把一件大衣和一堆 T 恤丢进一个"可逆搅拌机"，出来每件都差不多重——现在小箱子都装得下，事后还能原样还原。In systems terms: the "blender" is an orthogonal matrix (Q·Qᵀ=I), so it only rotates space; the un-rotate is its transpose, which fuses into the neighboring matmul, so it's nearly free at inference.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real blender destroys information — you could never rebuild the coat. An orthogonal rotation is perfectly invertible (multiply by the transpose and the original comes back). Also, the "blend" is a precise linear rotation, not random mush; the Hadamard version is a very specific +1/−1 pattern, not chaos.

4The rules of incoherence processing, and why it mattersHow & why

The rules

  • Rotate first, then round. Sandwich the weight matrix between two orthogonal matrices: W' = U · W · V. Because U and V are orthogonal (Q·Qᵀ = I), the rotation preserves length and loses nothing.
  • Smoothing is the point. The rotation smears every big outlier across many entries, so the entries of W' become even in size. The incoherence score μ (largest entry ÷ overall size) drops.
  • Now a coarse grid fits. With no spike to reach, the 2-bit menu can pack its 4 levels tightly around the smooth numbers — so rounding error is small.
  • Undo it at inference. Multiply the stored quantized weights by the transposes Uᵀ and Vᵀ to return to weight space. These are matrix multiplies, so they fuse into the neighboring layer matmuls — the runtime cost is tiny.
✅
W' = U · W · V  →  quantize W' to 2 bits  →  at inference undo with Uᵀ (·) Vᵀ
"Spin the outliers away, round the smooth matrix cheaply, then spin back — and the spin-back folds into matmuls you were already doing."
🪜
Math Ladder — the incoherence score μ
1 · In words: μ measures how "spiky" a matrix is — how big its largest entry is relative to its typical entry; smaller μ means flatter, easier to quantize.
2 · The formula: μ ≈ max|Wᵢⱼ| / RMS(W), where RMS(W) = √(mean(W²)) is the typical entry size. A rotation W' = U·W·V keeps the total energy (Frobenius norm) fixed but lowers max|·|, so μ drops.
3 · Tiny numbers: row [0.02,−0.05,0.10,−0.03,4.50,0.01] → RMS ≈ 1.84, max = 4.50 → μ ≈ 2.4 (spiky). After a Hadamard rotation the entries even out near ±0.7 → μ ≈ 1.0 (flat).
4 · Sanity check: the Frobenius norm must be identical before and after (rotation is lossless), so if μ drops but the norm changed, your transform isn't orthonormal; and a lower μ should translate to a lower 2-bit reconstruction error.

Why the Hadamard version won

  • Random Q is correct but heavy. A dense n×n random orthogonal matrix costs O(n²) time and memory — too much for real LLM layers.
  • Hadamard is structured. The Hadamard transform is an orthogonal matrix built only from +1/−1 entries. A fast recursive algorithm (like the FFT) applies it in O(n·log n) time with almost no extra memory, giving the same smoothing.
  • It sets up the next ideas. Once the weights are incoherent, smarter codebooks work: E8 lattice codebooks (QuIP#) and trellis codes (QTIP) only pack well on smooth, even data.
🔗
Causal chain: outliers force a coarse grid → rotate with an orthogonal matrix (lossless) → outliers smear away, matrix becomes incoherent → tight 2-bit grid fits → un-rotate at inference (nearly free) → usable 2-bit models. The full recipe also uses the Hessian (which error directions hurt the output most) to round smartly — but the headline is: rotate first, then round.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The whole method is a rotate-round-unrotate sandwich. It only stays lossless if the un-rotate exactly matches the rotate — and if it doesn't, nothing crashes.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): a rotate/un-rotate mismatch. The stored weights live in rotated space, so at inference you must un-rotate with the exact transpose Uᵀ/Vᵀ (for a randomized Hadamard transform, the exact same sign pattern and permutation seed used to rotate). If the seed drifts, or someone re-generates the sign vector, or the transform is applied on the wrong axis, the shapes still line up and the matmul still runs — it just returns weights rotated into the wrong basis. The model produces fluent, plausible-looking text that is quietly wrong, and perplexity is only mildly off, so it can pass a smoke test. In code review a senior engineer checks that the rotation seed/sign pattern is stored with the checkpoint and reused byte-for-byte at load, and adds a round-trip assertion (rotate then un-rotate an identity block ≈ identity).
⚖️
Trade-off: incoherence buys 2-bit accuracy but adds a calibration and runtime step. Rotating to kill outliers is what lets a 4-level grid work at all — but it means every layer now carries an extra transform on both weights and activations, plus a Hessian estimate from calibration data to round smartly. Even the fast Hadamard transform is not free, and it must fuse cleanly into the neighboring matmuls or it eats the memory-bandwidth win it was meant to protect. In a design review the question is whether the extra transform and calibration complexity are justified: at 4 bits, simpler outlier-tolerant schemes may match it; the rotation earns its keep mainly in the extreme 2–3 bit regime.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Two-bit quantization dies on outliers because one spike stretches a 4-level grid until every small weight collapses. QuIP's fix is incoherence processing: sandwich the weight matrix between orthogonal matrices W' = U·W·V — lossless because Q·Qᵀ=I — which smears the outliers so μ (max entry / RMS) drops and a tight grid fits, then un-rotate at inference by folding the transpose into neighboring matmuls. QuIP# uses the Hadamard transform (±1 entries, O(n log n)) for the same smoothing almost for free. The silent failure is a rotate/un-rotate seed mismatch — the model runs and sounds fluent but is quietly in the wrong basis — so I store the sign/seed with the checkpoint and assert a round-trip identity."

Gotcha: both the weights AND the Hessian must be made incoherent, and the rotation must be UNDONE at inference. Also: the fast Hadamard transform needs a power-of-two dimension — for real LLM layers you must pad or use a block-diagonal / Kronecker Hadamard, or the transform is undefined. Incoherence smooths outliers, but it does not make quantization error-free; it just makes the coarse grid fit well.
