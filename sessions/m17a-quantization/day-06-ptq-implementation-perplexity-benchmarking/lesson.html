PTQ Implementation
& Perplexity Benchmarking

This week's lab moves from hand-written kernels to extreme optimization via numeric compression. You'll take a fully-trained model and shrink its weights from FP16 down to INT8, using bitsandbytes' LLM.int8() — with no retraining, just a one-time numeric conversion. Then you'll measure exactly what that shrink costs you, using perplexity.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why naive int8 quantization breaks on large transformers, explain how LLM.int8()'s mixed-precision decomposition fixes it, compute the real memory savings of FP16 → INT8, and build a perplexity benchmark that quantifies the "quantization tax."
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is PTQ, and what is LLM.int8()?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know a model's weights are just numbers stored in memory, and you've seen the idea of "hand-written kernels" earlier this week. No prior quantization experience needed.

The problem: a trained model is huge, and you can't retrain it every time

A model finishes training. Its weights sit in memory as FP16 numbers — "16-bit floating point," 2 bytes per number. A 7-billion-parameter model needs about 14 GB just to hold its weights. That is a lot of expensive GPU memory, and it makes the model slower to run too.

You want to shrink those numbers down to INT8 — "8-bit integers," 1 byte per number — to cut memory roughly in half. But the model already finished training. You do not want to retrain it from scratch just to save memory.

The answer: Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)

Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) means: take a model that is already fully trained, and convert its weights to a lower-precision format — no retraining, no gradient updates, just a one-time numeric conversion — right before or during inference. This is different from Quantization-Aware Training (QAT), where the model sees quantization noise during training and learns to compensate for it. PTQ is cheap (minutes, not GPU-days) but riskier, because the model never got a chance to adapt. That is exactly why today's lab ends with a benchmark: you need to measure the risk, not just assume it is small.

The tool: bitsandbytes and LLM.int8()

bitsandbytes is an open-source library that patches PyTorch's nn.Linear layers with custom CUDA kernels for 8-bit and 4-bit matrix multiplication. You do not write your own quantization math — you load a Hugging Face model with load_in_8bit=True and bitsandbytes intercepts every linear layer's weights and forward pass.

LLM.int8() is the specific quantization algorithm bitsandbytes uses for int8. It exists to solve one specific failure: naive int8 quantization (just mapping the min/max weight range onto 256 int8 buckets) collapses accuracy on transformers larger than about 6.7B parameters. The reason is emergent outlier features — a small number of activation dimensions that develop huge values (sometimes 20x the normal size) as models scale. LLM.int8()'s fix is mixed-precision decomposition: detect the outlier columns, route just those through a normal FP16 matmul, and route everything else through int8 matmul with its own per-row/column scale factors. We will build the full intuition for this in Section 2 and the exact math in Section 4.

The measurement: perplexity

Perplexity (PPL) is the number you use today to answer "how much did quantization hurt the model?" It measures how confused the model is when predicting the next word in real text. Lower is better. You compute it once for the FP16 model and once for the INT8 model on the exact same text, and the gap between the two is your "quantization tax."

📚
Official docs (optional): bitsandbytes on GitHub · LLM.int8() paper (Dettmers et al., 2022) · EleutherAI's LM Evaluation Harness
2 · INTUITIONA cheap truck and one careful van✓

Think about shipping thousands of letters and a handful of fragile glass packages across the country.

📦🚚
The cheap truck = int8 matmul

Almost every letter is ordinary. Stack them tight, ship them on the slow-but-cheap truck (int8), and you save huge amounts of space and fuel. Fine for 99.9% of the load.

🍷🚐
The careful van = FP16 outlier path

A few boxes are fragile glass — squeeze them into the cheap truck's tight int8 packing and they shatter. LLM.int8() spots those specific boxes ahead of time and drives them separately, in full-precision FP16, on their own van.

In one line: LLM.int8() does not quantize everything the same way — it detects the small set of "fragile" outlier feature dimensions ahead of time, routes just those through FP16, and lets everything else travel cheaply as int8.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real trucks carry separate physical boxes. Here, the "cheap truck" and "careful van" results are two partial matrix products that get added back together into one output — the model never sees two separate deliveries, just one combined answer.

4 · HOW & WHYThe math of LLM.int8(), and why it matters✓

The rules

  • Vector-wise absmax quantization: for each row of the activation matrix X and each column of the weight matrix W, find the largest absolute value (absmax), and scale by 127 / absmax to map every number into the int8 range [-127, 127].
  • The int8 matmul runs on the scaled integers. To recover real values, dequantize by dividing the int32 accumulator output by the product of the row-scale and column-scale used going in.
  • Outlier detection: before quantizing, scan X for feature columns where any value's magnitude exceeds a threshold (default 6.0). These are the emergent outlier features — a small, consistent set of dimensions, often under 0.1% of columns, that carry disproportionately large values once a model is large enough (empirically, models around 6.7B+ parameters).
  • Mixed-precision decomposition: split the matmul into two parts. Outlier columns of X and their matching rows of W multiply in ordinary FP16. All remaining columns multiply via the int8 path above. Add the two partial results together for the final output.
✅
Y ≈ X_fp16_outliers @ W_fp16_outliers + dequant(X_int8_rest @ W_int8_rest)
"Add the small full-precision outlier product to the large dequantized int8 product." This is why LLM.int8() keeps near-FP16 accuracy while storing almost all weights in 1 byte instead of 2.
⚠️
Failure mode without outlier handling: if you quantize everything naively (map the full min/max range onto 256 int8 buckets with no outlier split), the huge outlier values stretch the scale factor so far that ordinary, non-outlier weights all collapse into just a few buckets. On models ≥6.7B parameters this measurably degrades perplexity and can break the model outright — this is the exact failure the mixed-precision path exists to prevent.

Why it's a superpower

  • Near-zero accuracy cost — because the handful of outlier dimensions that matter most stay in FP16, the int8 path only has to be accurate on the "easy" majority of values.
  • Roughly half the memory — 1 byte per weight instead of 2, cutting a 7B model's weight footprint from about 14 GB to about 7 GB.
  • No retraining needed — like all PTQ, this is a one-time conversion at load time, not a training run.
🔗
Causal chain: models scale past ~6.7B params → outlier features emerge in a few activation dimensions → naive int8 quantization crushes them → LLM.int8() detects and routes outliers to FP16, quantizes the rest to int8 → memory drops, accuracy barely moves → perplexity benchmarking (next) is how you verify that last claim instead of assuming it.

7 · PRODUCEQuantize a 7B model and measure the real cost✓

Today's task (from the curriculum) has two parts — a Saturday half and a Sunday half.

Saturday half — memory footprint

experiments/week15_ptq/quantize_and_measure.py: a script that (a) loads a 7B-parameter Hugging Face causal LM in FP16, (b) reloads it in INT8 via load_in_8bit=True (bitsandbytes), (c) measures the actual GPU memory footprint for both (via torch.cuda.memory_allocated() or summed numel() * element_size()), and (d) writes experiments/week15_ptq/results/memory_footprint.json with keys fp16_bytes, int8_bytes, reduction_pct.

Acceptance criteria — Saturday

  • Script runs end-to-end on a CUDA GPU without manual intervention.
  • reduction_pct is present and falls between 40-55% (accounting for the small FP16 outlier-path overhead).
  • Both raw byte counts are logged, not just the percentage.

Sunday half — perplexity benchmark

experiments/week15_ptq/perplexity_benchmark.py: extends the Saturday script (or runs standalone against saved checkpoints) to compute perplexity for both FP16 and INT8 models on an identical fixed held-out text sample (e.g. a WikiText-2 slice, tokenized once and reused for both), writing experiments/week15_ptq/results/perplexity_comparison.json with keys ppl_fp16, ppl_int8, pct_degradation, plus a short markdown note experiments/week15_ptq/results/degradation_curve.md documenting the observed degradation and whether it grows with sequence length (the "mathematical degradation curve" the curriculum names explicitly).

Acceptance criteria — Sunday

  • Both PPL values are finite and ppl_int8 ≥ ppl_fp16 (int8 should never beat the FP16 baseline).
  • pct_degradation is computed and logged.
  • The markdown note states in one sentence whether the observed degradation is "acceptable" (single-digit % increase) or a red flag (multiples of baseline).
