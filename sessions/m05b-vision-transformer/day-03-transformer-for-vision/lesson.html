Module 05b · Represent · Day 3

A Transformer for VisionThe Same Machine, Now on Tiles

Think about your favorite cookie recipe written on a card. Once you have that one card, you can bake cookies, or brownies, or bread — you follow the same set of steps, you just swap what you pour into the bowl. Here's the surprise of today: the Transformer, the exact machine that reads text and powers tools like ChatGPT, is that recipe card. Yesterday we turned a picture into a line of little tile-passports. Today we discover we don't need to invent a new machine to read them — we pour the tiles into the same Transformer recipe that reads words, and it just works. A picture goes in as a sentence of tiles; understanding comes out. That single idea — "one recipe, feed it anything you can turn into a sequence" — is the whole reason a Vision Transformer exists.

🎯
Together we'll follow the recipe that turns a line of tiles into a decision. You'll see that the Transformer block is reused unchanged from text, watch how attention lets every tile look at every other tile from the very first step (something a picture model never used to do), meet the older CNN this replaces and why we moved, read the final answer off one special token, and then meet — as friendly puzzles — the things this recipe struggles with and the exact fix waiting for each. Every new word gets explained the moment it appears.
1Feed the picture to the very same machineOne recipe for everything

Let's start with the most surprising fact of the whole day, so you know where we're going. Yesterday you turned a picture into a tidy line of tile-passports — an ordered list of vectors, one per tile. That kind of list has a name you already know: a sequence. And here's the punchline: a Transformer is a machine that eats a sequence and understands it. It does not know, and does not care, whether that sequence came from a sentence of words or a picture of tiles. So we don't build a new machine for vision — we pour our tiles into the very same one.

Think about that cookie recipe card again. The card says: "put the ingredients in the bowl, mix, bake, take out." It never says what the ingredients are. Pour in flour and sugar, you get cookies. Pour in something savory, you get a savory bake. The steps don't change — only what goes in the bowl. The Transformer is that recipe card. The steps are fixed; today we simply pour in tiles instead of words.

What the recipe-card picture gets right: one fixed set of steps handles many different fillings — you swap the ingredients, not the steps. That is exactly what the Transformer does: same machine, tiles instead of words. Where it breaks down: a real recipe card is frozen — it can't learn — but the Transformer's steps contain numbers that adjust during training, so it slowly gets better at whatever you keep pouring in.

The name of the whole thing: Vision Transformer

When we take the text Transformer recipe and pour picture-tiles into it, the result has a name: the Vision Transformer, written ViT for short. That's the entire big idea in one sentence: ViT = the text Transformer, unchanged, reading a sentence of tiles. Everything else today is us walking slowly through that same recipe, one step at a time, and pointing out what's exciting about running it on a picture. Keep the recipe card in your mind — we'll follow it all the way to an answer.

2One Transformer block — and we stack itThe block you reuse

So what is one step of the recipe? The recipe is built from one small repeated piece called a block, and we simply stack many identical copies of it in a row. The line of tiles walks through block 1, then block 2, then block 3, and so on — each block a little smarter about the picture than the last.

Think of a relay race. One runner takes the baton, runs their leg, and hands it to the next runner, who does the same kind of leg, and so on down the line. Every runner does the same job; the baton just keeps getting carried further along. Our line of tiles is the baton. Each block is one runner doing the same fixed job — and after enough legs, the baton (the tiles) has been carried all the way to understanding.

What the relay-race picture gets right: the same leg repeated by many runners, carrying one baton further and further — that's a stack of identical blocks refining the same line of tiles. Where it breaks down: relay runners are truly identical, but each Transformer block has its own learned numbers, so block 2 has learned to do something a little different with the tiles than block 1 did.

Inside one block: two jobs, each with a shortcut

Now let's peek inside one runner. A single Transformer block does just two jobs, in order. First, self-attention — every tile looks at all the other tiles and gathers what's useful (the "let the tiles talk" step we promised yesterday). Second, a feed-forward layer — a tiny bit of extra thinking applied to each tile on its own. That's the whole block: look around, then think.

Two small helpers wrap each job so the recipe stays stable as it gets tall. Before each job we tidy the numbers with a layer norm (keep them in a healthy range), and around each job we add a shortcut — a skip path that adds the input back to the output so nothing important gets lost on the way through. You don't need the inner math today; the two helpers exist so we can stack lots of blocks without the signal fading.

Here's the beautiful part, and the reason today matters: not one piece of that block was changed for vision. The layer norm, the attention, the feed-forward, the shortcuts — every part is copied straight from the text Transformer. We forward-point the inner details on purpose (attention gets its own days; the feed-forward, norm, and shortcut live in the transformer module). The takeaway you need today is bigger than any one part: the whole recipe is reused unchanged. A picture just walks the same relay a sentence would.

3From step one, each tile can look at the whole pictureEvery tile sees every tile

Now we reach the single most exciting thing about running the recipe on tiles — the "wow" of the whole day. In that first attention job, every tile can look at every other tile, all at once, from the very first block. A tile in the top-left corner can compare notes with a tile in the bottom-right corner immediately. Nothing has to travel step by step across the picture first. This ability — "each tile can reach the whole picture" — is called a global receptive field.

Picture a round dinner table where everyone can see everyone else's face. If you ask a question, any guest can answer any other guest directly — no whispering it down a chain. Now compare that to a game of telephone, where a message can only pass to the person right next to you, so news from the far end takes many hops to arrive. A CNN — the older picture model — is telephone: each spot starts by seeing only its tiny neighborhood, and word from across the picture takes many layers to reach it. A Transformer is the dinner table: from the first block, every tile talks to every tile.

What the dinner-table picture gets right: every guest sees every other guest, so any two can talk directly with no relay — exactly how attention lets any two tiles interact in one block. Where it breaks down: at a real table you turn your head to look at one person at a time; a tile "looks at" all the other tiles in one shot, weighing each one by how useful it is.

Build-up: watch the two grow differently

Let's make the difference concrete, because it's the heart of why people got excited about ViT. Ask a simple question: starting from one spot, how many other spots can it "see" after each layer? Predict first — will the Transformer or the CNN reach the whole picture sooner? Then reveal.

how many spots can ONE spot see, after 1 layer / 2 layers? (16-tile-wide picture)
CNN (small 3-wide window):  layer 1 → sees 3 spots   ·  layer 2 → sees 5 spots   · … many layers to reach all 16
ViT attention:              layer 1 → sees ALL 16     ·  layer 2 → still all 16
The Transformer sees the whole picture in ONE layer; the CNN grows its view slowly, a little wider each layer. That's the global receptive field: a top-left tile can relate to a bottom-right tile in the very first block, instead of waiting for the view to crawl across.

That's the property to remember: a Vision Transformer has the whole picture in view from block one. A face and the shadow it casts, an object and its background — the model can relate far-apart parts of the image right away. This is the gift the recipe gives vision, and it's exactly what the older telephone-style model couldn't do easily. Which raises a fair question — what was that older model, and why did we move on? That's next.

4The CNN it grew out of — and why we movedThe model it replaces

For years, the go-to picture model was the CNN (Convolutional Neural Network). It works by sliding a small window over the image, looking at little neighborhoods, the way we described the "telephone chain." A CNN comes with two rules built into its wiring from the start — and understanding those two rules is the key to why we moved to ViT.

Think of two kinds of helper in a kitchen. The CNN is a cook who was handed a strict rulebook: "only look at ingredients right next to each other, and treat every corner of the counter the same way." Those rules are genuinely helpful — pictures really are mostly local, and a cat is a cat wherever it sits — so the cook gets going fast with little training. The ViT is a cook given no rulebook at all, just a huge pile of example dishes: it has to figure out the useful habits on its own, which takes more examples, but it isn't boxed in by rules that might not always fit.

What the two-cooks picture gets right: rules-first gets going fast but is boxed in; examples-first is slower to start but freer to find its own patterns — that's exactly CNN-vs-ViT. Where it breaks down: a cook can choose to break their rulebook; a CNN literally can't — the two rules are wired into its math and it always follows them.

The two built-in rules have names

Those two kitchen rules have proper names, and they're worth knowing. Rule 1 — "only look nearby" — is called locality. Rule 2 — "a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears" — is called translation-equivariance. Together these are the CNN's inductive bias: helpful assumptions baked in before it sees any data. A ViT throws both rules away and learns spatial structure from the data instead. That's the whole "why we moved": with enough examples, a model that learns its own habits can beat one stuck with fixed rules — because sometimes far-apart parts of a picture do matter, and a rulebook that only looks nearby can miss them.

Why tiles, not raw pixels? (the reason patches exist)

There's one more thing the CNN story quietly explains: why we chopped the picture into tiles in the first place. Remember, attention lets every token look at every other token — that means it compares every pair. If each token were a single pixel, a small 224 × 224 picture would have about 50,000 pixels, and comparing every pair is 50,000 × 50,000 — billions of comparisons. Far too many. Tiles rescue us: group the pixels into 16 × 16 tiles and you have only ~200 tokens, so pair-comparisons drop from billions to tens of thousands.

So the tiles from Day 1 weren't a random choice — they're what makes feeding a picture to a Transformer possible at all. Now you can see the whole shape of the recipe: chop into tiles (to keep the count small), embed each tile (Day 2's passports), then run the reused Transformer relay (today) so every tile sees every other. One question remains — after all those blocks, where do we read the answer?

5Read the whole-picture answer off one tokenReading the answer

The tiles have walked the whole relay — every block let them look at each other and think a bit more. Out the other end comes a whole line of tokens again, one per tile, plus that special leader token we added yesterday. But we usually want just one answer: "what is this a picture of?" So which token do we read? Here's the elegant trick of the recipe: we read the answer off the one special leader token, the [CLS] token we pinned to the front of the line.

Remember the team captain from yesterday? The captain wears the armband, isn't a normal player, and their whole job is to listen to every teammate. Because of the dinner-table view, over all those blocks the [CLS] token got to look at every single tile and gather what mattered. So when the coach asks "what's the team's answer?", they don't poll all 196 players — they just ask the captain, who has quietly collected the summary of the whole picture into one vector.

What the team-captain picture gets right: one leader gathers from everyone and speaks for the group, so you ask them, not all the players — exactly how one [CLS] vector summarizes the picture. Where it breaks down: a captain has opinions of their own, but the [CLS] token started blank and holds only what it gathered from the tiles through attention — it has nothing to say on its own.

The last little step: a classification head

The [CLS] token's final vector is a length-D summary of the whole image — but it's still just numbers, not a label like "cat." So we bolt one tiny piece onto the end: a classification head. In plain words, it's a small final layer that takes the summary vector and turns it into a score for each possible answer — highest score wins. It's only attached to the [CLS] token; the other tile-outputs aren't used for the final label.

The main-flow rule is one gentle line: prediction = classification head applied to the [CLS] token's output vector. How that head is trained — the scores, the "surprise" it's punished for, the softmax that turns scores into a choice — is a story for the loss day (we forward-point it on purpose). Today the win is that you can trace the whole path with your finger: tiles → passports → the reused Transformer relay → read the captain → a label. That's a complete Vision Transformer, end to end. Take the victory lap — you just followed the entire recipe.

6Puzzle: why a plain ViT needs so much dataThe hungry puzzle

Here's an honest puzzle, and it's a fun one because the answer is already sitting in what you just learned. If you train a plain ViT on a small pile of pictures, it often loses to a same-size CNN. Wait — the dinner-table model, that sees the whole picture at once, loses? Yes, and the reason is exactly the freedom we celebrated. Remember the rulebook.

Think of two students learning to cook. The CNN student was handed a good rulebook ("look nearby, treat every spot the same"), so even with only a few practice dishes they cook something decent — the rulebook did a lot of the work. The ViT student got no rulebook, only examples. With just a few examples they flounder, because they haven't figured out any good habits yet. But give them a huge pile of examples and they surpass the rulebook student — because they learned habits tuned to the real world instead of relying on rules that don't always fit. The cause of ViT's data-hunger is precisely its missing rulebook: no built-in inductive bias, so it must learn everything from examples.

What the two-students picture gets right: rules help a lot when examples are scarce, but with enough examples, learning-your-own-habits wins — that's exactly why ViT trails on small data and pulls ahead on big data. Where it breaks down: a student can be told the rulebook to catch up quickly; you can't just hand a plain ViT the rules — it has to earn them from data (though clever later models bring some rules back, a later day).

The remedy has a name: pretrain big, then fine-tune

So how do we get a ViT the data it craves? Not by collecting a giant labeled set for your small task — that's usually impossible. Instead we use a two-step recipe that's now standard. Step 1 — pretraining: train the ViT once on a huge general image set (famous ones are ImageNet-21k and JFT-300M, with millions of pictures) so it learns broad visual habits. Step 2 — fine-tuning: take that pretrained ViT and train it a little more on your small task. The big pretraining feeds the hunger; the fine-tuning aims it at your problem.

That's the fix for the hungry puzzle: large-scale pretraining, then fine-tune. It's why the famous ViT results came from models pretrained on enormous datasets — with that much data, dropping the CNN's rulebook stops being a weakness and becomes a strength. (There are even clever tricks to train a ViT without the giant dataset — one is called DeiT — but those are a later day.)

7What this recipe still can't do — and the fixesHonest limits

Being honest about limits is what makes an engineer trustworthy — and every limit here comes with a satisfying fix already waiting. Picture the recipe as a first, plain version of a dish: it's genuinely good, but there are a few known rough edges, and a seasoned cook knows the exact tweak for each. Let's meet the three biggest ones as little puzzles, each with its answer.

What the first-version-of-a-dish picture gets right: the recipe already works and tastes good; the rough edges are known and each has a standard tweak — you don't throw the dish out. Where it breaks down: a cook fixes a dish by taste; these fixes are specific engineering techniques with names, which we point to below.

Limit 1 · Cost grows with the square of the token count

The dinner-table gift has a price. Because every tile talks to every tile, the work grows with the square of the number of tiles N — double the tiles and the cost roughly quadruples. This is the quadratic cost of attention. It's why very high-resolution images or very tiny patches (both of which mean lots of tiles) get expensive fast — the same "many tiny pizza slivers" trade-off from yesterday. The known softening: later models use windowed / hierarchical attention (each tile talks only to a nearby group at first, then groups merge) so the cost stops exploding. You'll meet the deep form of that (a family called Swin) in a later day; today just know the fix exists.

Limit 2 · A plain ViT only labels the whole picture

The recipe we built reads one answer off the [CLS] token — "this whole picture is a cat." That's image classification. It does not, on its own, tell you where the cat is (a box around it) or trace its exact outline. Those richer tasks — detection (draw boxes) and segmentation (outline every pixel) — need extra task-specific heads bolted on top of the ViT. A plain ViT is a strong reader of the whole picture; turning it into a locator is a separate build (a later topic).

Limit 3 · Position stamps are tied to the training image size

Remember yesterday's fix for the "shuffled tiles" puzzle — we added a learned positional embedding to each tile so the model knows where each one sat. (Without it, the tiles are just an unordered bag — that's the permutation-blindness we cured on Day 2.) But we only learned one stamp per slot, for exactly the grid size the training images had. Feed the model a bigger picture — more tiles, more slots — and there aren't enough learned stamps to go around. The known fix has a name: interpolate the positional embeddings — gently stretch the grid of learned stamps to the new number of slots, like resizing a photo, so a model trained at one resolution can run at another.

8Today in one pageRecap

You did it — you followed the whole recipe that turns a picture into a decision, and you saw that it's the same recipe that reads text. Here's one clean way to hold the day: picture the recipe card with its numbered steps. A line of tiles walks in at the top; a label walks out at the bottom. Each step only makes sense because of the one before it — that's exactly how today stacked up. Where the recipe-card picture breaks down: a real recipe is frozen, but this one learns its numbers during training and keeps getting better at whatever you pour in. Read the steps, then keep the cheat-sheets as your map.

The steps of the recipe, in order:

  • Step 1 · Pour in the tiles. Yesterday's line of tile-passports is a sequence — the exact input a Transformer eats. No new machine needed.
  • Step 2 · Run the reused block, stacked. One Transformer block = self-attention (look around) + a feed-forward layer (think), each wrapped in a layer norm and a shortcut. Stack L identical blocks in a relay. Nothing was changed from the text Transformer.
  • Step 3 · Every tile sees every tile. From block one, attention gives a global receptive field: any tile can relate to any other tile at once — the CNN's telephone chain could only crawl outward layer by layer.
  • Step 4 · Ask the captain. Read the whole-picture summary off the one special [CLS] token, which gathered from every tile.
  • Step 5 · A classification head. A tiny final layer turns the [CLS] summary vector into a score per class → a label.
  • The model it replaces: the CNN, whose built-in rules — locality and translation-equivariance — ViT drops and learns from data instead. That freedom is why we needed tiles (per-pixel tokens would be far too many pairs to compare).
  • And the honest bits: attention's quadratic cost (→ windowed/hierarchical attention), whole-image classification only (→ detection/segmentation heads), position stamps tied to image size (→ interpolate them), and data-hunger from the missing rulebook (→ large-scale pretraining, then fine-tune).
