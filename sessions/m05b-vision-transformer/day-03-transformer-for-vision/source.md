@@@ hero
@lede Think about your favorite cookie **recipe** written on a card. Once you have that one card, you can bake cookies, or brownies, or bread — you follow the *same set of steps*, you just swap what you pour into the bowl. Here's the surprise of today: the Transformer, the exact machine that reads text and powers tools like ChatGPT, is that recipe card. Yesterday we turned a picture into a line of little tile-passports. Today we discover we don't need to invent a *new* machine to read them — we pour the tiles into the **same** Transformer recipe that reads words, and it just works. A picture goes in as a sentence of tiles; understanding comes out. That single idea — "one recipe, feed it anything you can turn into a sequence" — is the whole reason a Vision Transformer exists.
@goal Together we'll follow the recipe that turns a line of tiles into a decision. You'll see that the Transformer block is *reused unchanged* from text, watch how attention lets every tile look at every other tile from the very first step (something a picture model never used to do), meet the older CNN this replaces and why we moved, read the final answer off one special token, and then meet — as friendly puzzles — the things this recipe struggles with and the exact fix waiting for each. Every new word gets explained the moment it appears.

@@@ concept id=c1 tag="One recipe for everything" title="Feed the picture to the very same machine" gotit="Got the big idea"
Let's start with the most surprising fact of the whole day, so you know where we're going. Yesterday you turned a picture into a tidy line of tile-passports — an ordered list of vectors, one per tile. That kind of list has a name you already know: a [[sequence||An ordered list of items, one after another. A Transformer reads a sequence — in text it's word-pieces, here it's tile-passports.]]. And here's the punchline: a [[Transformer||The machine that reads a sequence of vectors and lets each one gather information from the others. It's the model behind tools like ChatGPT — originally built for text.]] is a machine that eats a sequence and understands it. It does not know, and does not care, whether that sequence came from a sentence of words or a picture of tiles. So we don't build a new machine for vision — we pour our tiles into the very same one.

Think about that cookie **recipe** card again. The card says: "put the ingredients in the bowl, mix, bake, take out." It never says *what* the ingredients are. Pour in flour and sugar, you get cookies. Pour in something savory, you get a savory bake. The steps don't change — only what goes in the bowl. The Transformer is that recipe card. The steps are fixed; today we simply pour in tiles instead of words.

**What the recipe-card picture gets right:** one fixed set of steps handles many different fillings — you swap the ingredients, not the steps. That is exactly what the Transformer does: same machine, tiles instead of words. **Where it breaks down:** a real recipe card is frozen — it can't learn — but the Transformer's steps contain numbers that *adjust during training*, so it slowly gets better at whatever you keep pouring in.

#### The name of the whole thing: Vision Transformer
When we take the text Transformer recipe and pour picture-tiles into it, the result has a name: the [[Vision Transformer||A Transformer, unchanged from text, fed a sequence of image-patch tokens instead of word tokens. Written ViT for short.]], written **ViT** for short. That's the entire big idea in one sentence: *ViT = the text Transformer, unchanged, reading a sentence of tiles.* Everything else today is us walking slowly through that same recipe, one step at a time, and pointing out what's exciting about running it on a picture. Keep the recipe card in your mind — we'll follow it all the way to an answer.

@@@ concept id=c2 tag="The block you reuse" title="One Transformer block — and we stack it" gotit="Got the block"
So what *is* one step of the recipe? The recipe is built from one small repeated piece called a [[block||One repeated step of the Transformer recipe. A ViT stacks many identical blocks in a row; the output of one is the input to the next.]], and we simply stack many identical copies of it in a row. The line of tiles walks through block 1, then block 2, then block 3, and so on — each block a little smarter about the picture than the last.

Think of a **relay race**. One runner takes the baton, runs their leg, and hands it to the next runner, who does the *same* kind of leg, and so on down the line. Every runner does the same job; the baton just keeps getting carried further along. Our line of tiles is the baton. Each block is one runner doing the same fixed job — and after enough legs, the baton (the tiles) has been carried all the way to understanding.

**What the relay-race picture gets right:** the same leg repeated by many runners, carrying one baton further and further — that's a stack of identical blocks refining the same line of tiles. **Where it breaks down:** relay runners are truly identical, but each Transformer block has its *own* learned numbers, so block 2 has learned to do something a little different with the tiles than block 1 did.

#### Inside one block: two jobs, each with a shortcut
Now let's peek inside one runner. A single Transformer block does just **two jobs**, in order. First, [[self-attention||The step where every tile looks at every other tile and pulls in what's useful. It's how tiles share information. We meet the full mechanism in the attention module.]] — every tile looks at all the other tiles and gathers what's useful (the "let the tiles talk" step we promised yesterday). Second, a [[feed-forward layer||A small two-step math block applied to each tile on its own, to think a bit more about what it just gathered. Also called an MLP.]] — a tiny bit of extra thinking applied to each tile on its own. That's the whole block: *look around, then think.*

Two small helpers wrap each job so the recipe stays stable as it gets tall. Before each job we tidy the numbers with a [[layer norm||A gentle rescaling step that keeps the numbers in a healthy range so a tall stack of blocks trains smoothly.]] (keep them in a healthy range), and around each job we add a [[shortcut||A "skip" path that adds a step's input back to its output, so information and learning signal never get lost in a deep stack. Also called a residual connection.]] — a skip path that adds the input back to the output so nothing important gets lost on the way through. You don't need the inner math today; the two helpers exist so we can stack *lots* of blocks without the signal fading.

Here's the beautiful part, and the reason today matters: **not one piece of that block was changed for vision.** The layer norm, the attention, the feed-forward, the shortcuts — every part is copied straight from the text Transformer. We forward-point the inner details on purpose (attention gets its own days; the feed-forward, norm, and shortcut live in the transformer module). The takeaway you need today is bigger than any one part: the *whole recipe is reused unchanged*. A picture just walks the same relay a sentence would.

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="Every tile sees every tile" title="From step one, each tile can look at the whole picture" gotit="Got the global view"
Now we reach the single most exciting thing about running the recipe on tiles — the "wow" of the whole day. In that first attention job, **every tile can look at every other tile, all at once, from the very first block.** A tile in the top-left corner can compare notes with a tile in the bottom-right corner *immediately*. Nothing has to travel step by step across the picture first. This ability — "each tile can reach the whole picture" — is called a global [[receptive field||How much of the picture one tile is allowed to look at. "Global" means it can see the whole picture at once, from the very first step.]].

Picture a **round dinner table** where everyone can see everyone else's face. If you ask a question, any guest can answer any other guest directly — no whispering it down a chain. Now compare that to a **game of telephone**, where a message can only pass to the person right next to you, so news from the far end takes many hops to arrive. A CNN — the older picture model — is telephone: each spot starts by seeing only its tiny neighborhood, and word from across the picture takes many layers to reach it. A Transformer is the dinner table: from the first block, every tile talks to every tile.

That's the property to remember: **a Vision Transformer has the whole picture in view from block one.** A face and the shadow it casts, an object and its background — the model can relate far-apart parts of the image right away. This is the gift the recipe gives vision, and it's exactly what the older telephone-style model couldn't do easily. Which raises a fair question — what *was* that older model, and why did we move on? That's next.

@@@ concept id=c4 tag="The model it replaces" title="The CNN it grew out of — and why we moved" gotit="Got the CNN story"
For years, the go-to picture model was the [[CNN||Convolutional Neural Network — the classic image model that slides small learned filters across a picture, looking at little neighborhoods at a time.]] (Convolutional Neural Network). It works by sliding a small window over the image, looking at little neighborhoods, the way we described the "telephone chain." A CNN comes with two rules built into its wiring from the start — and understanding those two rules is the key to *why* we moved to ViT.

Think of two kinds of helper in a kitchen. The **CNN is a cook who was handed a strict rulebook**: "only look at ingredients right next to each other, and treat every corner of the counter the same way." Those rules are genuinely helpful — pictures really are mostly local, and a cat is a cat wherever it sits — so the cook gets going fast with little training. The **ViT is a cook given no rulebook at all, just a huge pile of example dishes**: it has to *figure out* the useful habits on its own, which takes more examples, but it isn't boxed in by rules that might not always fit.

**What the two-cooks picture gets right:** rules-first gets going fast but is boxed in; examples-first is slower to start but freer to find its own patterns — that's exactly CNN-vs-ViT. **Where it breaks down:** a cook can *choose* to break their rulebook; a CNN literally can't — the two rules are wired into its math and it always follows them.

#### The two built-in rules have names
Those two kitchen rules have proper names, and they're worth knowing. Rule 1 — "only look nearby" — is called [[locality||The built-in assumption that nearby pixels matter most; a CNN only looks at small neighborhoods at a time.]]. Rule 2 — "a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears" — is called [[translation-equivariance||The built-in assumption that a pattern (say, an eye) means the same thing no matter where in the picture it sits.]]. Together these are the CNN's *inductive bias*: helpful assumptions baked in before it sees any data. A ViT throws both rules away and **learns spatial structure from the data instead**. That's the whole "why we moved": with enough examples, a model that learns its own habits can beat one stuck with fixed rules — because sometimes far-apart parts of a picture *do* matter, and a rulebook that only looks nearby can miss them.

#### Why tiles, not raw pixels? (the reason patches exist)
There's one more thing the CNN story quietly explains: *why we chopped the picture into tiles in the first place.* Remember, attention lets every token look at every other token — that means it compares **every pair**. If each token were a single pixel, a small `224 × 224` picture would have about **50,000 pixels**, and comparing every pair is 50,000 × 50,000 — billions of comparisons. Far too many. Tiles rescue us: group the pixels into `16 × 16` tiles and you have only ~200 tokens, so pair-comparisons drop from billions to tens of thousands.

So the tiles from Day 1 weren't a random choice — they're what makes feeding a picture to a Transformer *possible at all*. Now you can see the whole shape of the recipe: chop into tiles (to keep the count small), embed each tile (Day 2's passports), then run the reused Transformer relay (today) so every tile sees every other. One question remains — after all those blocks, **where do we read the answer?**

@@@ concept id=c5 tag="Reading the answer" title="Read the whole-picture answer off one token" gotit="Got the readout"
The tiles have walked the whole relay — every block let them look at each other and think a bit more. Out the other end comes a whole line of tokens again, one per tile, plus that special leader token we added yesterday. But we usually want just *one* answer: "what is this a picture of?" So which token do we read? Here's the elegant trick of the recipe: we read the answer off the **one special leader token**, the `[CLS]` token we pinned to the front of the line.

Remember the **team captain** from yesterday? The captain wears the armband, isn't a normal player, and their whole job is to *listen to every teammate*. Because of the dinner-table view, over all those blocks the `[CLS]` token got to look at every single tile and gather what mattered. So when the coach asks "what's the team's answer?", they don't poll all 196 players — they just ask the captain, who has quietly collected the summary of the whole picture into one vector.

**What the team-captain picture gets right:** one leader gathers from everyone and speaks for the group, so you ask *them*, not all the players — exactly how one `[CLS]` vector summarizes the picture. **Where it breaks down:** a captain has opinions of their own, but the `[CLS]` token started blank and holds *only* what it gathered from the tiles through attention — it has nothing to say on its own.

#### The last little step: a classification head
The `[CLS]` token's final vector is a length-`D` summary of the whole image — but it's still just numbers, not a label like "cat." So we bolt one tiny piece onto the end: a [[classification head||A small extra layer on top of the [CLS] token's output that turns its summary vector into a score for each possible class.]]. In plain words, it's a small final layer that takes the summary vector and turns it into a score for each possible answer — highest score wins. It's *only* attached to the `[CLS]` token; the other tile-outputs aren't used for the final label.

The main-flow rule is one gentle line: **prediction = classification head applied to the `[CLS]` token's output vector.** How that head is trained — the scores, the "surprise" it's punished for, the softmax that turns scores into a choice — is a story for the loss day (we forward-point it on purpose). Today the win is that you can trace the *whole* path with your finger: **tiles → passports → the reused Transformer relay → read the captain → a label.** That's a complete Vision Transformer, end to end. Take the victory lap — you just followed the entire recipe.

@@@ concept id=c6 tag="The hungry puzzle" title="Puzzle: why a plain ViT needs so much data" gotit="Got data-hunger"
Here's an honest puzzle, and it's a fun one because the answer is already sitting in what you just learned. If you train a plain ViT on a *small* pile of pictures, it often **loses** to a same-size CNN. Wait — the dinner-table model, that sees the whole picture at once, loses? Yes, and the reason is exactly the freedom we celebrated. Remember the rulebook.

Think of two students learning to cook. The **CNN student was handed a good rulebook** ("look nearby, treat every spot the same"), so even with only a few practice dishes they cook something decent — the rulebook did a lot of the work. The **ViT student got no rulebook**, only examples. With just a few examples they flounder, because they haven't figured out *any* good habits yet. But give them a *huge* pile of examples and they surpass the rulebook student — because they learned habits tuned to the real world instead of relying on rules that don't always fit. The cause of ViT's data-hunger is precisely its missing rulebook: no built-in [[inductive bias||Helpful assumptions baked into a model before training. A CNN has locality and translation-equivariance; a plain ViT has almost none, so it must learn structure from data.]], so it must learn everything from examples.

#### The remedy has a name: pretrain big, then fine-tune
So how do we get a ViT the data it craves? Not by collecting a giant labeled set for *your* small task — that's usually impossible. Instead we use a two-step recipe that's now standard. **Step 1 — [[pretraining||Training a model first on a very large general dataset so it learns broad structure, before you adapt it to your specific task.]]:** train the ViT once on a *huge* general image set (famous ones are ImageNet-21k and JFT-300M, with millions of pictures) so it learns broad visual habits. **Step 2 — [[fine-tuning||Taking a pretrained model and training it a little more on your own smaller dataset to specialize it for your task.]]:** take that pretrained ViT and train it a little more on *your* small task. The big pretraining feeds the hunger; the fine-tuning aims it at your problem.

**What the first-version-of-a-dish picture gets right:** the recipe already works and tastes good; the rough edges are known and each has a standard tweak — you don't throw the dish out. **Where it breaks down:** a cook fixes a dish by taste; these fixes are specific engineering techniques with names, which we point to below.

#### Limit 1 · Cost grows with the square of the token count
The dinner-table gift has a price. Because every tile talks to every tile, the work grows with the **square** of the number of tiles `N` — double the tiles and the cost roughly *quadruples*. This is the [[quadratic cost||Attention compares every pair of tokens, so its cost grows like N × N. Doubling the tokens roughly quadruples the work.]] of attention. It's why very high-resolution images or very tiny patches (both of which mean lots of tiles) get expensive fast — the same "many tiny pizza slivers" trade-off from yesterday. The known softening: later models use **windowed / hierarchical attention** (each tile talks only to a nearby group at first, then groups merge) so the cost stops exploding. You'll meet the deep form of that (a family called Swin) in a later day; today just know the fix exists.

#### Limit 2 · A plain ViT only labels the whole picture
The recipe we built reads *one* answer off the `[CLS]` token — "this whole picture is a cat." That's [[image classification||Assigning one label to the whole image. A plain ViT does this; it does not by itself locate or outline objects.]]. It does **not**, on its own, tell you *where* the cat is (a box around it) or trace its exact outline. Those richer tasks — **detection** (draw boxes) and **segmentation** (outline every pixel) — need extra task-specific heads bolted on top of the ViT. A plain ViT is a strong *reader* of the whole picture; turning it into a *locator* is a separate build (a later topic).

#### Limit 3 · Position stamps are tied to the training image size
Remember yesterday's fix for the "shuffled tiles" puzzle — we *added* a learned [[positional embedding||A learned vector added to each tile so the model knows where the tile sat. Without it, shuffled tiles look identical to the model.]] to each tile so the model knows where each one sat. (Without it, the tiles are just an unordered bag — that's the permutation-blindness we cured on Day 2.) But we only learned *one stamp per slot*, for exactly the grid size the training images had. Feed the model a **bigger** picture — more tiles, more slots — and there aren't enough learned stamps to go around. The known fix has a name: **[[interpolate the positional embeddings||Gently stretch or shrink the grid of learned position stamps to a new number of slots, like resizing a photo, so a model trained at one resolution runs at another.]]** — gently stretch the grid of learned stamps to the new number of slots, like resizing a photo, so a model trained at one resolution can run at another.

The steps of the recipe, in order:
- **Step 1 · Pour in the tiles.** Yesterday's line of tile-passports *is* a sequence — the exact input a Transformer eats. No new machine needed.
- **Step 2 · Run the reused block, stacked.** One Transformer *block* = self-attention (look around) + a feed-forward layer (think), each wrapped in a *layer norm* and a *shortcut*. Stack `L` identical blocks in a relay. Nothing was changed from the text Transformer.
- **Step 3 · Every tile sees every tile.** From block one, attention gives a *global receptive field*: any tile can relate to any other tile at once — the CNN's telephone chain could only crawl outward layer by layer.
- **Step 4 · Ask the captain.** Read the whole-picture summary off the one special `[CLS]` token, which gathered from every tile.
- **Step 5 · A classification head.** A tiny final layer turns the `[CLS]` summary vector into a score per class → a label.
- **The model it replaces:** the *CNN*, whose built-in rules — *locality* and *translation-equivariance* — ViT drops and learns from data instead. That freedom is why we needed *tiles* (per-pixel tokens would be far too many pairs to compare).
- **And the honest bits:** attention's *quadratic cost* (→ windowed/hierarchical attention), whole-image *classification only* (→ detection/segmentation heads), position stamps *tied to image size* (→ interpolate them), and *data-hunger* from the missing rulebook (→ large-scale pretraining, then fine-tune).

#### Cheat-sheet · the words you met today
%%% jargon
Vision Transformer (ViT) | the text Transformer, unchanged, reading a sequence of image-tile tokens
Transformer block | one repeated step: self-attention + a feed-forward layer, each with a norm and shortcut
self-attention | the step where every tile looks at every other tile and pulls in what's useful
feed-forward layer | a small per-tile "think a bit more" step inside each block (an MLP)
layer norm | a gentle rescaling that keeps numbers healthy so a tall stack trains smoothly
shortcut (residual) | a skip path that adds a step's input back to its output so nothing gets lost
receptive field | how much of the picture one tile can see; ViT's is global from block one
CNN | the older image model that slides small windows over neighborhoods (locality + translation-equivariance)
inductive bias | helpful assumptions baked in before training; a CNN has them, a plain ViT barely does
class token / [CLS] | the leader token that gathers a whole-image summary; we read the answer off it
classification head | a tiny final layer turning the [CLS] summary into a class score
quadratic cost | attention compares every pair, so cost grows like N × N
pretrain then fine-tune | train on a huge general set first, then specialize on your small task — feeds ViT's data-hunger
interpolate positional embeddings | stretch the learned position stamps to a new image size
