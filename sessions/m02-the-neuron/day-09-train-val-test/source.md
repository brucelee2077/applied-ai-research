@@@ hero
@lede Think about studying for a school test. Your teacher gives you a **practice sheet** with the answers on the back so you can learn from it. But the *real* exam has different questions — and the answer key stays locked in the teacher's desk until grading day. Why not just let you practice on the real exam itself? Because then you would not learn the subject at all — you would only memorize those exact questions, and your grade would be a lie. Here is the surprising part: a neural network falls into the exact same trap. If we grade it on the very examples it studied, we cannot tell whether it *learned the pattern* or just *memorized the answers*. So we split our data into a practice pile, a check-up pile, and a sealed exam — the same trick every serious model behind ChatGPT uses to prove it really learned. Today you get to run that split with your own hands and catch a model in the act of memorizing.
@goal Together we will build a real gut feel for splitting data: why we never grade a model on what it studied, what each of the three splits (train, validation, test) is actually *for*, how the gap between two of them lets you *see* overfitting happen, and the sneaky ways a split can go wrong — peeking at the sealed exam, the same question leaking into two piles, an unlucky slice — each with its simple fix. Every idea comes with a picture first. No heavy math — just intuition you can steer.

@@@ concept id=c1 tag="Why split?" title="Why we never grade a model on what it studied" gotit="Got why we split"
Let us start with the one question the whole day answers. Imagine a friend who "studied" for a history test by memorizing the answers to *last year's exact test paper*. On that old paper they score 100%. But hand them *this year's* paper, with fresh questions on the same topic, and they fall apart. Did they learn history? No. They learned *those answers*. A neural network can do the very same thing: it can score perfectly on the examples it trained on by quietly memorizing each one, yet be useless on anything new. The word for succeeding on *new, unseen* examples is [[generalization||doing well on fresh examples the model never saw during training — the real goal, as opposed to just memorizing the ones it studied]] — and generalization is the only thing we actually care about. So here is the rule that fixes everything: **a model must be judged on data it has NOT seen, or the judgement is dishonest.** That is the reason we hold some data back instead of letting the model study all of it. This is the *exam-vs-answer-key* idea at the heart of today.

**What the exam picture gets right:** grading someone on the exact paper they memorized tells you nothing about whether they know the subject — you learn the truth only from *fresh* questions. That is precisely why we hold data back. **Where it breaks down:** a student *knows* they are memorizing and could choose to stop; a model has no such awareness — it will happily memorize every example unless we design the check that catches it.

#### The honest-grade rule, in one line
So the whole practice rests on a single sentence: **keep some examples hidden during learning, and grade only on those.** A score on data the model already saw is like a chef tasting a dish they cooked — of course they like it. The only score worth trusting comes from a plate they have never tasted. Everything else today is just deciding *how* to carve out those hidden examples wisely — and every way it can quietly go wrong.

@@@ concept id=c2 tag="Three piles" title="The three splits — and what each one is for" gotit="Got the three piles"
Now, *how many* hidden piles do we need, and what is each one's job? The clean answer is **three**, and the practice sheet makes each one obvious. Think of three separate stacks of question cards. The first is the **practice sheet** — questions *with* the answers, the ones you study from. The second is a small **weekly quiz** you take during the week to see if your studying is working and to decide what to change (study more? switch methods?). The third is the **final exam**, sealed away, opened once at the very end for your real grade. In machine learning those three stacks have names: the [[training set||the pile the model actually learns on — it adjusts its weights to fit these examples]], the [[validation set||a held-out pile checked DURING development to track progress and pick settings, without ever touching the final exam]], and the [[test set||a pile sealed away and scored only ONCE at the very end, to get one honest final number]]. Each pile has a different job, and mixing up the jobs is how things go wrong.

**What the three-stacks picture gets right:** the three piles really do have three distinct jobs — study, check, grade — and only one of them is ever opened for the real score. **Where it breaks down:** real cards are physical, so you cannot accidentally study the exam; with data it is dangerously easy to let the exam pile leak into studying, which is the whole set of traps we cover soon.

#### The one salient rule: who touches what
Here is the property to burn into memory — *which* pile is allowed to change the model:

- **Only the training set updates the model's weights.** It is the *only* pile the model learns from.
- **The validation set guides your choices** — is it improving? should I stop now? which settings are best? — but the model never *learns* from it directly.
- **The test set is a one-time honest verdict.** You look at it once, at the end, and never let it change anything.

Keep those three roles straight and everything else today is just protecting them. Next: why *two* piles were not enough, and where the middle one came from.

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="Why three, not two" title="Where the middle pile came from" gotit="Got the history"
You might wonder: why not just *two* piles — one to study, one to grade? That is exactly how people did it at first, and it seems fine until you watch what really happens. Picture a student with only a practice sheet and one final exam, no weekly quiz. They study, take the final, score 70%. They tweak how they study, take *the same final again*, score 80%. Tweak again, take it again, 88%… After enough rounds of tuning-against-the-exam, that 88% is not an honest grade anymore — they have quietly shaped their studying to *that specific exam*. The exam got **contaminated** the moment it started guiding their choices. The same thing happens to a model: every time you look at the test score and change something because of it, you leak a little test-knowledge back into the model. The fix was to carve out a *third* pile — a **validation set** — to be the thing you tune against, so the real exam stays pristine.

**What the re-taking-the-exam picture gets right:** the moment a score starts steering your decisions, it stops being an unbiased grade — repeated tuning leaks the exam's contents into your choices. That is a real, subtle danger. **Where it breaks down:** a person contaminates an exam slowly and might notice; a model can contaminate its test set instantly and invisibly, in a single automated tuning run — so the discipline of keeping validation and test separate matters even more than it does at school.

So: **two piles are not enough, because you always end up tuning against the grade-pile and spoiling it.** The middle pile — validation — is the thing you are *allowed* to tune against, sacrificing it to keep the test set honest. Now for the sneaky failure that all of this is really designed to catch.

@@@ concept id=c4 tag="Overfitting" title="Overfitting — when the model memorizes instead of learns" gotit="Got overfitting"
This is the failure the whole *exam-vs-answer-key* setup exists to catch, so it deserves a name and a clear picture. Picture a student who, instead of understanding the topic, memorizes their practice sheet down to the tiniest useless detail — every exact word, the order the answers came in, even the little **coffee stain** on question 4. On that one sheet they are flawless. But coffee stains and answer-order are *noise* — they say nothing about the subject — so on a fresh exam, where none of that junk appears, the student falls apart. When a model does this, we call it [[overfitting||when a model memorizes the exact training examples (including their random noise) instead of learning the general pattern — so it scores high on training data but poorly on new data]].

**What the memorizing-student picture gets right:** clinging to noise — the exact words, the order, the coffee stain — feels like knowledge on the one sheet you studied, but it is worthless anywhere else, so a fresh exam exposes it. **Where it breaks down:** a student could, in principle, *notice* they are cramming trivia; a model cannot — it will absorb every stray detail unless the pattern is simpler to store than the noise, which it usually is not for a big flexible model.

#### From "memorizing the noise" to a wiggly curve
Now watch *what that memorizing does to the model's shape.* This is the heart of overfitting, so let us build it up one step at a time. Say your training data are seven dots that follow a gentle rising trend, jiggled up and down a little by random noise. A model that **learns the pattern** draws a *smooth* line through the middle of the cloud — it ignores the jiggle. A model that **memorizes** does the opposite: it bends and detours to pass *exactly* through every single dot, chasing each bit of noise. Passing through every dot forces the line to whip up and down between them — a **wiggly** curve. The picture below shows the same seven dots twice so you can *see* memorizing turn into wiggle:

In panel ③ the wiggly line really does bend through every dot — that is memorizing made visible. **Where the drawing breaks down:** here you see one tidy curve in 2-D, but a real model has thousands of weights doing this in many dimensions at once — you cannot eyeball the wiggle, which is exactly why you need a *number* (the next concept) to detect it.

#### Cause and symptom, side by side
Let us pin the two halves down so you can name them on sight:

- **Cause:** the model *memorizes the training data* — it has enough capacity to store the examples (and their random noise) instead of learning the pattern beneath them.
- **Symptom:** it **aces the practice** (high training score) **but fails on new data** (low score on unseen examples). The bigger that gap, the worse the overfitting.

The word "overfitting" literally means *fitting too much* — bending to the training data past the real pattern into the noise. Good news: because the symptom is a *gap between two scores*, we can measure it directly. That measurement is the single most useful diagnostic in all of training — and it is next.

@@@ concept id=c5 tag="The gap" title="The train–val gap — how you SEE overfitting happen" gotit="Got the gap"
Here is the beautiful part: you do not need to *guess* whether a model is overfitting — you can *watch* it happen on a chart. Think of two runners on the same track: one is the training score, one is the validation score. Early in training they run side by side — both improving together, staying close. That closeness means the model is learning real patterns that work on *both* piles. But if the model starts memorizing, the two runners split apart: the training runner keeps sprinting ahead (training loss keeps dropping) while the validation runner slows and even turns back (validation loss stops dropping and starts *rising*). **The space that opens up between the two runners is overfitting, made visible.** We call it the [[train–val gap||the difference between the training score and the validation score — a small, steady gap is healthy; a gap that keeps widening is overfitting you can see]]. Watching that gap is how every practitioner catches memorizing in the act.

#### Reading the gap — the practitioner's habit
Drag the slider above from left to right and watch the story unfold: early epochs, curves together with a small steady gap (learning); at the lowest validation point, the sweet spot; later epochs, validation rises while training falls and the gap opens wide (memorizing). **The lowest point of the validation curve is the best model** — training past it only makes things worse on new data. #### The three traps, named — cause → fix
- **Peeking (test-set leakage).** *Cause:* you make decisions by looking at the test score — even once — so it stops being an honest grade. *Fix:* keep the test set untouched and use it exactly **once**, at the very end.
- **Leakage across splits.** *Cause:* the same or near-duplicate rows land in more than one pile, or you *preprocess* (scale, fill blanks) using the whole dataset *before* splitting — so information from the eval piles bleeds into training. *Fix:* **split the data FIRST**, then de-duplicate across piles, and fit any preprocessing (scaling, filling blanks) on the **training split only** — then apply those same fitted values to the validation and test splits (never re-fit them on the eval piles).
- **Unshuffled / unrepresentative slice.** *Cause:* ordered data (say, sorted by date or label) sliced straight down the middle sends all of one kind to train and another kind to test — a lopsided, unrepresentative split. *Fix:* **shuffle before splitting** so each pile is a fair sample of the whole.

Get these three habits right and your splits stay honest. But there is one more failure that no single split can fix on its own — the unlucky slice — and it has its own named remedy.

@@@ concept id=c8 tag="What it can't do" title="What a good split cannot do for you" gotit="Got the limits"

**What the thermometer picture gets right:** it gives you an honest reading you can act on, but the reading itself changes nothing — and it is only valid when used correctly. That is precisely a data split. **Where it breaks down:** a thermometer measures one number; a split measures *generalization to data like your splits* — a subtler thing, and the "used correctly" condition (same world) is easy to forget.

#### Limit 1 — the thermometer diagnoses, it does not cure
Follow the thermometer one step further. It can *read* your fever perfectly — but reading is not healing; you still need medicine. A split is the same: it *reveals* whether learning transferred to new data, but it does not *cause* that learning. If your model is too simple to capture the pattern (think of trying to draw a curve with a straight ruler), a perfect train/val/test split will faithfully report "this does not generalize" — and then stop, having done its one job. **The split diagnoses generalization; it does not create it.** No amount of clever splitting will *make* a too-simple model fit — that needs a better model or better features. The split just tells you honestly that you need them.

#### Limit 2 — a thermometer trusted in the wrong place lies
Now the second step of the analogy. Press a thermometer to a *warm radiator* and it will happily read 40°C — a perfectly precise number about the *wrong thing*. The instrument is only trustworthy when it measures the body you care about. A split has the same catch: its grade is honest **only for data drawn from the same world as your splits.** If tomorrow's real-world data looks different — new users, a new season, a new kind of input — your glowing test score is measuring the wrong world, and says *nothing* about how the model does there. That is the whole arc in four clicks — study, check, grade, and watch the gap. Now the same story in words:

- **Why split.** A model must be judged on data it has NOT seen, or the grade is a lie. We care about generalization, not memorizing.
- **Three piles.** *Train* — the only pile the model learns on. *Validation* — a held-out pile you check progress on and tune settings against, during development. *Test* — sealed away, opened ONCE for an honest final score.
- **Why three, not two.** Tuning against the test pile quietly contaminates it, so the validation pile exists to protect the test pile.
- **Overfitting.** *Cause:* the model memorizes the training data (and its noise), which shows up as a wiggly curve. *Symptom:* aces the practice, fails on new data.
- **See it with the gap.** Watch the train–val gap: close and both dropping is healthy; a widening gap where validation *rises* is overfitting. The **lowest validation point is the best model** — stopping right there (early stopping) is one of a family of fixes called regularization, for a later lesson.
- **Split traps.** *Peeking* → keep test sealed, use once. *Leakage* → split FIRST, then de-dup and fit preprocessing on train only. *Unshuffled* → shuffle before slicing.
- **Unlucky slice.** A single small split is high-variance; **cross-validation (k-fold)** rotates the slice and averages. Common ratios ~70/15/15 or ~80/10/10, but no ratio is sacred.
- **Two honest limits.** A split *diagnoses* generalization, it does not *create* it; and its verdict holds only for data from the *same distribution* as your splits.

The quick diagnosis table — spot the trouble, know the fix:

%%% table
:: What you see :: What it means :: The fix
train score high, val score low & rising :: overfitting (memorizing) :: stop at the lowest val point (early stopping); regularize
you tuned using the test score :: test set contaminated (peeking) :: keep test sealed; use it ONCE
same/duplicate rows in two piles :: leakage across splits :: split FIRST, de-dup, fit preprocessing on train only
one slice easy, another hard :: unlucky, high-variance split :: cross-validation (k-fold), average scores
great test score, bad in the real world :: distribution shift (different world) :: (monitoring lesson) — retrain on new data

%%% jargon
generalization | doing well on fresh, unseen examples — the real goal, not memorizing the ones you studied
training set | the pile the model actually learns on (adjusts its weights)
validation set | a held-out pile you check progress on and tune settings against, during development
test set | a sealed pile scored ONCE at the very end for an honest final grade
overfitting | memorizing the training examples (and their noise) instead of the pattern — high train score, low new-data score

@@@ produce id=produce tag="Produce" title="Catch a model overfitting with your own eyes" gotit="Done"
Time to *feel* the whole spine in one run: train a model, watch the train and validation losses, and catch the exact moment memorizing beats learning. You will see the *exam-vs-answer-key* idea turn into two curves and a gap.

The setup is the simplest honest experiment: make a small dataset with a real pattern plus a little random noise, **shuffle it, then split it** into a training pile and a validation pile (say 80/20). Train a flexible little model for many epochs. After each epoch, record two numbers: the loss on the training pile and the loss on the validation pile.

**Predict first, before you run it:** for the *early* epochs, will the two losses be close or far apart? And for the *late* epochs, what will happen to the validation loss while the training loss keeps dropping? Write your two guesses down.

**What you should see** when you plot both losses against epoch:
- **Early epochs** → both losses drop **together and stay close** — the model is learning real pattern that works on both piles (healthy).
- **The lowest validation point** → the validation loss reaches its **minimum**, then turns around — this lowest-validation epoch is the *best* model, and exactly where early stopping would stop.
- **Late epochs** → the training loss keeps dropping toward zero but the **validation loss rises** past its minimum — the gap widens. That gap *is* overfitting, now visible on your screen.
- Note the epoch of lowest validation loss — stopping there (early stopping) would have given the best model on new data.
