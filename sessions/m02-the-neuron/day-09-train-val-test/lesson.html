Module 2 · Train · Day 9

Train / Val / Test & OverfittingDid the Model Learn, or Just Memorize?

Think about studying for a school test. Your teacher gives you a practice sheet with the answers on the back so you can learn from it. But the real exam has different questions — and the answer key stays locked in the teacher's desk until grading day. Why not just let you practice on the real exam itself? Because then you would not learn the subject at all — you would only memorize those exact questions, and your grade would be a lie. Here is the surprising part: a neural network falls into the exact same trap. If we grade it on the very examples it studied, we cannot tell whether it learned the pattern or just memorized the answers. So we split our data into a practice pile, a check-up pile, and a sealed exam — the same trick every serious model behind ChatGPT uses to prove it really learned. Today you get to run that split with your own hands and catch a model in the act of memorizing.

🎯
Together we will build a real gut feel for splitting data: why we never grade a model on what it studied, what each of the three splits (train, validation, test) is actually for, how the gap between two of them lets you see overfitting happen, and the sneaky ways a split can go wrong — peeking at the sealed exam, the same question leaking into two piles, an unlucky slice — each with its simple fix. Every idea comes with a picture first. No heavy math — just intuition you can steer.
1Why we never grade a model on what it studiedWhy split?

Let us start with the one question the whole day answers. Imagine a friend who "studied" for a history test by memorizing the answers to last year's exact test paper. On that old paper they score 100%. But hand them this year's paper, with fresh questions on the same topic, and they fall apart. Did they learn history? No. They learned those answers. A neural network can do the very same thing: it can score perfectly on the examples it trained on by quietly memorizing each one, yet be useless on anything new. The word for succeeding on new, unseen examples is generalization — and generalization is the only thing we actually care about. So here is the rule that fixes everything: a model must be judged on data it has NOT seen, or the judgement is dishonest. That is the reason we hold some data back instead of letting the model study all of it. This is the exam-vs-answer-key idea at the heart of today.

What the exam picture gets right: grading someone on the exact paper they memorized tells you nothing about whether they know the subject — you learn the truth only from fresh questions. That is precisely why we hold data back. Where it breaks down: a student knows they are memorizing and could choose to stop; a model has no such awareness — it will happily memorize every example unless we design the check that catches it.

The honest-grade rule, in one line

So the whole practice rests on a single sentence: keep some examples hidden during learning, and grade only on those. A score on data the model already saw is like a chef tasting a dish they cooked — of course they like it. The only score worth trusting comes from a plate they have never tasted. Everything else today is just deciding how to carve out those hidden examples wisely — and every way it can quietly go wrong.

2The three splits — and what each one is forThree piles

Now, how many hidden piles do we need, and what is each one's job? The clean answer is three, and the practice sheet makes each one obvious. Think of three separate stacks of question cards. The first is the practice sheet — questions with the answers, the ones you study from. The second is a small weekly quiz you take during the week to see if your studying is working and to decide what to change (study more? switch methods?). The third is the final exam, sealed away, opened once at the very end for your real grade. In machine learning those three stacks have names: the training set, the validation set, and the test set. Each pile has a different job, and mixing up the jobs is how things go wrong.

What the three-stacks picture gets right: the three piles really do have three distinct jobs — study, check, grade — and only one of them is ever opened for the real score. Where it breaks down: real cards are physical, so you cannot accidentally study the exam; with data it is dangerously easy to let the exam pile leak into studying, which is the whole set of traps we cover soon.

The one salient rule: who touches what

Here is the property to burn into memory — which pile is allowed to change the model:

  • Only the training set updates the model's weights. It is the only pile the model learns from.
  • The validation set guides your choices — is it improving? should I stop now? which settings are best? — but the model never learns from it directly.
  • The test set is a one-time honest verdict. You look at it once, at the end, and never let it change anything.

Keep those three roles straight and everything else today is just protecting them. Next: why two piles were not enough, and where the middle one came from.

3Where the middle pile came fromWhy three, not two

You might wonder: why not just two piles — one to study, one to grade? That is exactly how people did it at first, and it seems fine until you watch what really happens. Picture a student with only a practice sheet and one final exam, no weekly quiz. They study, take the final, score 70%. They tweak how they study, take the same final again, score 80%. Tweak again, take it again, 88%… After enough rounds of tuning-against-the-exam, that 88% is not an honest grade anymore — they have quietly shaped their studying to that specific exam. The exam got contaminated the moment it started guiding their choices. The same thing happens to a model: every time you look at the test score and change something because of it, you leak a little test-knowledge back into the model. The fix was to carve out a third pile — a validation set — to be the thing you tune against, so the real exam stays pristine.

What the re-taking-the-exam picture gets right: the moment a score starts steering your decisions, it stops being an unbiased grade — repeated tuning leaks the exam's contents into your choices. That is a real, subtle danger. Where it breaks down: a person contaminates an exam slowly and might notice; a model can contaminate its test set instantly and invisibly, in a single automated tuning run — so the discipline of keeping validation and test separate matters even more than it does at school.

So: two piles are not enough, because you always end up tuning against the grade-pile and spoiling it. The middle pile — validation — is the thing you are allowed to tune against, sacrificing it to keep the test set honest. Now for the sneaky failure that all of this is really designed to catch.

4Overfitting — when the model memorizes instead of learnsOverfitting

This is the failure the whole exam-vs-answer-key setup exists to catch, so it deserves a name and a clear picture. Picture a student who, instead of understanding the topic, memorizes their practice sheet down to the tiniest useless detail — every exact word, the order the answers came in, even the little coffee stain on question 4. On that one sheet they are flawless. But coffee stains and answer-order are noise — they say nothing about the subject — so on a fresh exam, where none of that junk appears, the student falls apart. When a model does this, we call it overfitting.

What the memorizing-student picture gets right: clinging to noise — the exact words, the order, the coffee stain — feels like knowledge on the one sheet you studied, but it is worthless anywhere else, so a fresh exam exposes it. Where it breaks down: a student could, in principle, notice they are cramming trivia; a model cannot — it will absorb every stray detail unless the pattern is simpler to store than the noise, which it usually is not for a big flexible model.

From "memorizing the noise" to a wiggly curve

Now watch what that memorizing does to the model's shape. This is the heart of overfitting, so let us build it up one step at a time. Say your training data are seven dots that follow a gentle rising trend, jiggled up and down a little by random noise. A model that learns the pattern draws a smooth line through the middle of the cloud — it ignores the jiggle. A model that memorizes does the opposite: it bends and detours to pass exactly through every single dot, chasing each bit of noise. Passing through every dot forces the line to whip up and down between them — a wiggly curve. The picture below shows the same seven dots twice so you can see memorizing turn into wiggle:

What this build-up shows: the exact same seven dots give a calm, useful curve when the model ignores the jiggle (②), and a frantic, useless curve when it insists on touching every dot (③). In panel ③ the wiggly line really does bend through every dot — that is memorizing made visible. Where the drawing breaks down: here you see one tidy curve in 2-D, but a real model has thousands of weights doing this in many dimensions at once — you cannot eyeball the wiggle, which is exactly why you need a number (the next concept) to detect it.

Cause and symptom, side by side

Let us pin the two halves down so you can name them on sight:

  • Cause: the model memorizes the training data — it has enough capacity to store the examples (and their random noise) instead of learning the pattern beneath them.
  • Symptom: it aces the practice (high training score) but fails on new data (low score on unseen examples). The bigger that gap, the worse the overfitting.

The word "overfitting" literally means fitting too much — bending to the training data past the real pattern into the noise. Good news: because the symptom is a gap between two scores, we can measure it directly. That measurement is the single most useful diagnostic in all of training — and it is next.

5The train–val gap — how you SEE overfitting happenThe gap

Here is the beautiful part: you do not need to guess whether a model is overfitting — you can watch it happen on a chart. Think of two runners on the same track: one is the training score, one is the validation score. Early in training they run side by side — both improving together, staying close. That closeness means the model is learning real patterns that work on both piles. But if the model starts memorizing, the two runners split apart: the training runner keeps sprinting ahead (training loss keeps dropping) while the validation runner slows and even turns back (validation loss stops dropping and starts rising). The space that opens up between the two runners is overfitting, made visible. We call it the train–val gap. Watching that gap is how every practitioner catches memorizing in the act.

epoch 6 → both curves dropping together, small steady gap — healthy learning ✓

What the two-runners picture gets right: while the model learns real patterns, both scores improve together and stay close; the moment it starts memorizing, only the training score keeps improving and the two split apart — the widening space is the overfitting. Where it breaks down: real runners can only slow down, but a validation curve can actively turn upward — the model getting genuinely worse on new data even as it looks better on practice.

Reading the gap — the practitioner's habit

Drag the slider above from left to right and watch the story unfold: early epochs, curves together with a small steady gap (learning); at the lowest validation point, the sweet spot; later epochs, validation rises while training falls and the gap opens wide (memorizing). The lowest point of the validation curve is the best model — training past it only makes things worse on new data. Stopping training right at that lowest-validation epoch has a name you will meet in a later lesson: early stopping (one of a family of overfitting fixes called regularization, along with weight decay and dropout — those mechanics are for the training-loop lesson ahead). For today, the whole point is this: the train–val gap is how you SEE overfitting, and the remedy-check is simply to watch it — and stop at the lowest validation point. Next: the sneaky ways a split itself can lie to you.

6Three sneaky ways a split lies — and their fixesSplit traps

Even with three piles, a split can quietly betray you. These are the traps that fool even careful people, and each has a one-move fix. Think of dealing cards for a fair game: the deck must be shuffled, no card can be in two hands at once, and nobody peeks at the sealed pile. Break any of those three and the game is rigged — and so is your model's score.

What the card-game picture gets right: a fair game needs a shuffled deck, no card in two hands, and a sealed pile nobody peeks at — break any and the outcome is rigged, exactly like a split. Where it breaks down: in cards you can see a cheat; in data these leaks are invisible — a duplicate row or a peek leaves no mark, so you have to prevent them by discipline, not spot them by eye.

Each trap as a before → after

Now let us watch each trap turn from rigged into honest — the cause on the left, the one-move fix on the right. Read each row as "here is how it breaks, and here is the single move that repairs it."

What this before → after build-up shows: each trap has the same shape — a leak of information into the wrong pile — and each fix is a single disciplined move that plugs the leak. Where it breaks down: the drawing makes each trap look separate and obvious, but in a real pipeline they hide inside ordinary-looking code (a .sort() here, a fit() before a split there), so the fixes are habits you apply up front, not bugs you notice later.

The three traps, named — cause → fix

  • Peeking (test-set leakage). Cause: you make decisions by looking at the test score — even once — so it stops being an honest grade. Fix: keep the test set untouched and use it exactly once, at the very end.
  • Leakage across splits. Cause: the same or near-duplicate rows land in more than one pile, or you preprocess (scale, fill blanks) using the whole dataset before splitting — so information from the eval piles bleeds into training. Fix: split the data FIRST, then de-duplicate across piles, and fit any preprocessing (scaling, filling blanks) on the training split only — then apply those same fitted values to the validation and test splits (never re-fit them on the eval piles).
  • Unshuffled / unrepresentative slice. Cause: ordered data (say, sorted by date or label) sliced straight down the middle sends all of one kind to train and another kind to test — a lopsided, unrepresentative split. Fix: shuffle before splitting so each pile is a fair sample of the whole.

Get these three habits right and your splits stay honest. But there is one more failure that no single split can fix on its own — the unlucky slice — and it has its own named remedy.

7The unlucky slice — and cross-validationCross-validation

Even a perfectly shuffled, leak-free split can be unlucky. Picture cutting a small cake into slices and judging the whole cake by tasting one slice — if that slice happened to land on the one raisin, you would wrongly declare the whole cake raisin-heavy. With a small validation pile, one random slice can flatter or punish your model purely by chance: you might get a lucky-easy validation set (score looks great) or an unlucky-hard one (score looks terrible), and neither is the true picture. The cause is that a single small split is high-variance — its score wobbles a lot depending on which examples happened to land in it. The remedy has a name: cross-validation — most often k-fold cross-validation.

What the tasting-one-slice picture gets right: judging a whole cake from one slice can badly mislead you if that slice was unusual — and rotating so every slice gets tasted, then averaging, gives the true flavor. That is exactly k-fold cross-validation. Where it breaks down: cake slices are identical in size but data slices vary in content, and cross-validation costs you k times the training work — so on huge datasets a single big split is usually enough, because a big slice is already a fair sample (the full mechanics of k-fold are a later data lesson).

How much goes in each pile?

One last practical question a beginner always asks: how big should each pile be? Common starting points you will see are around 70/15/15 or 80/10/10 — train / validation / test. But here is the freeing truth: no ratio is sacred. The reason is simple — a validation or test pile just needs to be big enough to be a fair sample. With a tiny dataset you might give eval a bigger fraction; with millions of examples, even 1% is plenty for an honest grade, so you keep almost everything for training. The picture below shows the two common ratios — read them as "most for study, a slice each to check and to grade."

So even a good split can be unlucky, and cross-validation (k-fold) is the named fix: rotate which slice is validation, average the scores. And the exact ratio is a judgement call, not a law. One thing left — the honest limits of even a perfect split.

8What a good split cannot do for youWhat it can't do

A great split is honest — but honesty has limits, and knowing them keeps you from expecting magic. Think of a thermometer. A good thermometer tells you truthfully that you have a fever — but it does not make you healthy, and it only reads correctly if you use it the right way. A data split is exactly this kind of honest instrument: it reveals the truth about your model; it does not improve the model, and its reading is only trustworthy under one condition.

What the thermometer picture gets right: it gives you an honest reading you can act on, but the reading itself changes nothing — and it is only valid when used correctly. That is precisely a data split. Where it breaks down: a thermometer measures one number; a split measures generalization to data like your splits — a subtler thing, and the "used correctly" condition (same world) is easy to forget.

Limit 1 — the thermometer diagnoses, it does not cure

Follow the thermometer one step further. It can read your fever perfectly — but reading is not healing; you still need medicine. A split is the same: it reveals whether learning transferred to new data, but it does not cause that learning. If your model is too simple to capture the pattern (think of trying to draw a curve with a straight ruler), a perfect train/val/test split will faithfully report "this does not generalize" — and then stop, having done its one job. The split diagnoses generalization; it does not create it. No amount of clever splitting will make a too-simple model fit — that needs a better model or better features. The split just tells you honestly that you need them.

Limit 2 — a thermometer trusted in the wrong place lies

Now the second step of the analogy. Press a thermometer to a warm radiator and it will happily read 40°C — a perfectly precise number about the wrong thing. The instrument is only trustworthy when it measures the body you care about. A split has the same catch: its grade is honest only for data drawn from the same world as your splits. If tomorrow's real-world data looks different — new users, a new season, a new kind of input — your glowing test score is measuring the wrong world, and says nothing about how the model does there. Let us draw that mismatch:

What the two-world build-up shows: every split you hold out is still scooped from the same World A, so it can only promise how you do on World-A-like data; the moment reality becomes World B, the promise expires. That mismatch has a name you will meet in a monitoring lesson: distribution shift. Where the drawing breaks down: the two worlds look cleanly separate, but real shift is usually gradual and partial — World B slowly bleeds in — which is exactly why you keep watching in production rather than trusting one old test score forever.

So even the best split has two honest limits, both flowing straight from the thermometer: it measures generalization without creating it, and its verdict holds only for data like your splits. Now let us gather the whole day onto one page.

9The whole day on one pageRecap

Here is a fresh way to hold the whole day in your head. Picture a driving test. You practice for weeks in your own neighbourhood (the training set). Now and then a friend rides along and quizzes you on tricky moves so you can fix them before the big day (the validation set). Finally an examiner you have never met takes you on a route you have never driven, once, for your real licence (the test set). If you had "practiced" by memorizing that exact examiner's route, you would pass the test but be a danger on any other road — that is overfitting, and the gap between your smooth practice runs and your shaky new-route runs is how anyone would see it. Where the driving-test picture breaks down: a driving examiner can adapt the route on the spot, but your test set is fixed the moment you seal it — so you must design it fairly up front, because you only get to use it once.

  • Why split. A model must be judged on data it has NOT seen, or the grade is a lie. We care about generalization, not memorizing.
  • Three piles. Train — the only pile the model learns on. Validation — a held-out pile you check progress on and tune settings against, during development. Test — sealed away, opened ONCE for an honest final score.

  • Unlucky slice. A single small split is high-variance; cross-validation (k-fold) rotates the slice and averages. Common ratios ~70/15/15 or ~80/10/10, but no ratio is sacred.

Catch a model overfitting with your own eyesProduce

Time to feel the whole spine in one run: train a model, watch the train and validation losses, and catch the exact moment memorizing beats learning. You will see the exam-vs-answer-key idea turn into two curves and a gap.

The setup is the simplest honest experiment: make a small dataset with a real pattern plus a little random noise, shuffle it, then split it into a training pile and a validation pile (say 80/20). Train a flexible little model for many epochs. After each epoch, record two numbers: the loss on the training pile and the loss on the validation pile.

Predict first, before you run it: for the early epochs, will the two losses be close or far apart? And for the late epochs, what will happen to the validation loss while the training loss keeps dropping? Write your two guesses down.

What you should see when you plot both losses against epoch:

  • Early epochs → both losses drop together and stay close — the model is learning real pattern that works on both piles (healthy).
  • The lowest validation point → the validation loss reaches its minimum, then turns around — this lowest-validation epoch is the best model, and exactly where early stopping would stop.
  • Late epochs → the training loss keeps dropping toward zero but the validation loss rises past its minimum — the gap widens. That gap is overfitting, now visible on your screen.
  • Note the epoch of lowest validation loss — stopping there (early stopping) would have given the best model on new data.

Write it in experiment.py and run it. Then poke it: try not shuffling before the split and watch the curves misbehave; shrink the dataset and watch the validation curve get noisier (the unlucky-slice effect); make the model smaller and watch the gap shrink because a simpler model has less room to memorize.

Option A — write it yourself. In experiment.py, build a noisy dataset, shuffle and split 80/20, train a flexible model for many epochs, and after each epoch append train_loss and val_loss to two lists. Print (or plot) both and mark the epoch where val_loss is lowest.
