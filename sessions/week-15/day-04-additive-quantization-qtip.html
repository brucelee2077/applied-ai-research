Additive Quantization
AQLM & QTIP

Your Pallas/CUDA kernels from earlier this week can multiply matrices fast — but if the weights are still fat FP16 numbers, the kernel is starving on memory bandwidth. Today you learn how AQLM and QTIP shrink weights down to 2-3 bits each, using learned codebooks instead of a fixed rounding grid.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain how Additive Quantization (AQ) differs from Product Quantization and from scalar round-to-nearest, walk through AQLM's per-layer-then-joint-block algorithm, explain why QTIP's trellis codes beat AQ's codebooks at extreme compression, and build a small from-scratch AQ reproduction.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is Additive Quantization, and why does it exist?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know what post-training quantization (PTQ) is — shrinking a trained model's weights from 16-bit floats to fewer bits, after training is done. You've also just written fast matmul kernels earlier this week. No prior quantization-algorithm experience needed.

The problem: your fast kernel is only as good as its inputs

Say you wrote a beautiful Pallas kernel that multiplies matrices at full GPU speed. If the weight matrix it reads is still stored as FP16 (16 bits per number), the kernel spends most of its time just moving those bytes from memory, not computing. This is called being memory-bandwidth bound. The fix: make the weights smaller. That's what today is about — shrinking weights down to 2-3 bits per number, roughly 8x smaller than FP16.

The old way: scalar quantization

The simplest PTQ method is round-to-nearest (RTN): pick a small grid of allowed numbers (say, 4 values for 2 bits), and snap each weight independently to the closest grid point. GPTQ, a more careful cousin, does the same "one weight at a time" snapping but corrects for errors as it goes. Both are direct or scalar methods: each number's rounding decision does not know about its neighbors.

The waste: two weights sitting next to each other in a row are often correlated — their rounding errors could partly cancel if you quantized them together instead of alone. Scalar methods throw this opportunity away.

The new way: Additive Quantization (AQ)

Additive Quantization comes from a 15-year-old field called Multi-Codebook Quantization (MCQ), originally built to compress databases of vectors for fast image search. Instead of quantizing one weight at a time, AQ groups a few weights together (say, g=8 in a row) and represents that whole group as a sum of entries picked from several small lookup tables, called codebooks. Each codebook holds 2^B candidate vectors (B = bits per codebook). You pick one entry from codebook 1, one from codebook 2, and so on, and add them up to approximate the group.

AQLM (Additive Quantization for Language Models — today's first paper) adapts this specifically for LLM weights, with two twists: it uses real calibration text to optimize for actual output error (not abstract vector similarity), and it jointly fine-tunes codebooks across a whole transformer block after quantizing each layer, so errors in one layer's codebook can be corrected by adjustments in another's.

QTIP (Quantization with Trellises and Incoherence Processing — today's second paper) solves the same 2-3 bit problem a different way: instead of summed codebooks, it uses trellis-coded quantization, an idea borrowed from 1970s-80s communications theory, which lets it represent a much bigger effective codebook while storing only a tiny lookup table.

So what's today's catch?

The catch is cost: finding the best combination of codebook entries is a hard combinatorial search, not a simple lookup. AQLM pays for this with beam search; QTIP pays for it with a cheaper trellis decoder. We'll unpack both in the playground and walkthrough.

📚
Official papers: AQLM (arXiv:2401.06118) · QTIP (arXiv:2406.11235) · AQLM code (GitHub)
2 · INTUITIONPacking cubes for your suitcase✓

Think about packing a suitcase using pre-made packing cubes.

👕📦
Scalar PTQ = one bag per item

Round-to-nearest packs every item into its own tiny labeled bag. Simple, but wasteful — a sock and a shirt packed together could share space far better than packed alone.

🗂️➕🗂️
AQ = stacked transparent trays

Additive Quantization is like having a "shape tray," a "color tray," and a "size tray" — see-through overlays. You describe your pile of clothes as tray_1 + tray_2 + tray_3 stacked together. Sums of a few standard trays can represent far more combinations than any single pre-made bag.

In one line: instead of picking the one closest pre-made bag for a group of weights, AQ describes the group as a sum of a few small, learned components — far more expressive at the same storage budget.

AQLM's "joint fine-tuning across layers" is like zipping up the whole suitcase, noticing which trays squish each other, and reshaping the trays slightly so the entire suitcase — not each item — ends up as close as possible to how it should look.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real packing trays are physical and fixed once made. AQLM's codebooks are numbers re-learned by gradient descent, and the "which tray-slot did I pick" choice is a discrete combinatorial search (beam search) — nothing like real packing. Also, there's no suitcase equivalent of QTIP's trellis trick; that one is closer to how a zip file's compression dictionary builds context as it scans forward through data.

4 · HOW & WHYAQ vs. PQ, and why QTIP goes further✓

The rules

  • AQ splits a weight row into groups of g consecutive weights. Each group is reconstructed as ŵ = C_1[b_1] + C_2[b_2] + ... + C_M[b_m] — a sum of one entry from each of M codebooks, where each codebook has 2^B candidate vectors.
  • Product Quantization (PQ), the older MCQ method, instead concatenates disjoint sub-vectors from separate codebooks. AQ's codebooks each cover the whole group and get summed — strictly more expressive, at the cost of a harder search.
  • AQLM optimizes for ||Wx - Ŵx||² over real calibration inputs x (data-aware), not just ||W - Ŵ||² in the abstract.
  • QTIP swaps summed codebooks for a trellis: a stateful decoder where each step emits a symbol and transitions to a new state based on a few "surprise" bits. This decouples bitrate from codebook size, so it can jointly quantize much higher-dimensional blocks than AQ's small groups (g≈8), more cheaply.
✅
ŵ_group = Σ_{m=1}^{M} C_m[b_m]
"Sum one selected entry from each of M codebooks." Same total bit budget as scalar quantization, but the sum can hit combinations a single fixed grid point never could.

Why it's a superpower

  • Expressive at fixed bit budget — a sum of a handful of learned components represents vastly more combinations than one lookup ever could.
  • Jointly optimized — AQLM's Phase 3 fine-tunes codebooks across a whole transformer block, letting quantization errors in different layers partially cancel through the block's own math.
  • Composable with kernels — like vmap composes with jit/grad, AQ's tiny stored codebooks compose with fused GPU/CPU kernels that reconstruct weights on the fly during the matmul.
🔗
Causal chain: fast kernels (earlier this week) → need small operands to stay fast → scalar PTQ wastes correlation between weights → AQ sums learned codebooks to capture it → QTIP's trellis pushes even further by decoupling bitrate from codebook size.

7 · PRODUCEBuild a from-scratch Additive Quantization demo✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): implement a small, demonstrative (not training-scale) reproduction of AQLM's core mechanism, and show that reconstruction error strictly drops as you add more codebooks at a fixed bit budget.

Acceptance criteria

  • A function that splits a synthetic weight matrix into groups of g elements.
  • Residual K-means initialization of M codebooks (cluster the groups, then cluster the residuals, and so on).
  • A beam-search (or brute-force for small M·2^B) code-assignment step that picks the one-hot codes minimizing reconstruction MSE.
  • An alternating optimization loop: codes via search, codebooks via gradient descent (plain numpy or JAX autodiff), that converges.
  • A comparison cell: scalar round-to-nearest vs. AQ with M=1 (pure vector quantization) vs. AQ with M=2/3/4 codebooks, at matched total bit budget.
  • Prints a table showing reconstruction MSE strictly decreasing as M increases at fixed bit budget, plus one plot of MSE vs. number of codebooks M.
