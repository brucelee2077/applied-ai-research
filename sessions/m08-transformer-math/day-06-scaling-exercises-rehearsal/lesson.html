What this assumes: Week 3's daily lessons on transformer arithmetic, QKV matrices, the KV cache, and pretraining logic. You've already met every idea used today. No new concepts — this day is about re-deriving what you know, out loud.

The problem: knowing a fact vs. being able to derive it

All week you learned individual pieces: how to count FLOPs, how the KV cache grows, why C≈6ND shows up in scaling laws. Each piece made sense in the moment, with the lesson guiding you. But there's a gap between "I followed that explanation" and "I can reproduce that derivation from a blank page, under pressure, while someone asks me follow-up questions."

That gap is exactly what a systems-focused interview at a frontier lab tests. Nobody hands you a calculator and asks for the final number. They ask: "Walk me through why weight-loading dominates decode latency" — and they expect you to build the argument live, step by step, the way you'd build it on a whiteboard.

The answer: a day with no new code

Today has two halves, both borrowed straight from the curriculum's own weekend structure:

  • Saturday half — Mathematical Verification. Sit with a blank page and the Scaling Book's own worked exercises for Chapters 1–4. Re-derive each one by hand: no code, no calculator shortcuts, every intermediate number written down.
  • Sunday half — Screencast Rehearsal. Take two of those derivations (C≈6ND and KV-cache sizing) and explain them out loud, in full sentences, as if a staff engineer were sitting across from you asking "why?" after every step.

So what's today's catch?

There is no new mechanism to learn — that's intentional. The catch is discipline: it is tempting to skim the exercises because "I already know this." Skimming is exactly what this day is built to catch. If you can't rebuild the derivation without looking at last week's notes, you don't own it yet.

4The rules of a verification-and-rehearsal day, and why it mattersHow & why

The rules

  • Solve on paper first. No calculator shortcuts, no code. Write every intermediate number — if a step feels "obvious," write it down anyway; that's the step an interviewer will probe.
  • Check against the Scaling Book's own worked answers. The book gives you the exercises and its own solutions — use them to verify, not to skip the derivation.
  • Then say it out loud. Writing the derivation and speaking it are different skills. Speaking forces you to sequence your reasoning into sentences a listener can follow in real time.
  • Rehearse under a simulated follow-up. After you explain a step, ask yourself "why?" one more time than feels natural. That's what an interviewer does.
✅
arithmetic intensity = FLOPs / bytes moved, compared to hardware ridge point = peak FLOPs/s ÷ peak bytes/s
"If my op's intensity is below the ridge point, I'm memory-bound — the chip is waiting on data, not compute." That single comparison is the seed of most systems-interview questions this week.

Why it's a superpower

  • Derivations transfer — once you can rebuild C≈6ND or KV-cache sizing from scratch, you can adapt it instantly when the interviewer changes a number (bigger model, longer context, different dtype).
  • Speaking under scrutiny is a distinct skill — many candidates can solve a problem silently but freeze when asked to narrate their reasoning live. Rehearsal fixes that before it costs you an interview.
  • It exposes the gaps code was hiding — a notebook cell that runs correctly can mask a step you don't actually understand. Paper forces you to write out every step, so gaps have nowhere to hide.

🪜
Math Ladder — training compute C ≈ 6·N·D
1 · In words: one token, in one training step, costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter — so total training compute is 6 × (parameters) × (tokens).
2 · The formula: C ≈ 6·N·D — N = parameters; D = training tokens; the 6 = 2 FLOPs per multiply-add × 3 (≈ 1 forward + 2 backward-equivalent passes).
3 · Tiny numbers: N = 7e9, D = 2e12 → C ≈ 6 × 7e9 × 2e12 = 8.4e22 FLOPs.
4 · Sanity check: forward alone is ≈ 2ND; if your "6" ever comes out "2", you dropped the backward pass — which costs about twice the forward.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Two things separate a rehearsed answer from a lucky one: the unit slip that flips a verdict, and how you spend limited prep time.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): comparing arithmetic intensity to the wrong ridge point. Intensity = FLOPs / bytes means nothing until you compare it to the hardware's ridge = peak FLOPs/s ÷ peak bytes/s. Mix the units — quote peak FLOPs without the matching bandwidth, or count fp32 bytes for a bf16 kernel — and your memory-bound-vs-compute-bound verdict flips with no error, so you optimize the wrong thing. In a live answer, a staff engineer states the ridge point explicitly and keeps the dtype consistent between the FLOP count and the byte count.
⚖️
Trade-off: depth vs. breadth in prep. You can re-derive a few core formulas until they are automatic, or skim many topics shallowly. Depth wins a systems interview — they change one number and watch you re-derive live; breadth wins a recall quiz. This weekend is deliberately depth: two derivations, spoken cold, over many topics half-remembered.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "C≈6ND: 2 FLOPs per multiply-add, times 3 for roughly one forward plus two backward-equivalent passes, per parameter per token — for a 7B model on 2T tokens that's about 8.4e22 FLOPs. The nuance is the '6' — forward alone is 2ND and backward costs about twice forward, so slipping to '2' or '4' is the classic error. And arithmetic intensity only tells you compute- vs memory-bound relative to the hardware's ridge point, with the same dtype used in the byte count."
