Transformer ArithmeticC ≈ 6ND

How much does it cost to train a large model? You don't need the source code — you need two numbers. With C ≈ 6ND you can estimate the training compute of GPT-3, or any model, on the back of a napkin. Today: what the formula means and where the 6 comes from.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: state the rule C ≈ 6ND, explain why it's 6 (2 forward + 4 backward FLOPs per parameter per token), plug in real numbers for GPT-3, and turn FLOPs into wall-clock time on an H100.
1What is C ≈ 6ND, and why does it exist?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you can multiply big numbers and you know a neural network is made of "weights" (also called parameters). No prior knowledge of FLOP counting needed.

The question: how expensive is it to train a model?

Training a large model can cost millions of dollars and weeks of GPU time. Before you start, you want an estimate: how much compute will this take? Compute is measured in FLOPs — a FLOP is one floating-point operation, meaning a single arithmetic step on decimal numbers, like one multiply or one add. Training a big model takes a huge number of these.

You could try to read all the model code and count every operation. That is slow and error-prone. There is a much simpler way.

The answer: two numbers and a small constant

The total training compute is well approximated by:

🧮
C ≈ 6 × N × D
C = total training compute in FLOPs · N = number of parameters (weights) in the model · D = number of training tokens (pieces of text the model reads during training).

That is the whole thing. If you know how big your model is (N) and how much data you train it on (D), multiply them together, times 6, and you have a solid estimate of the training cost.

What does "per parameter per token" mean?

Here is the key idea. During training, every parameter does a fixed amount of arithmetic for every token the model reads. So the total work grows with parameters times tokens. The 6 is that fixed amount of work — roughly 6 FLOPs each time one parameter meets one token. We'll unpack where the 6 comes from in the next sections.

😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 很多人记得住 6ND 这个公式，却说不清那个 6 是从哪来的，于是设定一变就慌。In plain terms: people memorize "C ≈ 6ND" but can't say why it's 6, so the moment the setup changes — longer context, activation recomputation, low utilization — they can't tell whether the estimate still holds.

2Every worker shakes every guest's handIntuition

Picture a big party. The workers are the parameters (there are N of them). The guests are the tokens (there are D of them). Training is every worker greeting every guest with a fixed little routine.

🤝
Forward pass = 2 ops

When a worker meets a guest, they do one multiply and one add — that's 2 FLOPs. This is the model making a prediction: weight × input + running total.

🔁
Backward pass = about 4 ops

Then the worker figures out how to improve, which is roughly twice the greeting work — about 4 FLOPs. Learning costs more than predicting.

In one line: each of the N workers greets each of the D guests, doing 2 + 4 = 6 FLOPs each time. Total work = 6 × N × D.

直觉 / Intuition: 训练之所以贵，是因为「每个参数都要为每个 token 干一点固定的活」，所以总量 = 参数数 × token 数 × 每次的固定量(6)。In systems terms: N is the total size of your weight matrices and D is how many tokens flow through them; one training step is a forward pass plus a backward pass over a batch, and 6ND just totals that fixed per-touch work across the whole run.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: workers don't literally meet guests one at a time. The 6 is an average that folds in the forward and backward passes; the real per-layer math varies, but 6ND is an excellent estimate for the dense parts of a Transformer.

Where the 6 comes from — and why inference is 3× cheaper per (param·token): 6 FLOPs = 2 fwd + 4 bwd → C ≈ 6ND

Learning question: the rule is C ≈ 6ND — where does the 6 come from, and what happens to the bill if you only run the model forward (inference) instead of training it?

👀
What you should notice: the 6 is not magic — it is 2 (forward) + 4 (backward) FLOPs stacked, per parameter per token. Flip to inference and the backward block disappears: only the forward 2ND is left, so serving a model costs about one-third of training it. The right-hand bill scales with both N and D — double either and it doubles.
⚠️
Common misconception: the 6 is a floor, not the whole bill. It counts only dense parameter·token FLOPs — it ignores attention's O(seq²) term (which grows with context length), activation recomputation, and sub-100% hardware use (MFU). A real run always costs more than 6ND.

How to read it: the toggle switches between forward-only (inference) and forward + backward (training) accounting; the sliders set model size and token count. Effective throughput assumed: ~1e15 FLOP/s — an idealized teaching estimate (one H100's bf16 peak, rounded for clean hand-arithmetic). The interactive calculator in Section 5 uses a realistic effective throughput of 989 TFLOP/s × 40% MFU ≈ 3.96×10¹⁴ FLOP/s instead, so its wall-clock time runs ~2.5× longer. Where this simplifies reality: the backward pass being ~2× the forward is an average over the dense layers, and the H100 time here ignores the O(seq²) attention term.

4Where the 6 comes from, and why the rule holdsHow & why

Breaking down the 6

  • Forward pass = 2 FLOPs per parameter per token. Using a weight means one multiply (weight × input) and one add (into a running sum). That is 2 FLOPs, and it happens once per parameter for each token.
  • Backward pass ≈ 4 FLOPs per parameter per token. Backpropagation — the step that computes how to change each weight — does roughly twice the forward work. It needs one pass to send the error back to the inputs and one to compute the gradient for each weight. That is about 2 × 2 = 4 FLOPs.
  • Add them up: 2 (forward) + 4 (backward) = 6 FLOPs per parameter per token.
✅
C ≈ (2 + 4) × N × D = 6ND
Forward is 2ND. Backward is about 4ND. Training does both for every token, so total training compute is about 6ND.
🪜
Math Ladder — C ≈ 6ND
1 · In words: training compute is a fixed ~6 FLOPs of work for every (parameter, token) pair, summed over the whole run.
2 · The formula: C ≈ 6·N·D — C = total training compute in FLOPs; N = parameters; D = training tokens; the 6 = 2 (forward) + 4 (backward) FLOPs per parameter per token.
3 · Tiny numbers: a 10M-param toy run on 1B tokens → C = 6 × 1e7 × 1e9 = 6e16 FLOPs. Assuming the idealized teaching estimate of ~1e15 FLOP/s on one H100, that is 6e16 / 1e15 ≈ 60 seconds.
4 · Sanity check: GPT-3 (N=175e9, D=300e9) → 6 × 175e9 × 300e9 ≈ 3.15e23 FLOPs, which matches the published estimate. If your number is off by orders of magnitude, check you used parameters (not bytes) and tokens (not sequences).

Why this rule is so useful

  • You only need two numbers. Model size N and token count D are usually the first things you know. No architecture details required.
  • It scales linearly. Double the tokens D and compute doubles. Double the parameters N and compute doubles. This makes budgeting and planning easy.
  • It powers scaling laws. Later work (like Chinchilla) uses 6ND to decide the best split between model size and data for a fixed compute budget.
🔗
Causal chain: each weight does a multiply + add per token (2 FLOPs forward) → backprop roughly doubles that (4 FLOPs backward) → 6 FLOPs per parameter per token → total training compute C ≈ 6ND.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The rule is an accounting identity, which is exactly why it fools people. A staff engineer knows what it quietly leaves out, and what question it cannot answer at all.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): trusting 6ND as the whole bill. It never errors — it's just arithmetic — so a GPU-hour budget built on it looks authoritative. But 6ND counts only the dense parameter-token FLOPs. It ignores attention's O(seq²) term, the extra forward passes from activation recomputation (gradient checkpointing), and the fact that real hardware rarely exceeds ~50% utilization (MFU). Your run comes in over budget with no bug to find. A senior engineer treats 6ND as a floor, not the total.
⚖️
Trade-off: 6ND prices a config, it doesn't choose one. The formula treats N and D as interchangeable multipliers, so it tells you the cost of a given (model size, tokens) pair but says nothing about which split of a fixed budget gives the lowest loss. That compute-optimal N/D question is the design-review decision — and it's exactly what Chinchilla-style scaling laws exist to answer.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Training compute is about 6ND: 6 FLOPs per parameter per token — 2 forward, 4 backward — times N parameters times D tokens. For GPT-3 that's 6 × 175B × 300B ≈ 3×10²³ FLOPs. The nuance that separates a real answer: it's a floor, not the whole bill — it ignores attention's O(seq²) term, activation recomputation, and sub-100% hardware utilization (MFU), so a real run always costs more."
