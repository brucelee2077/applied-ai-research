Writing the Methodology in LaTeX

You stop coding today and start writing science. Your job: draft the Methodology section of the report that documents exactly how you derived the Dense-model Chinchilla scaling law for your addition transformer — using LaTeX, the typesetting language academic ML papers are written in.

1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is LaTeX, and what is a Methodology section?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Weeks 9–11 (you trained the addition transformer, ran the IsoFLOP grid, fit the parabola-then-power-law scaling curve). No prior LaTeX experience needed.

The problem: your experiment lives only in your head and your code

Over Weeks 9–11 you made dozens of small choices: how many tokens in your vocabulary, how you padded sequences, which optimizer and schedule you used, which parameter-count range you swept, and a two-stage curve-fitting procedure. Right now, all of that lives in scattered notebooks and your memory. If a labmate — or you, in six months — tried to redo your experiment, could they get your exact numbers back? Today you write that down, precisely, for the first time.

The answer: LaTeX

LaTeX (pronounced "lay-tek" or "lah-tek") is a markup-based typesetting system. You don't format text by clicking "bold" or dragging margins like in Word. You write plain-text commands — \section{Methodology}, $L(N,D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$ — and a compiler turns that source into a professionally typeset PDF.

It exists because academic papers need two things Word is bad at:

  • Beautiful, unambiguous math notation — fractions, Greek letters, subscripts nested inside superscripts, all rendered exactly the same way every time.
  • Exact reproducibility — a .tex file is plain text. It diffs cleanly in git, compiles identically on any machine, and never suffers "it looked fine until I opened it on your laptop" formatting drift.

Nearly every arXiv paper you've read this program — Kaplan et al., Chinchilla, Switch Transformers — was written in LaTeX. Today you write the same genre of document, about your own experiment.

The specific artifact: a Methodology section

A Methodology section answers one question: "if a stranger wanted to redo exactly what you did, what would they need to know?" It is not a results section (no numbers, no conclusions), not an abstract, not an introduction motivating the problem. It is a precise, procedural account: what model you built, what data you generated, what grid you ran, and what fitting procedure you used to pull out the scaling-law exponents.

⚠️
The most common mistake: smuggling a result into the Methodology, e.g. "we found alpha ≈ 0.35, showing the model is data-hungry." That sentence does two jobs — state a number, interpret it — and both belong to a later Results section. Methodology states the procedure that would produce a number, never the number itself.

2 · INTUITIONA recipe card for a dish you invented✓

Writing a Methodology section in LaTeX is like writing a recipe card for a dish you invented, using a strict template printed by a cooking magazine.

💬
Slack message = vague prose

"Then you cook the sauce for a while until it's ready." Fine for a friend, useless for someone in a different kitchen who needs to get the exact same dish.

📋
Recipe card = the Methodology

"Simmer at low heat for 12 minutes, stirring every 2 minutes." Exact measurements, exact steps — someone else can reproduce your dish without ever watching you cook.

In one line: the magazine's template (LaTeX's \section, \subsection, equation environments) forces consistent structure so every recipe looks and reads the same way, and your equations are the measurements — exact, never "a pinch of."

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a recipe card doesn't need to justify its choices (why 12 minutes and not 10). A Methodology section does — one sentence per major design decision explaining why (why FLOPs 1M–15M, why parabola-then-power-law rather than one joint fit) — because reviewers, unlike diners, will ask "why not do it differently?"

4 · HOW & WHYWhat belongs in Methodology, and why LaTeX for it✓

The rules

  • Every experimental choice that could change the answer belongs here: vocabulary size, sequence-length padding scheme, FLOP-budget range, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and the exact two-stage fitting procedure.
  • State choices as facts and one-line justifications, not narrative: "we trained at eight parameter counts N" plus one sentence on why that range, not a story about how the week went.
  • Every symbol you introduce in an equation (N, D, C, E, A, B, α, β) must be defined in the surrounding prose the first time it appears — a reviewer should never have to guess what a letter means.
  • Zero numbers from your actual runs. No fitted α, no accuracy, no "we found." Those live in Results, written later.
✅
\label{eq:chinchilla} on your key equation, \label{sec:methodology} on your section
Add labels the first time you write an equation or table — you'll need \ref{} or \eqref{} to point back to it from Results and Discussion in Week 12. Adding labels retroactively across a growing multi-file report is far more error-prone than adding them now.

Why LaTeX specifically

  • Precision — \frac{A}{N^\alpha} renders identically every time; there's no "which font did Word use for the Greek letter" ambiguity.
  • Reproducibility — a .tex file is plain text: it diffs cleanly in git, and compiles the same PDF on any machine, any year.
  • Composability — \label{} + \ref{} let a 12-page, multi-file report cross-reference itself correctly, even as you add sections in Week 12.

7 · PRODUCEWrite the Methodology section — by hand, in LaTeX✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): write the Methodology section of your Weeks 9–11 report directly in LaTeX, and compile it to a clean PDF. This is a hands-on writing exercise — no code to run, no experiment to design. You write the .tex file yourself.

Acceptance criteria

  • Deliverable: sessions/m13-isoflops-scaling-law/day-06-scientific-documentation/reports/week11_scaling_laws/methodology.tex, compiling with no errors via pdflatex methodology.tex (or Overleaf), producing methodology.pdf in the same directory.
  • A top-level \section{Methodology} containing at minimum these \subsections: Model Architecture, Data Generation, Training Procedure, IsoFLOP Experimental Design, Curve Fitting (state Stage 1: per-budget parabolas, and Stage 2: power law, as two distinct steps).
  • At least two labeled equations — the compute approximation C \approx 6ND and the three-term loss law L(N,D) = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta — each with every symbol (N, D, C, E, A, B, α, β) defined in prose the first time it appears.
  • At least one table environment (for example, architecture hyperparameters) using booktabs-style rules (\toprule, \midrule, \bottomrule).
  • Zero numerical results or conclusions — no fitted α/β, no accuracy numbers. Those belong to a later Results section.
  • Every experimental choice stated precisely enough that a reader who never saw Weeks 9–11 could redo the experiment from this section alone: the vocabulary of 13 tokens, fixed-length padding, the parameter range N from 1M to 15M, the optimizer and learning-rate schedule, and the number of IsoFLOP grid cells.

How to do it

  1. Create the folder sessions/m13-isoflops-scaling-law/day-06-scientific-documentation/reports/week11_scaling_laws/ at the project root and a new file methodology.tex inside it.
  2. Start from a minimal preamble: \documentclass{article}, \usepackage{amsmath} (for equations), \usepackage{booktabs} (for the table).
  3. Write each required subsection as its own paragraph or two — state the choice, then one sentence on why, using the exact numbers from Weeks 9–11 (13-token vocabulary, fixed-length padding, N from 1M–15M, your optimizer/schedule, and how many (N, D) grid cells your IsoFLOP sweep used).
  4. Add the two labeled equations with \label{eq:compute} and \label{eq:chinchilla}, defining every symbol in the sentence right after each equation.
  5. Add one table (e.g. architecture hyperparameters: vocabulary size, sequence length, layer count, hidden size) using \begin{tabular}{@{}...@{}} with \toprule/\midrule/\bottomrule from booktabs.
  6. Compile with pdflatex methodology.tex from inside sessions/m13-isoflops-scaling-law/day-06-scientific-documentation/reports/week11_scaling_laws/ and confirm methodology.pdf is produced with no errors.
⚠️
Before you consider it done, re-read your own draft and delete any sentence that states a number you actually measured (a fitted exponent, an accuracy, a loss value). If you can't tell from a sentence alone whether it's a design choice or a result, it's probably a result — move it out.
