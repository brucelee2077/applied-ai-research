Your Own IsoFlops Scaling Law · Week 10 · Day 4

IsoFLOPsExperiment Design

Chinchilla's whole paper boils down to one question: for a fixed compute budget, what is the best split between model size and training data? Today you design the exact grid of runs that answers that question — before you train a single model.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain why fixing a FLOP budget C and a model size N forces the token count D, design a ladder of FLOP budgets and model sizes, compute the required D for every cell, and write out the (N, D) matrix your capstone will actually train next session.
1What is an IsoFLOPs experiment, and why does it exist?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you know your capstone trains a small transformer to do addition, and you've heard the word "scaling law" (bigger models / more data change loss in predictable ways). No prior IsoFLOPs experience needed — every term below is defined the first time it shows up.

The question nobody can answer by guessing

Say you have a fixed amount of compute — a fixed number of FLOPs (floating point operations, the basic unit of "how much math a computer does"). You can spend that compute on a big model trained on little data, or a small model trained on lots of data, or anything in between. Which split gives the lowest training loss?

You can't just test every possible combination — that's infinite. IsoFLOPs experiment design is a method for answering this question with a small, careful set of training runs instead of an infinite grid.

Where the name comes from

"Iso" means "equal" — you've maybe seen it in "isotherm," the lines on a weather map connecting points of equal temperature. An IsoFLOP curve is the set of all training runs that use the same total compute (same FLOPs), even though each run splits that compute differently between model size and data.

Why fixing the budget also fixes the trade-off

For a dense transformer, the total training compute is well approximated by one formula:

🧮
C ≈ 6 · N · D
C = total training FLOPs, N = number of parameters, D = number of training tokens. The 6 comes from roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter per token on the forward pass, and roughly 4 more on the backward pass.

Look closely at that formula: once you pick a compute budget C and a model size N, the number of training tokens D is no longer a free choice — it's D = C / (6N). So "sweep model size at a fixed budget" and "sweep the model-size-vs-data trade-off at a fixed budget" are the same sweep. That's the whole trick.

Why this matters: the mistake it caught

This exact method — called Chinchilla's "Approach 2" — found that GPT-3-era language models were trained with too many parameters and too little data for their compute budget. Today you design the same kind of experiment, shrunk down to your capstone's scale (1M–15M parameters instead of billions), so you can find your own answer instead of trusting someone else's.

Today is a design day, not a training day. You will not train any models today. You are writing the spec — the exact table of runs — that next session's training script will consume.

2Best car for a fixed tank of gasIntuition

Imagine testing "what's the best car for a fixed amount of gasoline." You don't get to try every car with every amount of gas — that's infinite. Instead you fix the tank size first, then compare cars within that one fixed budget.

⛽🚚
Big truck, same 10-gallon tank

A huge vehicle. Given exactly 10 gallons, it drives a short distance — heavy engine, short range. In transformer terms: big N, small D (few tokens).

⛽🚲
Tiny car, same 10-gallon tank

A small, efficient vehicle. Given the same 10 gallons, it can go very far — but it's not powerful. In transformer terms: small N, huge D (lots of tokens).

In one line: fix the gas tank (compute budget), test several vehicle sizes (model sizes) against it, and look for the sweet-spot size that goes farthest (lowest loss) on that exact tank. Then repeat with a 20-gallon tank, a 40-gallon tank, and so on — and plot how the sweet-spot vehicle size grows as the tank grows.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: gas-to-distance for a real car follows one clean physical rule (bigger engine, more weight, less range). Loss-vs-(N, D) for a transformer is a noisy result you only see after actually training — there's no clean formula to solve on paper. That's exactly why you need many real runs and a curve fit, not algebra.

4The IsoFLOP protocol, and why it mattersHow & why

The protocol (Chinchilla Section 3.2, "Approach 2")

  • Pick a ladder of FLOP budgets — Chinchilla used 9 budgets, log-spaced across three orders of magnitude (6×10¹⁸ to 3×10²¹).
  • For each budget, train several model sizes N — each for exactly D = C/(6N) tokens, so every run in that budget "row" costs the same total compute.
  • Plot final loss vs. N for each budget. This traces a U-shaped curve — an IsoFLOP curve, or "isoFLOP slice." Too-small N: not enough capacity, loss stays high even with abundant data. Too-large N: not enough data to train it, loss stays high even though the model is "good." There's a valley in the middle.
  • Fit a parabola to each curve to find its minimum precisely — this gives the loss-optimal (N*, D*) at that specific budget.
  • Fit power laws across the (C, N*) and (C, D*) pairs — one log-log linear regression each. Chinchilla found N_opt ∝ C^0.49 and D_opt ∝ C^0.51 — almost exactly 0.5/0.5, meaning model size and data should scale equally as compute grows.
✅
D = C / (6N)
The one line that turns "pick a budget and a model size" into "the token count is decided for you." Every cell in today's matrix is built from this formula.

                                                                          ▼
                                                                N_opt ∝ C^a   (and D_opt ∝ C^b)

Why it matters: the mistake it caught

  • Kaplan et al. (2020) used a different (flawed) methodology and found a ≈ 0.73, b ≈ 0.27 — heavily favoring bigger models over more data.
  • Chinchilla's IsoFLOP method corrected this — models like GPT-3 and Gopher turned out to be undertrained: too many parameters, not enough tokens, for their compute budget.
  • Controlled-experiment principle — holding total compute fixed within each row means the loss differences you observe are caused only by the N/D trade-off, not by one run simply getting more total compute than another.
🔗
Causal chain: C ≈ 6ND (Section 1) → fixing C and N forces D → sweeping N at fixed C sweeps the N/D trade-off → the loss valley reveals the optimal split → fitting across budgets gives you N_opt(C) and D_opt(C) as power laws.

7Design your IsoFLOPs training matrixProduce

Today's task (from the curriculum): design — but do not yet run — the exact matrix of FLOP budgets × model sizes that your capstone will train next session to fit N_opt(C) and D_opt(C) power laws.

Acceptance criteria

  • Define a FLOP budget ladder (log-spaced, at least 5 values) as constants.
  • Define an N ladder strictly within [1,000,000, 15,000,000] parameters (at least 4-5 log-spaced values), each mapped to a concrete Flax transformer config (d_model, n_layers, n_heads, d_ff) — with a helper that computes actual parameter count and asserts it lands within ±10% of the target N.
  • Compute D = C / (6N) for every (C, N) cell.
  • Filter out degenerate cells (D too small to train meaningfully, or too many steps for a reasonable run time), printing which cells were dropped and why.
  • Emit the final matrix (print + CSV/JSON) with columns: flop_budget, target_N, actual_N, architecture config, D_tokens, estimated_train_steps.
  • Print a per-budget summary confirming at least 3 surviving N values bracket a plausible valley.

⚠️
Gotcha: the 6ND formula only counts matmul FLOPs — it ignores embedding lookups, attention's O(seq_len²) term, and optimizer/recompute overhead. At 1M-15M parameters these ignored terms are a much bigger fraction of total FLOPs than they were for Chinchilla's multi-billion-parameter models. Decide up front whether your "FLOP budget" means the theoretical 6ND number or a profiler-measured count, and write that choice into the design doc — switching definitions mid-experiment silently breaks the "equal compute" comparison.
