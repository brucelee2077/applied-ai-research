Your Own IsoFlops Scaling Law · Week 11 · Day 1

Plottingthe Parabolas

Over the weekend you trained a whole matrix of small addition-transformers, sweeping both parameter count N and token budget D, and you saved every run's final loss. Today you group those runs by compute budget and fit a parabola to each group with scipy.optimize.curve_fit — this is Chinchilla's "Approach 2," and it hands you the exact compute-optimal model size for each budget.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: group your Week 10 training runs into IsoFLOP slices, fit a·log10(N)² + b·log10(N) + c to each slice with curve_fit, find each slice's vertex analytically, and produce a clean (C, N*, D*, L_min) results table for tomorrow's power-law fit.
1What is an IsoFLOP slice, and what is curve_fit?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Week 10's training matrix — you trained many small addition-transformers, sweeping parameter count N (roughly 1M–15M) and token budget D, and saved every run's compute cost C and final loss L. No prior SciPy or curve-fitting experience needed.

What you already have

Saturday you ran the matrix. Sunday you aggregated it into one clean table of rows: (N, D, C, L). Here, N is the parameter count, D is the number of training tokens, C is the total training compute in FLOPs (floating-point operations) — approximately C = 6 * N * D for a dense transformer's forward-plus-backward pass — and L is the final training loss.

The new idea: an IsoFLOP slice

An IsoFLOP slice is a group of runs that all spent the same total compute C, but reached it with different (N, D) splits. Example: a big model trained on few tokens, and a small model trained on many tokens, both burning exactly 6×10¹⁸ FLOPs. "Iso" means "same" — same FLOPs, different shape.

Chinchilla's finding (Hoffmann et al. 2022): if you plot final loss against log(N) for one fixed C, you get a U-shaped curve, not a straight line down. Too few parameters, and the model is too weak to use even unlimited tokens well. Too many parameters, and — at fixed compute — you starve it of tokens, so it undertrains. Somewhere in between is a genuine minimum: the compute-optimal N for that exact FLOP budget.

Today's tool: curve_fit

scipy.optimize.curve_fit is SciPy's general-purpose curve fitter. You give it three things: (1) a function f(x, *params) describing the shape you believe your data follows, (2) your x data, (3) your y data. It hands back popt — the best-fit values for those params — and pcov, a covariance matrix (useful later for error bars).

Today's f is nothing exotic — it's a plain second-degree polynomial, a parabola: f(x, a, b, c) = a·x² + b·x + c. You fit about nine of these tiny 3-parameter parabolas, one per FLOP budget, and from each one's vertex (its lowest point) you read off exactly one number: the loss-optimal N for that compute level.

📚
Official docs (optional): Matplotlib · Seaborn · scipy.optimize.curve_fit · Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022)
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: parabola 是最"看得见"的东西，可这一天却只给你数字、不给你图 — a parabola is the most visual object there is, yet the concept is usually taught with numbers, not a picture. In plain terms: without seeing the U and its vertex, people can run curve_fit but can't tell a good fit from a flat, failed one.
2Same dough, different pansIntuition

Think of one IsoFLOP slice like baking the same amount of dough (fixed compute budget) in different pan shapes (different N/D splits), then tasting which shape browns best.

🥘
huge shallow pan = big N, few tokens

The dough spreads too thin and never develops flavor. A big model trained on too few tokens — undertrained, no matter how big it is.

🍮
tiny deep pan = small N, tons of tokens

It stays gooey in the middle no matter how long you bake it. A small model can't absorb all that data — it's just too weak to use the tokens.

In one line: somewhere between those two extremes is a pan shape that bakes best for that exact amount of dough — and you find it by baking six or seven test pans (six or seven values of N), tasting each (measuring loss), and fitting a smooth curve through the results.

curve_fit is you taking that scatter of taste scores and drawing a parabola through it with a pencil, so you can point exactly at the bottom of the U — even though you never actually baked a pan at that exact precise width.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: dough doesn't come with a convexity guarantee. Your real loss curves might be noisy, slightly asymmetric, or under-sampled near the true minimum, so the "U-shape" you fit is a mathematical idealization of data a baker would call "pretty U-shaped, mostly." The parabola is a good local approximation near the minimum — not a claim that loss is quadratic in log(N) everywhere.

直觉 / Intuition: 关键动作是"取对数再拟合"，raw N 挤成一团，log N 才是干净的碗 — the key move is "log first, then fit": raw N crushes the points together, log N turns them into a clean bowl. The technical point: the parabola's vertex is only trustworthy when your sampled sizes actually straddle the bottom.

What you should notice: on the left (raw N) the points pile up near zero and the bowl is invisible; on the right (log N) they spread into a clean U. ⚠️
Misconception: a vertex is not a measurement. curve_fit returns N* = 10−b/2a even when that point lies past every model you trained — a clean-looking number with zero evidence under it. Only a vertex inside the sampled window is real.

How to read it: a least-squares parabola L = a·x² + b·x + c is fit to the 5 windowed points (x = log₁₀ N); the vertex is x* = −b/(2a). Simplified: the points sit on one clean synthetic bowl with tiny fixed noise, so the fit is stable — real slices are noisier and can need more runs near the minimum.

4The rules of the fit, and why it mattersHow & why

The rules

  • Group runs by (rounded) target FLOP budget C first — always. Never fit a parabola across different budgets; that throws away the IsoFLOP structure entirely (more on this in the gotcha below).
  • Within one slice, transform the x-axis: fit against logN = np.log10(N), not raw N. N spans orders of magnitude, so raw-space loss curves are skewed and hard to fit; log-space curves are a clean, symmetric bowl.
  • curve_fit(parabola, logN, L) returns popt = [a, b, c] — the best-fit curvature, slope, and intercept — and pcov, whose diagonal gives rough parameter uncertainty.
  • The vertex — the parabola's lowest point — sits where the derivative 2a·x + b is zero: logN* = -b / (2a). Exponentiate to get back an actual parameter count: N* = 10 ** logN*.
✅
D_star = C / (6 * N_star)
Once you know the compute-optimal N* for this budget, the same C ≈ 6ND approximation used to design the matrix hands you the matching optimal token budget D* for free.
🪜
Math Ladder — the parabola vertex
1 · In words: within one budget, loss vs. log(model size) is a bowl; the bottom is the best size. A parabola is the simplest bowl, so fit one and take its lowest point.
2 · The formula: fit L ≈ a·x² + b·x + c with x = log₁₀(N); the vertex (where the slope 2a·x + b = 0) is x* = −b/(2a), then N* = 10x* and D* = C/(6N*). a = curvature (must be > 0); b, c = fit constants.
3 · Tiny numbers: a fit gives a = 0.20, b = −2.8. Then x* = −(−2.8)/(2·0.20) = 7.0, so N* = 107 = 10M params.
4 · Sanity check: a > 0 (a smiling U, not a frown) and x* lands inside your sampled [min log N, max log N]. If a ≤ 0 or x* is outside the window, the vertex is extrapolated — flag it, don't trust it.

Why it matters

  • One number per budget — instead of six or nine noisy dots, each FLOP budget collapses to a clean triple: (C, N*, L_min). That table is exactly what tomorrow's cross-budget power-law fit needs as input.
  • Sign check is a built-in sanity test — a must come out positive (a smiling U). If it's negative or near zero, this slice's sampled N values didn't actually bracket a real minimum, and you should flag it, not trust its vertex.
  • Composable — this is the same curve_fit you'll reuse Tuesday for the harder two-variable Chinchilla loss surface, just with a friendlier 3-parameter shape today.
🔗
Causal chain: Week 10's training matrix → group by fixed compute C → per-slice loss-vs-log(N) is genuinely U-shaped → fit a parabola per slice → read off the vertex → (C, N*, D*, L_min) table → Tuesday's power-law fit across all budgets.
⚠️
Common mistake: pooling every run together and fitting one giant parabola against log(N) regardless of compute budget. That throws away the whole IsoFLOP structure — you'd just see that bigger overall runs (more N and more D at once) tend to have lower loss, a mostly-monotonic trend, not the intended U-shaped valley. Always groupby the fixed budget first, one parabola per group.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The fit always returns three numbers and a vertex, so it never crashes even when the vertex is nonsense — that gap between "ran fine" and "answered correctly" is exactly what a staff engineer guards.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): the vertex sits outside your sampled range. curve_fit happily returns logN* = -b/(2a) even when that minimum lies past your smallest or largest sampled N. The formula just extrapolates the parabola beyond the data, so you get a clean-looking N* that was never bracketed by a real run. Your compute-optimal frontier then bends toward a point you have zero evidence for, and nothing errors. In code review, a senior engineer checks that every reported logN* falls inside [min(logN), max(logN)] for its slice — a vertex outside the sampled window is flagged and re-sampled, not trusted.
⚖️
Trade-off: a rigid parabola vs. a flexible fit. A 3-parameter parabola is stable — it fits reliably from just six noisy points and gives a closed-form vertex. But it assumes loss is truly quadratic in log(N), which only holds locally near the bottom of the bowl. If your slice is asymmetric or the true curve is flatter on one side, the parabola pulls the vertex off the real minimum. In a design review you decide: keep the parabola for its stability with sparse data, or spend more runs near the minimum (or use a richer curve) to buy accuracy you can only afford with a denser sweep.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Per compute budget I fit a parabola to loss vs. log(N) and read the vertex x* = −b/(2a), giving that budget's compute-optimal N* = 10x* and D* = C/6N*; those (C, N*) points fit a cross-budget power law next. The nuance is that curve_fit returns a vertex even when it's extrapolated past your sampled sizes or when a ≤ 0, so I assert a > 0 and x* inside the sampled window — and I always groupby fixed compute first, never pool across budgets."
