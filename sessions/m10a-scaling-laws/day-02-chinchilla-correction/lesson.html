Scaling Laws · Week 5 · Day 2

The Chinchilla CorrectionFixing Kaplan's Compute-Optimal Recipe

Last week you saw loss fall as a power law in compute, params, and data — each on its own. Today: what happens when the 2020 scaling-law fit that told the field to build huge, undertrained models turns out to have a hidden bug, and how a 2022 DeepMind paper caught it.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: state the compute identity C ≈ 6ND, explain why Kaplan's fit favored model size over data, explain how Chinchilla's three independent methods found the opposite (N and D should scale equally), and reproduce the Gopher-vs-Chinchilla head-to-head with real numbers.
1What does "compute-optimal" even mean?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: last week's scaling-laws basics — loss falls as a power law when you scale compute, params, or data one at a time. No prior Chinchilla knowledge needed.

The problem: you can't grow N and D for free

Training a model costs compute — measured in FLOPs (floating point operations). That compute is roughly fixed by your budget C. There is a simple identity connecting it to the two things you control:

🧮
C ≈ 6 · N · D
N = number of parameters (model size). D = number of training tokens (dataset size). The 6 comes from roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter per token going forward, plus 4 more going backward during backprop.

This means N and D are in tension: if your compute budget C is fixed and you make the model bigger (N up), you are forced to train on fewer tokens (D down), and vice versa. A scaling law here is an empirical formula — fit by actually training many models — that tells you the best split of a fixed C between N and D to get the lowest possible loss. That best split is what "compute-optimal" means.

The bug: Kaplan et al. (2020) got the split wrong

OpenAI's Kaplan et al. paper (2020) fit this trade-off first and concluded model size should grow much faster than data as compute grows: N ∝ C^0.73, D ∝ C^0.27. That recipe is exactly what produced GPT-3: 175 billion parameters, but only about 300 billion training tokens — under 2 tokens per parameter. The field took this as gospel and built a generation of huge, data-starved models.

The fix: Chinchilla (2022)

DeepMind's Hoffmann et al. paper (2022) — the Chinchilla paper — reran the experiment far more carefully, training over 400 models from 70 million to 16 billion parameters, using three independent methods. All three agreed: N and D should scale equally (N ∝ C^0.5, D ∝ C^0.5), not 0.73/0.27. Every large model trained on the Kaplan recipe — GPT-3, Gopher, MT-NLG, Jurassic-1 — had too many parameters and too little data for its compute budget.

📚
Official links (optional): Kaplan et al. 2020 (arXiv:2001.08361) · Hoffmann et al. 2022, Chinchilla (arXiv:2203.15556) · Feinberg, Princeton talk on scaling laws (PDF)
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 两个 scaling law 看起来都对、都是干净的直线，谁错了并不显然 — both fits look clean and authoritative, so it's not obvious one is wrong. In plain terms: the disagreement isn't about the math (both split obey a+b=1) — it's about the experiment, and people miss that a biased training recipe can produce a confident, wrong curve.
2A restaurant's grocery budgetIntuition

Think of training compute as a fixed grocery budget split between kitchen size (model parameters N, your cooking capacity) and ingredients (training tokens D, what you actually cook with).

直觉 / Intuition: 固定预算下，把模型放大一点，就必须少喂一点数据 — at fixed compute, every extra parameter is paid for with fewer tokens. The technical point: because C ≈ 6ND is fixed, loss as a function of model size is a U — too small starves capacity, too big starves data — and the bottom of that U is the compute-optimal split.

🏰🍽️
Kaplan's advice: mostly kitchen

Whenever the budget grows, spend almost all of it on a bigger kitchen and only a little on ingredients. Restaurants built huge kitchens (GPT-3's 175B parameters) but stocked few ingredients (300B tokens) — a mansion kitchen with a dinner party's worth of food.

⚖️🥘
Chinchilla's correction: split evenly

The food critic reran the taste tests across far more restaurants and found the real rule: split every extra dollar evenly between kitchen and ingredients. Smaller kitchen, way more food — and the food came out better.

In one line: DeepMind rebuilt Gopher's setup at the even-split ratio — a smaller kitchen (70B parameters) but 4x the ingredients (1.4T tokens) — and it beat the original, while being cheaper to serve every day afterward, since serving cost tracks kitchen size (N), not how many ingredients you trained with (D).

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a too-small real kitchen physically cannot produce more food no matter the ingredients. But the loss landscape has no hard wall — it's a smooth, continuous trade-off. The "right" ratio is a matter of degree fit from data, not a fixed size limit.

What you should notice: drag model size right (Kaplan's instinct) and loss rises — the model is huge but starved of tokens; drag left past the valley and loss rises again — now it's over-fed and under-sized. The lowest loss sits at an interior point, and moving there shrinks the blue N bar while the orange D bar grows. That's "smaller kitchen, more food."
⚠️
Misconception: Kaplan and Chinchilla do not disagree about the math — both splits obey C ≈ 6ND. They disagree about where the valley is, because Kaplan's fitting recipe (one schedule length for all sizes) put small models below their true floor and slid the apparent minimum too far right.

How to read it: the curve is the Chinchilla parametric loss L = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ with D = C/6N at one fixed C. Simplified: one clean budget and the paper's approach-3 constants — real IsoFLOP profiles use many noisy runs per budget, and the exact tokens-per-parameter at the valley shifts with scale (see the §7 gotcha; the robust finding is the near-equal exponents, not a fixed "20×").

4Where Kaplan's fit went wrong, and how Chinchilla fixed itHow & why

The rules

  • A power-law loss model per axis: at infinite data, L(N) − L∞ = A/N^a; at infinite params, L(D) − L∞ = B/D^b. Both describe how loss falls as you scale one axis while the other is not the bottleneck.
  • Kaplan's method fit N and D scaling separately, each optimized at a fixed training step count, using early-stopped training curves.
  • The flaw: a fixed learning-rate schedule length across all model sizes biases the fit. It makes small models look artificially undertrainable relative to their true optimum, which skews the whole curve toward "always make the model bigger."
  • Consequence: Kaplan's fit produced N ∝ C^0.73, D ∝ C^0.27 — favoring parameter growth almost 3x over data growth.
✅
Chinchilla ran three independent methods and they agreed:
(a) fix model sizes, vary tokens, find the loss-minimizing token count per size; (b) IsoFLOP profiles — fix a compute budget, train many (N,D) pairs at that same C, plot loss vs. N, find the minimum; (c) fit one parametric loss surface L(N,D) to all 400+ runs at once. All three gave exponents near 0.5/0.5, not 0.73/0.27.
🪜
Math Ladder — why there is an interior optimum
1 · In words: at a fixed budget, making the model bigger cuts one error term (more capacity) but starves data and grows another (less to learn from); the best size is where the two pulls balance — the bottom of a U.
2 · The formula: L(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ, with D = C/(6N) fixed by the budget. E = irreducible loss; A/Nα = capacity term (falls as N grows); B/Dβ = data term (rises as N grows, because D shrinks). Chinchilla fit α ≈ 0.34, β ≈ 0.28.
3 · Tiny numbers: hold C fixed. Tiny N → huge A/Nα; huge N → few tokens → huge B/Dβ; in between the sum dips. The three methods put that dip at N ∝ C0.46, D ∝ C0.54 — near a 50/50 split.
4 · Sanity check: the two exponents on C must sum to 1 (because C ≈ 6ND). Kaplan's 0.73 + 0.27 and Chinchilla's ≈0.46 + 0.54 both pass — so the fight is about where the valley sits, not the accounting.

Why it matters

  • Same compute, better model — Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) matched Gopher's (280B params, 300B tokens) training compute but beat it on the large majority of benchmarks.
  • Cheaper to deploy — inference cost scales with N, not D. A 4x-smaller model that was trained compute-optimally is 4x cheaper to serve, forever, at no quality cost.
  • Convergent evidence — three methods, three different assumptions, same conclusion is much stronger evidence than one fit, which is exactly why the field trusted the correction.
🔗
Causal chain: fixed compute budget C (Week 5 Day 1) → compute identity C ≈ 6ND forces a trade-off between N and D → a biased fitting procedure (Kaplan) can silently distort that trade-off → a more careful, triple-checked fit (Chinchilla) corrects it and reshapes how every lab since has trained models.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Chinchilla is a lesson in how a fit can be confidently wrong, and in what "compute-optimal" does and doesn't buy you.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): a biased fitting procedure that never errors. Kaplan's fixed schedule length across model sizes stopped small models before their loss floor. There is no crash and the power laws look clean — but the exponents come out systematically tilted toward "bigger model." An entire generation of models (Gopher, GPT-3-era) was sized on that quietly-wrong fit. A senior engineer distrusts a scaling result until it's reproduced by an independent method (Chinchilla used three).
⚖️
Trade-off: compute-optimal for training vs. cheap to serve. Chinchilla-optimal (a smaller model on more tokens) matches Kaplan-era quality at equal training compute and is far cheaper to serve, since inference cost scales with N. But it needs more data D (pressing on the data wall) and longer wall-clock. Labs often deliberately train past Chinchilla-optimal — a smaller model on even more tokens (e.g. LLaMA) — because lifetime inference savings outweigh the extra training cost. That's a design-review decision about where the model will spend its life.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "At fixed compute, loss is E + A/Nα + B/Dβ with D = C/6N, so there's an interior-optimal model size — the bottom of the U. Chinchilla's three independent methods put it near N ∝ C0.5, D ∝ C0.5, versus Kaplan's parameter-heavy 0.73/0.27. The nuance is why Kaplan was off — a fixed learning-rate-schedule length biased small models low — and that Chinchilla-optimal minimizes training cost, not inference, so labs like LLaMA deliberately overtrain a smaller model for cheaper serving."
