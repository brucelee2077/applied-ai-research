Scaling Laws · Week 5 · Day 4

Chaining ParabolasInto a Power Law

Wednesday you fit one parabola per compute budget and found one best model size, N_opt, for that budget. Today you take the minimum points from many of those parabolas and chain them into a single formula, N_opt(C) ∝ C^a, that predicts the best model size for any compute budget — the exact algebra behind Chinchilla's "scale N and D equally" result.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain why a power law is a straight line in log-log space, chain 9 IsoFLOP vertices into one global fit, compute the slope a by hand from two points, and reproduce Chinchilla's a≈0.49, b≈0.51 from real Table 3 numbers.
1What is a power law, and why do we need one here?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Monday's Kaplan scaling laws, Tuesday's Chinchilla correction, and Wednesday's IsoFLOP methodology (fix compute C, sweep model size N, fit one parabola per budget). No prior regression experience needed.

A power law, in plain words

A power law is a relationship of the form y = k · x^p: y equals some constant k times x raised to a fixed power p. The special trick of a power law is this: if you plot log(y) against log(x) instead of the raw numbers, the curve becomes a perfectly straight line, and its slope is exactly p.

Chinchilla needs one specific power law: N_opt(C) ∝ C^a — "the best model size to train, given a fixed compute budget C, grows as C raised to some fixed exponent a." There's a matching one for data too: D_opt(C) ∝ C^b. Getting a and b right is the entire point of the paper.

Why this matters: Kaplan got the exponent wrong

Kaplan et al. (2020) ran this same kind of analysis and found a=0.73, b=0.27 — meaning almost all extra compute should go into more parameters, and only a little into more data. Chinchilla's IsoFLOP method (Wednesday) exists to generate better raw ingredients for finding the true a and b.

The raw ingredient: 9 vertices, one per budget

For each of 9 fixed FLOP budgets C_i (from 6×10^18 to 3×10^21 FLOPs in the paper), Wednesday's method trains several model sizes, fits a parabola to loss vs. log(N), and reads off the minimum — that minimum's N-coordinate is N_opt for that one budget. Doing this once per budget gives a small table of 9 points: (C_1, N_opt at C_1), ..., (C_9, N_opt at C_9).

Today's step: chaining

Chaining the parabolas means taking exactly these 9 (C, N_opt) vertex points — throwing away everything else about each parabola except its minimum — and fitting a new curve through just those 9 points, in log-log space, using linear regression. The slope of that regression line is the exponent a. Repeat with the 9 (C, D_opt) points to get b.

📦
Two stages, two very different jobs: many local parabolas first (Wednesday — "what's best for this one budget?"), then one global power law second (today — "what's the formula for any budget?").

So what's today's catch?

The chaining step itself is simple linear regression — nothing new mathematically. The catch is doing it in the right space: raw (C, N_opt) values do not fall on a line, only (log C, log N_opt) does. Forget to take logs, and the fit silently produces a meaningless slope. We'll cover this in the playground.

😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 单看线性回归谁都会，但一到「两层拟合叠在一起」就晕：先给每个预算拟合一条抛物线取顶点，再把这些顶点拟合成一条幂律线。In plain terms: the algebra is only linear regression, but people lose the plot because it's two fits stacked — one parabola per budget to get each vertex, then one global line through those vertices — and both fits hand back a confident-looking number no matter what you feed them.
📚

2Tire pressure for different loadsIntuition

Imagine tuning a car's tire pressure for different cargo loads. For each specific weight you might carry — 100 kg, 200 kg, 400 kg — you run an experiment: try several tire pressures, measure fuel efficiency at each, and plot a U-shaped curve. Too low a pressure wastes fuel from rolling resistance; too high wastes fuel from a harsh, bouncy ride. The bottom of that U is the single best pressure for that load.

🚗⚖️
One parabola = one load

Fix the cargo weight (that's your "budget"), sweep the tire pressure (that's your "model size"), find the minimum. This is exactly Wednesday's IsoFLOP parabola — one curve per fixed compute budget.

📈📉
Chain the 9 answers

Do this for 9 different cargo weights and you get 9 "best pressure" answers. Plot those 9 (weight, best-pressure) pairs on log-log axes, and if they fall on a straight line, you've found a formula: best pressure scales as weight^p.

In one line: many small experiments (one per budget) hand you 9 "best answer" points; chaining those points into one log-log line gives you a formula that works for every budget, including ones you never tested.

直觉 / Intuition: 先为每个「预算」找到当下最省的模型大小（一条抛物线的最低点），再把这些「最优点」串成一条线，就得到一个能预测任意预算的公式。The technical point is that the per-budget minima are the only thing you keep; on log-log axes they fall on a straight line whose slope is the exponent a, so one regression extrapolates to compute budgets you never trained.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: tire pressure and cargo weight are measured in units that already mean something familiar. In scaling laws, the exponents a and b themselves are the discovery — nobody knew in advance that N and D should scale at nearly the same rate (a≈b≈0.5) until this exact chaining procedure was run on real training data.

4The regression that produced Chinchilla's headline numberHow & why

The rules

  • Each of the 9 IsoFLOP parabolas gives you exactly one number to keep: its vertex, N_opt,i — the loss-minimizing model size for that one specific budget C_i. Everything else about the parabola's shape is discarded.
  • Because every point on one IsoFLOP curve shares the same C (by construction), once N_opt,i is fixed, the matching D_opt,i is fixed too, via C ≈ 6·N·D (the compute-cost identity from Week 3).
  • A power law N_opt(C) ∝ C^a is only a straight line once you take log of both C and N_opt. Fit the line in that log-log space, and the slope is a.
  • The same chaining procedure, run on the (C_i, D_opt,i) pairs, gives the second exponent b.
✅
a_hat, log10k = numpy.polyfit(log10(C), log10(N_opt), 1)
"Fit a straight line through the 9 (log C, log N_opt) points." The slope a_hat is your fitted exponent; k = 10^log10k is the constant, giving the full formula N_opt(C) = k · C^a.
🪜
Math Ladder — the exponent is the slope
1 · In words: on log-log axes the (compute, best-size) points fall on a straight line, and the slope of that line is the exponent a.
2 · The formula: N_opt(C) = k·C^a → log N_opt = log k + a·log C — C = compute budget; N_opt = compute-optimal params; a = the exponent (slope); k = constant. Two points give it directly: a = (log N₂ − log N₁) / (log C₂ − log C₁).
3 · Tiny numbers: two real Table-3 vertices — (C₁=1.23e22, N₁=10e9) and (C₂=5.76e23, N₂=67e9). In log₁₀ that's (22.09, 10.00) and (23.76, 10.83), so a = 0.83 / 1.67 ≈ 0.494 — the paper's a=0.49.
4 · Sanity check: because C ≈ 6·N·D holds everywhere, a + b must land near 1 (Chinchilla: 0.49 + 0.51 = 1.00). If a+b drifts from 1 — or you regress on raw (C, N) without taking logs — the slope is meaningless.

Why it matters

  • Extrapolation beyond what you trained — once you have the formula, you can plug in a compute budget you never actually swept (like the real Gopher/Chinchilla budget) and read off the predicted N_opt and D_opt before spending a single token of real training.
  • A sanity check comes for free — because C ≈ 6 · N_opt · D_opt must hold everywhere, the fitted exponents should satisfy a + b ≈ 1. Chinchilla gets 0.49 + 0.51 = 1.00 exactly.
  • It quantifies exactly how wrong Kaplan was — Kaplan's a=0.73, b=0.27 vs. Chinchilla's a≈0.49, b≈0.51 is not a rounding difference; it's the empirical basis for Tuesday's "Kaplan models were undertrained" claim.
🔗
Causal chain: IsoFLOP parabolas (Wednesday) → keep only each vertex → 9 (C, N_opt) points → log-transform both axes → linear regression slope = exponent a → repeat for D_opt to get b → a≈b≈0.5 is Chinchilla's central finding.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Chaining is two regressions stacked on top of each other, and both stages return a confident-looking number no matter what you feed them — that is exactly where this quietly goes wrong.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): a single bad vertex poisons the slope. The global fit trusts all 9 vertices equally. If one IsoFLOP parabola was fit on too few or too noisy points, its vertex N_opt,i = 10^(-p1/(2·p2)) can land far off — and because numpy.polyfit never errors, it just tilts the regression line and hands you a wrong exponent that still looks reasonable (say a=0.44 instead of 0.49). Nothing crashes; you only find out when your extrapolated model size is off. In code review, a senior engineer checks the per-budget fits before the global one: are the vertices monotonic in C, is each parabola actually convex (p2 > 0), and does a + b come out near 1 as the built-in cross-check?
⚖️
Trade-off: a clean power law buys extrapolation but hides the model. Collapsing every parabola down to one vertex, then one straight line, gives you a formula you can extend to compute budgets you never trained — that is the whole payoff. What you pay is that the line assumes the exponent is the same at every scale. If the true relationship bends at very large C, the fit will not warn you; it will happily extrapolate a straight line into a region where reality has curved away. In a design review, the decision is how far past your largest measured budget you are willing to trust that constant slope before you spend real money on it.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "You fit one parabola per compute budget, keep only each vertex — the loss-minimizing model size — then regress those (log C, log N_opt) points; the slope is the exponent a in N_opt ∝ C^a, and you repeat for D to get b. Chinchilla gets a≈0.49, b≈0.51, so scale parameters and data roughly equally — correcting Kaplan's 0.73/0.27. The nuance: it's two stacked fits and polyfit never errors, so one bad vertex silently tilts the slope — I check each parabola is convex and that a+b≈1 before trusting any extrapolation."
