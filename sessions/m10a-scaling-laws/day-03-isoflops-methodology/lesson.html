Scaling Laws · Week 5 · Day 3

The IsoFlopsMethodology

You have a fixed pile of compute to spend on one training run. Make the model bigger and it can afford fewer tokens; make it smaller and it wastes its data. So what size is best? IsoFlops is the hands-on recipe: fix the budget, train a few sizes, and let a parabola point at the winner.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: use C ≈ 6·N·D to trade model size against tokens under a fixed budget, spot the U-shaped loss valley, fit a parabola with curve_fit to read off the compute-optimal size, and stack budgets into a scaling law.
1FLOPs, budgets, and the IsoFlops recipeWhat is it
🧭
What this assumes: you know what a neural network is and that it has parameters (its adjustable numbers) and trains on tokens (chunks of text). This week's earlier days on Kaplan and Chinchilla scaling help, but are not required. No prior knowledge of compute budgets needed.

Start with the words

A FLOP is one floating-point operation — one multiply or one add on real numbers. Training a big model costs a huge number of them, so people count them in units like 1e18 or 1e21.

Your compute budget is the total number of FLOPs you are allowed to spend on one training run. Think of it as a fixed pile of money you must spend, no more and no less.

There is a well-known rule of thumb: training a transformer on D tokens with N parameters costs about C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs. The 6 comes from roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter in the forward pass and 4 in the backward pass, per token.

The tension

For a fixed budget C, if you make the model bigger (larger N), you can only afford fewer tokens (smaller D), because the product N·D must stay put. A tiny model has plenty of tokens but not enough capacity to learn — it underfits. A giant model has huge capacity but, under the same budget, sees too few tokens — it is undertrained. Somewhere in between is a sweet spot where loss is lowest.

🏷️
"IsoFlops" literally means "equal FLOPs": you hold the compute budget constant and vary only the model size. ("Iso-" is a prefix meaning "same," like isobar = same pressure.)

The recipe, step by step

  1. Choose a handful of fixed budgets, e.g. C = 1e18, 3e18, 1e19, 3e19, 6e19 FLOPs.
  2. For each budget, pick several model sizes N, and for each set the token count D = C / (6·N) so every run spends the same FLOPs.
  3. Train each run to completion and record its final loss (a number measuring prediction error; lower is better).
  4. Plot final loss (y) against model size N (x, log-scaled) — the points trace a U-shape: high on the left from underfitting, high on the right from undertraining, low in the middle.
  5. Fit a parabola (a quadratic y = a·x² + b·x + c) and read off its minimum. That minimum's x-value is the compute-optimal model size for that budget.
  6. Repeat for every budget, collect the (budget, optimal-size) pairs, and fit a line in log-log space — that line is your scaling law.

The Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022) did exactly this and found optimal N and D grow at roughly the same rate — about 20 training tokens per parameter. That is why a 70B "Chinchilla" model trained on ~1.4T tokens beat the larger 280B "Gopher" trained on fewer tokens. curve_fit (from SciPy) is the tool that does the fitting: hand it your data points and a function shape, and it returns the coefficients that best match.

📚
Official docs (optional): Feinberg — scaling laws talk (PDF) · Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022) · scipy.optimize.curve_fit · Matplotlib
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 大家以为"拟合出一个 vertex"就等于"找到了最优" — people think getting a vertex out of curve_fit means they found the optimum. In plain terms: the fit always returns a number, even when your model sizes never actually bracketed the valley — so the danger isn't the algebra, it's trusting a minimum you never trained.
2Packing a fixed-weight airline bagIntuition

Picture packing for a trip with one hard rule: the bag has a fixed weight limit you cannot exceed. That weight limit is your FLOP budget. Now you must choose how to fill it — a few big items, or many small ones.

🧥
One giant coat = too big

All your weight goes into a single huge item. You have "capacity" but almost nothing else, so you're unprepared for most situations. That's a big model with too few tokens: undertrained.

🧦
Only tiny socks = too small

Many little items, but nothing substantial. Also unprepared. That's a small model with lots of data but not enough capacity: it underfits.

The best trip comes from a middle mix. If you tried every possible split and scored each packing choice, then plotted score against "average item size," the points would trace a valley with a clear best point in the middle. That valley is your IsoFlops parabola, and its bottom is the compute-optimal size.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: an airline bag has one obvious weight number, but real training FLOPs are only approximated by C ≈ 6·N·D. And unlike luggage, the loss valley can be shallow and noisy, so the "bottom" is a fitted estimate — not a sharp edge you can feel.

直觉 / Intuition: 固定预算下，太大和太小都亏，最好的尺寸在"下弯又上弯"的谷底 — at fixed compute both extremes waste the budget, so the best size sits at the bottom of a valley. The technical point: because D = C/6N, loss vs. log N is a U, and the whole method is "find the bottom of that U."

4The math of the recipe, and why it worksHow & why

The rules

  • The budget constraint: C ≈ 6·N·D. Fix C, and N·D is pinned. So D = C / (6·N) — pick a size, the tokens follow automatically.
  • Fit in log-space. Use x = log10(N), not raw N. Model sizes span many orders of magnitude, so a parabola in raw N is skewed and biases the vertex.
  • The parabola: loss ≈ a·x² + b·x + c. A valley needs a > 0 (opens upward). Its bottom (minimum) sits at x* = −b / (2a).
  • Recover the size: N_opt = 10^(x*), and its matching tokens D_opt = C / (6·N_opt).
✅
a, b, c = polyfit(log10(N), loss, 2)  →  x_star = -b/(2*a)  →  N_opt = 10**x_star
Fit the quadratic, take the vertex, undo the log. That is the whole "read off the optimum" step.
🪜
Math Ladder — read the optimum off a parabola
1 · In words: at a fixed budget, loss vs. model size makes a U; the lowest point is the compute-optimal size. A parabola is the simplest curve with exactly one bottom, so fit one and read its bottom.
2 · The formula: fit loss ≈ a·x² + b·x + c with x = log₁₀(N); the vertex is at x* = −b/(2a), then N_opt = 10x*. Need a > 0 (opens upward). Here x = log model size, a = curvature, b, c = fit constants.
3 · Tiny numbers: suppose a fit gives a = 0.5, b = −8. Then x* = −(−8)/(2·0.5) = 8, so N_opt = 108 = 100M params — a size you may never have trained exactly.
4 · Sanity check: the vertex must land inside the sizes you swept (loss visibly goes down then up). If x* is past your largest N, or a ≤ 0, the "optimum" is an extrapolation — reject it and widen the sweep.

Why the valley exists

  • Left arm (small N): the model runs out of capacity before it runs out of data. Extra tokens can't help — it underfits, so loss is high.
  • Right arm (large N): the model has capacity to spare but, under the fixed budget, sees too few tokens. It is undertrained, so loss is high again.
  • The dip: the size where added capacity and available tokens are in balance. Lowest loss lives here.
🔗
Causal chain: fix budget C → N·D is pinned by 6·N·D → sweep sizes, each gives a loss → the losses form a valley → fit a parabola, take its vertex → that is the compute-optimal size → repeat across budgets → the (compute, optimal-size) points fall on a line → that line is the scaling law.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The IsoFlops recipe is simple, which is exactly why it's easy to run wrong and still get a number. A staff engineer knows the number that lies, and what the method costs.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): fitting a parabola to a sweep that never bracketed the minimum. If loss is still falling at your largest trained size, there is no valley in your data — but polyfit/curve_fit happily returns a vertex anyway, extrapolated past everything you measured. You report a compute-optimal N you never actually trained, and nothing errors. A senior engineer checks that the loss curve visibly turns back up inside the sampled range before trusting the vertex.
⚖️
Trade-off: robust sweep vs. cheap parametric fit. IsoFlops (fix C, sweep N) is assumption-light and hard to fool — it just needs a bracketed minimum per budget — but it's expensive, since most of the models you train at each budget are deliberately suboptimal. Fitting one parametric surface L(N,D) to all runs at once is far cheaper per datapoint, but it bakes in a functional form that can be wrong. How many budgets × sizes to sweep, versus trusting a fitted surface, is a design-review call about the accuracy vs. cost of the scaling study itself.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "IsoFLOP: fix a compute budget, sweep model size with D = C/6N, and loss traces a U in log N. Fit a parabola and its vertex x* = −b/(2a) is the compute-optimal size; stack those vertices across budgets and the slope of the log–log line is the scaling exponent. The nuance is you must fit in log-space and confirm the minimum is bracketed — curve_fit will happily hand back a vertex extrapolated past sizes you never trained, and it won't error."

7Build a working IsoFlops loopProduce

Today's task: write a documented IsoFlops loop that fixes several budgets, sweeps model sizes, records a loss per run, fits a parabola per budget, and plots the resulting compute → optimal-size scaling line.

Acceptance criteria

  • At least 3 distinct budgets, each with ≥4 model sizes; a printed table shows N, derived D = C/(6N), and that C stays constant within each group (assert |6·N·D − C| / C < 1e-6).
  • Each per-budget loss-vs-size scatter shows a visible U-shape — an interior minimum, not one pinned at an endpoint.
  • curve_fit returns quadratic coefficients; compute x* = −b/(2a) and convert to N_opt (and D_opt) for each budget, printed to stdout.
  • A final log-log plot of compute vs. N_opt with a fitted trend line and its printed slope; one line comparing to Chinchilla's ~20 tokens/param.
  • Runs top-to-bottom with no errors; every step prints shapes / intermediate values.
⚠️
The classic mistake: fitting the parabola over a size range that does not actually bracket the minimum. If all your sizes fall on the underfitting (left) arm, the vertex x* = −b/(2a) lands outside your data — an extrapolation, not a measurement. Confirm the loss curve goes down then back up before trusting curve_fit; if the minimum is pinned at an endpoint, widen your size range. A related trap: forgetting to fit in log-space (use log10(N) as x).
