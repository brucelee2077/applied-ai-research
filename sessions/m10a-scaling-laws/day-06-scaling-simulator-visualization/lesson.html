1The vocabulary, then the money questionWhat is it
🧭
What this assumes: you can read simple Python and you know that training a model means adjusting numbers to lower an error score. No prior scaling-law knowledge needed — every term below is defined the first time it appears.

Three words that carry the whole day

A FLOP (floating-point operation) is one arithmetic step on real numbers — one multiply or one add. Training a model costs a giant number of these.

The number of tunable numbers inside the model is called N — the parameters. The amount of text you train on is called D — the number of tokens (a token is a chunk of text, roughly a word-piece). The total arithmetic cost of the run is C, the total training FLOPs.

There is one simple accounting rule that ties them together:

📦
C = 6 · N · D
Total training cost ≈ 6 FLOPs per parameter, per token. The 6 comes from ~2 FLOPs for the forward pass and ~4 for the backward pass, per parameter, per token.

What is a scaling law?

A scaling law is a formula found from experiments that predicts model loss (a number measuring prediction error — lower is better) from N and D. Kaplan and others (2020) showed loss falls as a smooth power law: a straight line on a log-log plot. A power law means loss drops by a fixed factor every time you double the model — no sudden cliff, no plateau, just a steady slide.

The money question: Chinchilla

The Chinchilla paper (2022) asked: if C is fixed, what N and D give the lowest loss? Because C = 6ND, once you pick N the data D = C / (6N) is decided for you. So there is really one knob (the N/D split) and one best setting. Chinchilla's answer: grow N and D together — each about as C^0.5 — which works out to roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. Older giant models like Gopher (280B params, only 300B tokens) were far too big for their data — undertrained.

📚
Official references: Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022) · Kaplan et al. 2020 · Feinberg — scaling talk (2025) · JAX scaling book — roofline · SciPy curve_fit · Matplotlib · Seaborn

2A road trip with a fixed fuel budgetIntuition

Imagine planning a road trip with a fixed amount of money for fuel. You must split it between a bigger, thirstier engine (more horsepower = model size N) and more miles of actual driving (fuel burned on the road = training data D). You want to get as far as possible — the farthest distance is the lowest loss.

🏎️💨
All engine, no road

Spend everything on a monster engine and you barely leave the driveway — a huge model that never saw enough data. This is being undertrained (Gopher's mistake).

🚗🛣️
All road, tiny engine

Pour all the fuel into distance in an underpowered car and you crawl — mountains of data poured into a model too small to absorb it.

In one line: there is one sweet split that gets you farthest. Chinchilla found that for every unit of engine you should buy about 20 units of driving, and this ratio holds as the budget grows.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: fuel and engine are separate purchases, but N and D are chained by C = 6ND — pick the engine and the miles are decided, so it is really one dial, not two. Also, a real trip has a hard maximum distance; loss has a soft floor (the E term, the entropy of the data) that you approach but never cross.

直觉 / Intuition: 这一天不是新概念，而是把整周的东西接成一条流水线：钱 → 算力 → 最优配方 — today isn't a new idea, it's wiring the whole week into one pipeline: dollars → compute → optimal recipe. The technical point: once C is fixed, C = 6ND plus the ~20-tokens/param rule leaves exactly one best model size.

4The rules of the simulator, and why they matterHow & why

The rules

  • Dollars → FLOPs. gpu_hours = usd / price_per_gpu_hr, then C = gpu_hours × 3600 × H100_bf16 × MFU. MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization, usually 0.3–0.5) is the fraction of peak speed you actually get — GPUs never hit peak.
  • The one constraint. C = 6ND. Pick N and D = C / (6N) is forced. It is a see-saw, not two free choices.
  • The optimal split. Set D = 20·N (20 tokens per parameter), plug into C = 6ND, and solve: N* = sqrt(C / 120).
  • The full scaling law. L(N, D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β. E is the irreducible loss — the entropy of the data, which no compute removes.
✅
optimal_N_D(C=7.12e23) → N* ≈ 77B params, D* ≈ 1.54T tokens
$1M of H100 time lands almost exactly on Chinchilla's real 70B / 1.4T recipe.
🪜
Math Ladder — dollars to an optimal model size
1 · In words: turn a dollar budget into compute, then split that compute to buy about 20 tokens per parameter; that leaves exactly one best model size.
2 · The formula: C = gpu_hours × 3600 × peak_FLOP/s × MFU, then with D = 20N and C = 6ND: N* = √(C/120). MFU = fraction of peak you actually reach (0.3–0.5); the 120 = 6 × 20.
3 · Tiny numbers: $1M at ~$2/H100-hr → 500k GPU-hours; ×3600×~1e15×0.4 ≈ 7.2e23 FLOPs. N* = √(7.2e23/120) = √(6e21) ≈ 7.7e10 ≈ 77B params, and D = C/6N ≈ 1.5T tokens.
4 · Sanity check: D/N should come out ≈ 20 (1.5e12 / 7.7e10 ≈ 19.5 ✓). If you leave MFU = 1.0, C is ~2.5× too big and N* ~1.6× too big — the recipe silently inflates.

Why it matters

  • It is the plan every lab makes. Before spending millions, you decide N and D — get it wrong and you burn the whole budget on an undertrained giant.
  • The U-shape is real. At fixed C, too-small models underfit and too-large models starve on data; the valley bottom is the sweet spot.
  • The frontier is a power law. Connect the valley bottoms across budgets and, on log-log axes, they form a straight line of slope ≈ 0.5 for both N* and D*.
🔗
Causal chain: hardware throughput (Week 5) → dollars become C FLOPs (with MFU) → C = 6ND makes it one knob → the IsoFLOP U picks the best N → many U's trace a power-law frontier → the 20-tokens-per-param planning rule.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The simulator turns money into a model recipe through a chain of clean multiplications, so it always prints a plausible (N, D) — which is exactly why the one wrong assumption inside it can go unnoticed.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): budgeting at peak FLOPs instead of MFU. If you drop the MFU factor (or leave it at 1.0) in C = gpu_hours × 3600 × H100_bf16 × MFU, the dollars-to-C step overstates your real compute by 2–3x. Every later step still runs — N* = sqrt(C/120), D = C/(6N), the plots — and hands you a bigger, confident-looking recipe. Nothing errors; you only discover the gap when the real run costs 2–3x the money or blows past the wall-clock. In code review, a senior engineer checks that the compute number was derived with a realistic MFU (0.3–0.5), not peak, before any N/D plan is trusted.
⚖️
Trade-off: the 20-tokens-per-param rule optimizes training cost, not lifetime cost. Chinchilla's split gives the lowest training loss for a fixed C, so it is the right answer to "how do I spend this training budget?" But a smaller model trained on more tokens than compute-optimal (deliberately "overtrained") costs more to train yet is cheaper to serve for every request afterward — which can win overall when a model is queried billions of times. In a design review, the decision is whether you are optimizing the one-time training bill or the total training-plus-inference bill over the model's life.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "The simulator is a chain: dollars → GPU-hours → C = hours × 3600 × peak × MFU, then C = 6ND with the ~20-tokens/param rule gives N* = √(C/120). At $1M that's ≈77B params on ≈1.5T tokens — Chinchilla's recipe. The nuance is MFU: budgeting at peak FLOPs instead of a realistic 0.3–0.5 overstates compute 2–3× and silently inflates the whole recipe, and the 20× rule optimizes training cost, not lifetime serving cost."

7Build a scaling simulator + Chinchilla plotProduce

Today's capstone (Saturday + Sunday combined): build a small scaling simulator that turns dollars into an optimal (N, D) plan, then a notebook that visualizes the IsoFLOP parabolas and the power-law frontier — the Chinchilla figure in your own code.

Acceptance criteria

  • budget_to_flops(usd, price_per_gpu_hr, h100_bf16=989.5e12, mfu): for usd=1e6, price=2.0, mfu=0.4 returns 7.12e23 (±1%).
  • optimal_N_D(C, tokens_per_param=20): returns (N*, D*) with 6·N*·D* == C (±0.1%) and D*/N* == tokens_per_param; for C=7.12e23 gives N* ≈ 7.7e10, D* ≈ 1.5e12 (±5%).
  • isoflop_scan(C): returns arrays whose loss curve has a single interior minimum (the U), and its argmin matches optimal_N_D within a factor of 1.5.
  • Notebook: ≥3 IsoFLOP parabolas on one axis, each minimum marked, a frontier line through them; a second log-log panel fits the frontier with scipy.optimize.curve_fit and prints slope in 0.4–0.6.
