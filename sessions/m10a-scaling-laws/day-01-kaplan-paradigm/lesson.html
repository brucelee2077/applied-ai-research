Scaling Laws · Week 5 · Day 1

The Kaplan ParadigmScaling Laws for Neural Language Models

You now understand a transformer's mechanics inside and out — attention, training loops, the whole engine. Today's question is different: if you could only turn one knob to make a model better, which knob matters most — how big the model is, or how much data it sees? In 2020, Kaplan et al. measured this across hundreds of models and found a surprisingly clean, lopsided answer.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain what a power-law scaling curve is, state the Kaplan compute-optimal exponents (N ∝ C0.73, D ∝ C0.27), work a numeric example of splitting a compute budget, and name the key gotcha (C vs. C_min) before the Chinchilla correction arrives later this week.
1What is a scaling law, and what did Kaplan et al. find?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Weeks 1–4 — you know what a transformer's parameters (N) and training data (D) are, and roughly what a training run costs in compute. No prior scaling-law experience needed.

The question this paper asks

You've now built and understood a transformer. A natural next question: if you want a lower test loss (the model predicts held-out text better), what should you spend your money on? Two knobs, both of which help:

  • Model size N — the number of parameters (weights) in the model. Bigger N means more capacity to represent patterns.
  • Dataset size D — how many tokens (roughly, words/word-pieces) the model trains on.

In 2020, Jared Kaplan and collaborators at OpenAI trained hundreds of transformer language models — spanning more than 6 orders of magnitude in parameter count and 8 orders of magnitude in training compute — all on the same text corpus (WebText2), and measured the cross-entropy loss on held-out text for each one.

The answer: loss follows a power law

A power law means: if you plot loss against N (or D, or compute C) on a log-log plot (both axes stretched so each step multiplies by 10 instead of adding 1), the points fall on a nearly straight line. That's a very clean, very unusual finding — it held almost independent of architecture details like depth or width.

📦
Because both N and D reduce loss, and because your total compute C is limited, the paper asks: for a fixed compute budget, how should you split it between "bigger model" and "more data"?
Their headline answer is lopsided: grow N almost as fast as compute itself (N ∝ C0.73), and grow D much more slowly (D ∝ C0.27). This is the "Kaplan paradigm" — and it's part of the thinking that shaped GPT-3's design.

So what's today's catch?

The catch arrives later this week, not today: this 2020 finding was later revised by the 2022 Chinchilla paper. Hold today's conclusion clearly in mind first — you'll need it as the "before" picture when the correction shows up.

📚
Official sources (optional): Kaplan et al. 2020, "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" · Vlad Feinberg's Princeton talk slides on scaling laws
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: "scaling law" 听起来像"越大越好"，但真正的问题是固定预算下怎么分配 — it sounds like "bigger is better," but the real claim is about how to split a fixed budget. In plain terms: people memorize N ∝ C0.73 without noticing the two exponents must add to 1, so they can't say why "favor parameters" is a choice that costs something.
2A kitchen with a fixed one-time budgetIntuition

Imagine you're opening a new restaurant with a fixed, one-time budget. You can spend the money on two things.

🍳🧑‍🍳
Kitchen size = model size N

Bigger ovens, more stations, more cooks. This is the kitchen's raw capacity to represent and produce complex dishes — like a model's parameters.

🥕📦
Pantry size = dataset size D

More ingredients, more practice services before opening night. This is the raw material the kitchen learns from — like a model's training tokens.

Kaplan's finding, in one line: for a fixed total budget, you get a better result by spending most of the money on a much bigger kitchen and only a little more on extra ingredients. A huge kitchen with a modestly bigger pantry beats a modest kitchen with a huge pantry, dollar for dollar.

直觉 / Intuition: 固定预算下，问题不是"要不要更大"，而是"下一块钱该买厨房还是买食材" — with a fixed budget the question isn't "bigger?" but "does the next dollar buy kitchen or ingredients?" The technical point: C ≈ 6ND ties the two together, so the whole decision collapses to a single exponent.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a kitchen's skill doesn't decay if it never uses all its ingredients. But Kaplan's models are deliberately stopped before they finish learning from all the data — it's less like leftover ingredients, and more like closing practice service early because the next dollar is better spent on equipment than on the next minute of practice. Also, unlike independent purchases, N and D interact: a kitchen too big for its pantry "overfits" — the paper captures this with a combined formula, coming up in Section 4.

Allocation dial — where does compute go? N ∝ C0.73, D ∝ C0.27 → parameter-heavy (Kaplan)

Learning question: the exponent a in N ∝ Ca decides how a 100× jump in compute splits between a bigger model and more data (D ∝ C1−a). What did Kaplan claim a is — and why is a bigger a not automatically better?

a = 0.73
👀
What you should notice: drag a toward Kaplan's 0.73 and the blue model bar swallows almost all of a 100× compute jump (≈29×) while the orange data bar barely moves (≈3.5×); the log–log line on the right tilts steeper. Slide toward 0.50 and the two bars even out — the balanced split Chinchilla will argue for later this week.
⚠️
Misconception: a bigger a is not "better." It minimizes training loss under Kaplan's fit, but a huge a means an over-parameterized, under-fed model that is expensive to serve forever. The dial shows a design choice, not a quality score.

How to read it: a is the slope of log N_opt vs log C; data D takes the leftover exponent 1−a, and the two growth factors must multiply back to the compute factor. Simplified: one clean 100× jump with the constant held fixed — real fits use hundreds of noisy runs, and this folds the batch/step split (§4) into the single D exponent.

4The three power laws, and why "favor parameters" followsHow & why

The three individual power laws

  • Loss vs. model size: L(N) = (N_c / N)^{α_N}, with α_N ≈ 0.076 and N_c ≈ 8.8×10^13 (non-embedding parameters), holding D effectively infinite.
  • Loss vs. dataset size: L(D) = (D_c / D)^{α_D}, with α_D ≈ 0.103, holding N effectively infinite and training to convergence.
  • Loss vs. compute: a third power law in C_min, the compute you'd need if you trained at the compute-optimal critical batch size rather than an arbitrary fixed batch size (Section 5 of the paper).
⚠️
Gotcha: N_c, D_c are normalization constants fit to WebText2 with a specific tokenizer — the paper is explicit that they have no fundamental meaning on their own. Only the exponents (α_N, α_D) are the transferable finding.

Why "favor parameters" follows: the compute-optimal split

Training compute is roughly C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs (one forward+backward pass, standard accounting). Given a fixed C_min budget, the paper asks how to split it between model size N, batch size B, and training steps S to minimize loss. Their empirically-fit answer:

✅
N ∝ C_min^0.73  ·  B ∝ C_min^0.24  ·  S ∝ C_min^0.03
Since one epoch processes D = S·B tokens, this implies D_opt ∝ C_min^{0.03+0.24} = C_min^0.27 — matching the paper's directly-fit result. Almost all extra compute buys a bigger model, barely any buys more training steps.
🪜
Math Ladder — the compute-optimal allocation
1 · In words: split a fixed compute budget so the model size and the token count each grow as a fixed power of compute; the two powers must add to 1, because compute is roughly the product 6·N·D.
2 · The rule: N ∝ Ca, D ∝ Cb with a + b = 1; Kaplan fit a ≈ 0.73, b ≈ 0.27. Here a = the parameter exponent, b = the data exponent, C = training compute in FLOPs.
3 · Tiny numbers: multiply compute by 100. Model grows 1000.73 = 101.46 ≈ 29×; data grows 1000.27 = 100.54 ≈ 3.5×.
4 · Sanity check: the two growth factors must multiply back to the compute factor — 29 × 3.5 ≈ 100 ✓ — which is just a + b = 1 restated. If your fitted exponents don't sum to ≈1, your fit or your C ≈ 6ND accounting is wrong.

Why it matters

  • Stop before convergence — because S barely grows with compute, compute-optimal training means training a big model briefly, not training it to its loss floor.
  • Sample efficiency — the overfitting-avoidance relation D ∝ N^{α_N/α_D} ≈ N^0.74 (Section 6) means bigger models need proportionally less extra data per extra parameter than you'd naively expect.
  • Shaped early LLM design — this reasoning is part of the intellectual lineage behind GPT-3's 175B-parameter, comparatively data-modest design.
🔗
Causal chain: transformer mechanics (Weeks 1–4) → clean power laws in loss vs. N, D, C → fixed compute forces a trade-off → the fitted exponents say "grow N fast, D slowly" → GPT-3-era design choices.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Kaplan's exponents shaped a whole era of models — and then got corrected. A staff engineer knows why a clean-looking fit can mislead, and what the N-vs-D split really costs.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): trusting a scaling-law fit whose training procedure was biased. Kaplan used one learning-rate-schedule length for every model size, so small models were stopped before annealing to their own loss floor. Nothing errors — the power laws look clean and authoritative — but that size-dependent bias tilted the exponents toward "always make the model bigger," steering the field into over-parameterized, under-trained models (the GPT-3 era) until Chinchilla caught it. A senior engineer checks how the curve was fit (schedule length, early stopping) before trusting the exponents.
⚖️
Trade-off: parameters now vs. serving cost forever. At fixed compute C ≈ 6ND, every extra FLOP either grows N or grows D. Kaplan's answer (grow N fast, D slowly) minimizes training loss — but N sets inference cost for the life of the model, while D is paid once. Favoring parameters can win the training benchmark and lose the deployment budget. Which way to lean is a design-review call, not just a curve fit.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Kaplan's compute-optimal rule is N ∝ C0.73, D ∝ C0.27 — the exponents sum to 1 because C ≈ 6ND, so almost every extra FLOP buys a bigger model, not more data; that reasoning is the lineage behind GPT-3. The nuance is that the fit was biased: one learning-rate-schedule length for all model sizes cut small models off before their loss floor, tilting the exponents toward 'bigger', which Chinchilla later corrected to roughly 0.5/0.5."
