1What is MFU, and what is the roofline model?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Day 2's trace (you can already classify a step as compute-bound, communication-bound, or host-bound) and the C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs formula from earlier scaling-law work. No prior MFU or roofline experience needed.

The problem: "compute-bound" isn't a number

Yesterday's trace told you the step is compute-bound: the device is busy, not waiting on the host. That's good news, but it doesn't tell you how good. A chip like the TPU v5e is rated at 197 TFLOP/s (trillion floating-point operations per second) in bf16 — but it almost never actually delivers 197 TFLOP/s of useful work; real training steps might use anywhere from 10% to 60% of that peak. You need a number that says exactly how much of the chip's advertised power your step is actually using.

The answer: MFU

Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) is that number. It compares the FLOPs your model's math actually needs per step against the FLOPs the chip is theoretically capable of in the time that step took. If your step needs 1 TFLOP of math and takes 10ms, you did 100 TFLOP/s of real work. If the chip's peak is 197 TFLOP/s, your MFU is about 51%.

📦
MFU = (model FLOPs per step) / (step time × chip's peak FLOP/s)
The numerator is math you can compute from your model's shape (the same 6·N·D formula from earlier scaling-law work, applied to one step's tokens instead of the whole run). The denominator you measure with a stopwatch and look up on a spec sheet.

So what's today's catch?

A low MFU tells you that something is wrong, not why. The roofline model is the diagnostic: it classifies your step as compute-bound (the chip's math units are the bottleneck — MFU is your ceiling here) or memory-bound (the chip is waiting on data to move between HBM and its compute units — no amount of extra math throughput would help). Today you compute both numbers for your addition-transformer, then retune batch size and shapes to close the gap.

2A delivery truck, half emptyIntuition

Think of the chip's math units as a delivery truck, and HBM (the chip's memory) as the warehouse loading dock. The truck can carry a huge number of boxes per trip (that's peak FLOP/s). But the dock can only load boxes onto the truck at a limited rate (that's memory bandwidth).

🚚📦📦📦
compute-bound = truck is full, dock keeps up

The truck leaves every trip completely loaded. The bottleneck is how fast the truck can drive (the math), so a faster truck (more FLOPs) genuinely helps. This is where high MFU lives.

🚚📦___
memory-bound = truck waits at the dock

The truck could carry more, but the dock can't load boxes fast enough, so it drives away half-empty every trip. A faster truck does nothing here — you need a faster dock (more bandwidth) or bigger boxes (bigger batches, so each byte moved does more math).

In one line: the roofline model tells you whether your step is limited by the truck's speed (compute) or the dock's loading rate (memory bandwidth) — and MFU only measures how well you're using the truck.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real chip runs many operations back-to-back, and a single training step is usually a mix — some ops are compute-bound, others memory-bound. The roofline model gives you one verdict for the step's dominant operation (usually the big matmuls), not a guarantee that every single op behaves the same way.

4The rules of MFU and the roofline, and why they matterHow & why

The rules

  • Model FLOPs per step ≈ 6 · N · tokens_per_step (the same 6ND shape from earlier scaling-law work, but per step instead of the whole run — N is parameter count, tokens_per_step = batch_size × seq_len).
  • Achieved FLOP/s = (model FLOPs per step) / (measured step time). The step time must come from jax.block_until_ready(), exactly like Day 2 — otherwise you're timing async dispatch, not the device.
  • MFU = achieved FLOP/s / peak FLOP/s, where peak FLOP/s is the chip's spec-sheet number at your training precision (bf16 peak, not fp32 peak, if you train in bf16 — they differ by roughly 2×).
  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs / bytes moved from HBM. It measures how much math you do per byte you load — the higher, the more compute-hungry the op is relative to memory traffic.
  • Ridge point = peak FLOP/s / peak memory bandwidth (bytes/s). If your op's arithmetic intensity is above the ridge point, you're compute-bound; below it, you're memory-bound.
✅
MFU = (6·N·B·S) / (step_time · peak_flops); verdict = "compute-bound" if intensity > ridge_point else "memory-bound"
"How much of the chip's math capacity did this step actually use, and does the roofline say more math throughput would even help?"

Why it's a superpower

  • Turns "it feels slow" into a number — instead of guessing, you get one metric (MFU) that's comparable across runs, batch sizes, and even different models.
  • Tells you which knob to turn — if you're memory-bound, buying a faster chip (more FLOP/s) wastes money; you need bigger batches, fused ops, or less HBM traffic. If you're compute-bound, MFU is your true ceiling and the next lever is better kernels or bigger matmuls.
  • Explains the "donut of doom" — the ring-shaped gap you may have seen in a trace, where the device is idle waiting for tiny ops. Padding shapes to hardware-friendly tile sizes (multiples of 128 on TPU) often closes exactly this gap.
🔗
Causal chain: Day 2's trace tells you the step is compute-bound in a general sense → today's MFU turns that into a percentage → the roofline model explains why that percentage isn't higher → retuning batch size/shapes closes the gap → Thursday's IsoFLOPs sweep starts from a config you know is efficient.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

MFU is a ratio, and a ratio with the wrong denominator still prints a clean-looking percentage — which is how a whole optimization effort can chase the wrong number.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): dividing by the wrong peak. MFU is achieved FLOP/s over the chip's peak FLOP/s, and the spec sheet lists a different peak for each precision — bf16 peak is roughly double fp32 peak. If you train in bf16 but divide by the fp32 peak, your MFU comes out about half the real value; divide by the bf16 peak when you actually ran fp32 and it comes out double. Nothing errors, the report just says "we're at 4% MFU, we have tons of headroom" (or "90%, we're maxed out") when the truth is the opposite, and the team optimizes in the wrong direction. A senior engineer catches this in code review by checking that the peak-FLOP/s constant matches the actual training dtype, and that achieved and peak are quoted at the same precision.
⚖️
Trade-off: bigger batch buys MFU but spends memory and can dull learning. On a memory-bound step, raising the batch size amortizes each weight loaded from HBM over more tokens, lifting arithmetic intensity toward the ridge point and pushing MFU up. The cost is real: the batch's activations must fit in HBM (you can hit OOM), and a very large batch can need learning-rate retuning and sometimes generalizes worse per step of training. So "just make MFU higher" is not free efficiency — how far to push batch size is a design-review decision that weighs hardware utilization against memory limits and training dynamics.
