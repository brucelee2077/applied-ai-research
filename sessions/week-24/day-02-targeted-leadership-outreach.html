1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is targeted, artifact-led outreach?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you have at least one finished, checkable artifact from Weeks 1–22 (a kernel report, a scaling-law fit, a transformer repo). No prior outreach experience needed.

The problem: 200 generic applications a week

Frontier-lab research and research-engineering roles are not filled by resume screening. A recruiter or hiring manager skimming 200 applications a week gives each one about ten seconds. A line like "I am passionate about AI and would love the opportunity" gives that reader nothing to check, nothing to remember, and nothing that separates you from the other 199 people who wrote something almost identical.

This is not a job-application task. It is a targeted, artifact-led outreach task: you send a short, technically dense email directly to a named person, not to a careers inbox, and you lead with a real result instead of a hope.

The answer: prove it in three sentences

The task today is to prove, in the first three sentences of an email, that you have already done frontier-lab-shaped work — not to ask for a chance to prove it later. You pick your single strongest, most quantifiable result from 22 weeks of work, and you put the number in the subject line before the email is even opened.

📦
subject: "Pallas MoE kernel: 34% latency cut via SRAM residency"
The value is visible before the click. Compare that to subject: "Aspiring ML Engineer — Application", which is indistinguishable from every other subject line in the inbox.

So what's today's catch?

The recipient is not a stranger you are guessing about. Vlad Feinberg, who works on pretraining at a frontier lab, has stated in public writing that he wants candidates who complete specific exercises to email him directly, and he has written about his own technical taste in detail. You are not guessing what impresses him — you are answering a stated preference. We'll read his own words in the playground and walkthrough.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of targeted outreach, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • One hero artifact. Pick the single strongest, most quantifiable result from 22 weeks — the scaling-law fit, the Pallas MoE kernel, the ThunderKittens kernel, or the ADRS result — and fully explain only that one, with its mechanism.
  • Numbers, not adjectives. "Much faster" and "more efficient" are unfalsifiable. A number with a baseline, units, hardware, and measurement method can be checked in seconds.
  • Name the mechanism. Not just the result, but the one clause that explains why it worked — e.g. "by keeping expert weights resident in SRAM instead of re-fetching from HBM each forward pass."
  • The silent test. Delete every sentence that has no number, no named artifact, and no mechanism. What survives is the real email — usually under 150 words.
✅
"My ThunderKittens kernel reduces HBM traffic from 2.1 GB to 0.6 GB per forward pass... cutting latency from 8.2ms to 5.1ms on an H100 (nsys, 100-run average)."
Named mechanism (fusing softmax and matmul), exact before/after numbers, hardware, and measurement method. A systems-literate reader can evaluate this without a single follow-up question.

Why it's a superpower

  • Attention is scarce — a reader gives a cold email seconds, not minutes; one sharply proven claim beats five shallow mentions.
  • Verifiable beats persuasive — a real, checkable number invites scrutiny, which is the opposite of what a generic enthusiasm claim can survive.
  • Channel matters as much as content — Feinberg has publicly authorized direct email from candidates who complete specific exercises. Using that stated channel is itself a signal that you read carefully and followed instructions.
🔗
Causal chain: 22 weeks of real artifacts → one hero result chosen and quantified → number placed in subject line and first sentence → reader can verify competence in seconds → email gets read in full instead of skimmed.
