Module 06 · Represent · Day 3

A Small CNN ClassifierStacking Layers to Turn Pixels Into a Label

Have you ever poured cereal through a kitchen funnel — wide at the top, narrow at the bottom — so a big messy pile pours in and comes out as one neat stream? A funnel takes something wide and unmanageable and squeezes it, step by step, down to one small thing. Today you build exactly that shape for pictures. Over the last two days you made a tiny stencil that slides across an image, and you learned to predict the exact grid it spits out. Today those pieces click together into your first real machine that sees: a funnel that takes a wide picture of raw pixels in at the top, squeezes it layer by layer — spotting edges, then shapes, then whole objects — and pours out one small answer at the bottom: "this is a cat." This is a CNN classifier, the same shape behind the camera in your phone that tags faces, and the ancestor of nearly every vision system you have heard of. You already have all the parts. Today you assemble the whole thing — and you teach it to learn.

🎯
Together we'll build a complete image classifier from parts you already know, and then teach it to learn. You'll see the whole funnel end to end: the repeating conv → ReLU → pool block that does the squeezing, why the picture gets thinner (fewer pixels) but deeper (more feature stacks), how early layers spot edges and deep layers spot whole objects, the flatten step that turns the 3-D stack into one list, the classifier head that reads that list into one raw score per class, and then the three pieces that make it learn — softmax (scores into probabilities), cross-entropy (how wrong it is), and the training loop that sends that error back to fix every weight. Then we'll meet — as friendly puzzles — the three things that can go wrong when you stack too eagerly, and the fix for each. Every new word gets explained the moment it shows up.
1The big picture: an image goes in, one label comes outThe whole funnel

Before any details, let's stand back and see the whole machine at once. It helps to know where you're going before we walk each step.

Picture a funnel in a kitchen. It is wide at the top and narrow at the bottom. You pour in a big, messy pile of something — cereal, flour, sand — and it comes out the bottom as one thin, controlled stream. Nothing is lost that matters; the funnel just squeezes the wide mess down to one small, useful thing. A CNN classifier is a funnel for pictures: a wide grid of raw pixels pours in at the top, and one small answer — the class label, like "cat" — comes out the bottom.

What the funnel picture gets right: it goes wide-to-narrow, one direction, squeezing a big input down to one small output, and nothing important is lost on the way. Where it breaks down: a kitchen funnel only moves stuff, unchanged; this funnel transforms what flows through it — it doesn't just shrink the picture, it turns raw pixels into meaningful features, and then into an answer.

The two halves of every classifier

Every CNN classifier has exactly two parts, and it helps to name them now:

  • The feature extractor — the wide-to-narrow stack of conv/pool blocks. Its job: turn raw pixels into features (useful patterns like edges, then shapes, then "this looks like a cat's ear").
  • The classifier head — the small part at the narrow bottom. Its job: read those features and produce one score for each possible answer.

Feature extractor squeezes; head decides. That two-part shape is the whole design, and it is the thing you'll build today. And once it's built, three small extra pieces — which you'll meet in a moment — will teach the whole funnel to learn.

You now have the map. Everything else today zooms into one part of this funnel: what one block does, why the shape changes, what "features" really are, how the flatten and head work, how it turns scores into an answer and learns from its mistakes, and what breaks if you stack carelessly. Keep the funnel picture in your head — every concept is one slice of it.

2The block: conv → ReLU → pool, stacked over and overThe repeating block

The funnel isn't built from a hundred different parts. It is built from one small unit, repeated. Learn the one unit and you understand the whole stack.

Think of building a tall tower out of the same LEGO brick over and over. You don't need a hundred different pieces — you snap one kind of brick on top of another kind of brick, three times, and you have a tower. A CNN's tower is built from one repeating unit called a convolutional block: a convolution, then a bend, then a shrink. Snap a few of those together and you have the whole feature extractor.

What the LEGO picture gets right: the whole tower is the same simple unit repeated, so once you understand one brick you understand the stack; and the order inside the brick is fixed. Where it breaks down: LEGO bricks are identical, but each block learns its own stencils, so block 2's filters are different from block 1's — same shape, different contents.

What each of the three steps does

You've met all three parts already. The block just puts them in a fixed order:

  1. Convolution — slide the learned stencils across the incoming grid to make a stack of "found-it" maps. This is the pattern-spotting step.
  2. ReLU — a ReLU bend on every number: keep positives, zero out negatives. Without this little bend, stacking many conv layers would collapse into one — the bend is what lets depth actually add power.
  3. Pooling — shrink each map by summarizing small windows (e.g. keep the biggest value in every 2 × 2 square). This throws away exact positions but keeps what was found.

Why this exact order? Convolution finds patterns, the bend lets the network learn something a straight line can't, and pooling shrinks the result so the next block has less to chew on. Watch one block transform a small grid, step by step:

What you just saw: the same little grid goes through find → bend → shrink and comes out smaller but sharper — the negatives are gone and the strongest signal (7) survives the pool. You now know the atom of the whole network. Everything from here is stacking this atom and watching the shape change — which is the very next puzzle.

3The trade: the picture gets smaller but the stack gets thickerThin but deep

As the funnel squeezes, something surprising happens to the shape. The picture gets smaller across (fewer pixels), but thicker in a hidden direction (more stacked grids). Understanding this trade is the key to reading any CNN's shapes.

Think of squeezing a wide, floppy sponge into a tall thin glass. The sponge gets narrower — it no longer covers the table. But it also gets taller — it stacks up inside the glass. It's not gone; it's re-shaped: less wide, more deep. A feature map does the same as it goes down the funnel: its height and width shrink, but its channels — the number of stacked grids, each looking for one kind of pattern — grow. Thinner across, deeper down.

What the sponge picture gets right: the total "stuff" is roughly kept — it just moves from wide-and-flat to narrow-and-tall as you squeeze. Where it breaks down: a sponge shrinks passively when you squeeze; a CNN grows its depth on purpose — you choose how many stencils (filters) each conv layer has, and that choice sets the new depth.

Why grow channels while shrinking pixels?

This is the deep bargain of a CNN, and it's worth saying plainly:

  • Shrinking pixels (via pooling or striding, from Day 2) means each deeper unit covers a bigger chunk of the original picture — its receptive field grows. Deeper layers see the big picture, not single dots.
  • Growing channels means the layer can look for more kinds of pattern at once. Early on you only need a few detectors (a handful of edge directions). Deep down you need many (ear-shaped things, eye-shaped things, whisker-shaped things), so you add more channels.

Each channel is one filter's found-it map: one grid saying "here is where my pattern shows up." Watch the trade play out across three blocks — pixels fall, channels climb:

Read it left to right: the picture falls 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 (green — smaller each block), while the channels climb 3 → 16 → 32 → 64 (purple — more each block). This is the signature shape of every CNN you'll ever read. When you see 4 × 4 × 64 at the bottom of a network, you now know exactly what it means: a tiny grid, but 64 different detectors deep. And you'll remember from Day 2: the channel count is a separate ruler — it equals the number of filters, not the tape-measure formula.

4The ladder: early layers see edges, deep layers see whole thingsEdges to objects

Here is the most beautiful idea in the whole funnel. Nobody tells the network "look for eyes." Yet a trained CNN learns to spot edges in its early layers, parts in its middle layers, and whole objects deep down — all on its own. This build-from-simple-to-complex is why CNNs work at all.

Think of drawing a face the way you learned as a kid. First you draw a few lines — short strokes. Then those lines join into shapes — a circle, two dots, a curve. Then the shapes come together into a face — the whole thing you recognize. You never draw a "face" in one stroke; you build it up: lines → shapes → face. A deep CNN builds recognition the exact same way, layer by layer. This layered building has a name: hierarchical features.

What the drawing picture gets right: simple things combine into more complex things, in stages, and each stage builds only on the stage below it. Where it breaks down: a child plans to draw a face; a CNN plans nothing — it just adjusts its stencils during training until this edge-part-object ladder happens to be the most useful way to tell classes apart.

Why depth makes the ladder possible

This ladder isn't a lucky accident — it falls straight out of two things you already know:

  • Receptive field growth (Day 2). A single 3 × 3 stencil can only see a tiny patch — enough for a short edge, nothing more. But stack blocks and each deep unit's receptive field — the patch of the original image it can react to — grows. A deep unit sees a big enough region to hold a whole ear or eye.
  • Composition. A middle layer doesn't look at raw pixels; it looks at the edge-maps the layer below produced. So it can learn "a corner = this edge meeting that edge." A deeper layer looks at those, learning "an eye = these corners and curves together." Each layer composes the one below.

Put simply: small receptive field → simple features; big receptive field → complex features. Watch the reach grow and the feature it can hold grow with it:

Read it left to right: as the receptive field climbs 3 → 7 → 15, the feature it can capture grows from a bare edge, to a part like an eye, to a whole face. This is the payoff of stacking. You don't build a face-detector by hand — you stack cheap edge-detectors and let composition build the face-detector for free. That is the whole reason a CNN is deep and not just wide.

5The bottom of the funnel: flatten, then read out one raw score per classFlatten & score

We've squeezed the picture into a small, deep stack of features. Now comes the bottom of the funnel: turn that stack into one number per possible answer. Two small steps do it — flatten, then a dense head.

Think of tidying a messy desk of index cards into a single stack, then reading them one by one to decide. First you gather all the scattered cards (from every drawer and pile) into one neat pile — you don't lose any, you just line them up. Then you read the whole pile and make one call: "cat." The first step — pouring the whole 3-D feature stack into one long line of numbers — is called flatten. It changes the shape, not the numbers.

What the index-cards picture gets right: gathering the cards into one pile loses nothing — every card is still there, just lined up so you can read them in order. Where it breaks down: you read index cards in a meaningful order; a flattened list has no special order the head cares about — the dense layer learns which entries matter, wherever they sit.

The head: one raw score per class, called logits

Now the flattened list feeds a classifier head — a small dense (fully-connected) layer, exactly the kind you built in early modules. If there are 10 possible classes, the head has 10 outputs: one number per class. Those raw output numbers have a name — logits. A bigger logit means "I favor this class more." They are not yet probabilities — just raw scores. A logit can be 8.2, or 3.7, or a hundred; there's no rule that they add up to anything tidy. Turning them into clean percentages is the very next step (softmax).

What you just saw: the head turns a pile of features into three raw scores; the biggest (cat 8.2) is the network's pick. Let's watch the shape march down the whole pipeline in one shot — pixels in, logits out — so you can see every step line up:

🪜
Math Ladder — one forward pass — a 32×32 color photo becomes 10 logits
1 · In words: pour the picture through three blocks (each shrinks the grid and grows the channels), flatten the last stack into one list, then the dense head reads it into one raw score per class
2 · The formula: image → [conv→ReLU→pool] × 3 → flatten → dense → logits
3 · Tiny numbers: 3×32×32 → block1 → 16×16×16 → block2 → 32×8×8 → block3 → 64×4×4 → flatten → 1024 → dense → 10 logits
4 · Sanity check: 64×4×4 = 1024, so the flattened list has 1024 numbers, and 10 output classes give exactly 10 logits — shapes line up end to end

Read the ladder: the picture starts 3 × 32 × 32 (3 color channels, 32 by 32 pixels). Three blocks shrink the grid and grow the channels until it's 64 × 4 × 4. Flatten multiplies those out: 64 × 4 × 4 = 1024 numbers in one list. The dense head maps 1024 → 10, giving 10 logits — one per class. You've now traced the whole funnel top to bottom. But 10 raw scores aren't yet an answer a human can read — the next concept turns them into clean percentages.

6Softmax: turn the raw scores into clean percentagesScores → percents

The head gave us raw scores like cat 8.2, dog 5.8, bird 3.7. Those tell you the order, but not "how sure." Is 8.2 a near-certain cat, or a coin-flip? We want an answer a human can read: cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%. The step that does this is called softmax, and it's the first of three small pieces that turn our funnel from a guesser into a learner.

Think of slicing one whole pizza among a few hungry friends. Everyone gets a slice, so all the slices together make exactly one whole pizza — no more, no less. The hungrier friend (bigger appetite) gets a bigger slice, and the least hungry still gets a sliver. softmax slices up "100% of the belief" the same way: the class with the biggest logit gets the biggest slice of certainty, and every slice added together makes exactly 100%.

What the pizza picture gets right: there is exactly one whole pizza (all shares add to 100%), and a bigger appetite (bigger score) means a bigger slice. Where it breaks down: pizza slices are cut by straight lines; softmax cuts by exponentials — it exaggerates gaps, so a score a little bit bigger can grab a lot more than its "fair" share (a 2× bigger score can win far more than double the slice).

From words to numbers — how softmax cuts the slices

You don't need the equation to use softmax, but here's the one line, narrated in plain words: soften each score by raising e to it (this makes every value positive), then divide by the total so the slices add to one.

🧮
softmax(scores)_i = e^(score_i) / (sum of e^(score) over all classes)
e^score makes each score positive; dividing by the total makes all outputs add up to exactly 1 (100%)

Numbers are far easier to see than to read. Predict which class wins the biggest slice, then reveal the exact percentages:

softmax([8.2, 5.8, 3.7]) # cat, dog, bird
[0.91, 0.08, 0.01]   → cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%   (they add to 1.00)
Softmax = shares of one pizza. The biggest score (cat 8.2) grabs the biggest slice (91%), and all three slices add to exactly 100%. Nothing changed about which class wins — softmax just made the confidence readable.

Notice two things: the order didn't change (cat was on top before and after), and the three percentages add to 1.00. You now have a readable answer — a probability for every class. But a probability alone doesn't tell the network how to improve. For that, we need to measure how wrong the answer is — the next piece.

7Cross-entropy: measure how wrong the guess wasHow wrong?

Softmax handed us cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%. Suppose the true answer really was "cat" — great, the model was 91% sure of the right thing, so it was only a little wrong. But if the true answer was "bird" and the model said bird 1%, it was badly wrong. We need one single number that captures "how wrong was this guess?" — small when the model is confident and right, big when it's confident and wrong. That number is called the loss, and for classification the standard one is cross-entropy.

Think of a weather forecaster and how surprised you are. If the forecaster says "99% chance of rain" and it rains, you shrug — no surprise, tiny penalty. If they say "1% chance of rain" and it pours, you're shocked — huge penalty. cross-entropy loss scores exactly that surprise: it looks only at the probability the model gave to the true class, and it grows as that probability shrinks. Confident and right → almost no loss. Confident and wrong → a giant loss.

What the forecaster picture gets right: you only care how much belief was placed on what actually happened, and being confidently wrong is punished far harder than being unsure. Where it breaks down: a forecaster's surprise is a feeling; cross-entropy is an exact number the computer can do arithmetic on — and it can shoot all the way to infinity if the model gives the true class a probability of exactly 0, something a human's surprise never does.

From words to numbers — the surprise, made exact

The one-line rule, in plain words: the loss is the negative log of the probability the model gave the true class. "Negative log" is just a knob that turns a probability near 1 into a number near 0, and a probability near 0 into a huge number — exactly the surprise shape above.

📎 Optional (skippable)
For one image with true class c, cross-entropy is L = −log( p_c ), where p_c is the softmax probability the model gave the true class. Averaging this over a batch of images gives the batch loss the training loop minimizes. You never compute this by hand in practice — the library does it — but the shape (small when p_c → 1, huge when p_c → 0) is the whole idea.

Watch the penalty change as the model's confidence in the true class rises. Predict which row has the smallest loss, then reveal:

cross_entropy(true="cat", p_cat=[0.01, 0.30, 0.91, 0.999])
p_cat=0.01 → loss 4.61   |   0.30 → loss 1.20   |   0.91 → loss 0.09   |   0.999 → loss 0.001
Loss = surprise at the true answer. When the model gives "cat" only 1% it is badly surprised (loss 4.61); when it gives "cat" 99.9% the loss is almost 0. Getting the right class more confident always drives the loss down — that's the number training will push on.

Notice: the loss falls 4.61 → 1.20 → 0.09 → 0.001 as the true-class probability climbs 1% → 30% → 91% → 99.9%. You now have one honest number for "how wrong." The last piece is the machine that uses that number to fix every weight — the training loop.

8Train end-to-end: one loop that fixes every weightLearning to see

We have all three ingredients: the funnel makes logits, softmax makes probabilities, cross-entropy makes one "how wrong" number. Now the magic: the network fixes itself. It runs one picture forward, measures the loss, sends the blame backward through every layer, and nudges each weight a hair in the direction that would have been less wrong. Do that over many pictures and the whole funnel — every conv filter and every head weight — slowly learns to see. This whole cycle is called training the network end to end.

Think of learning to shoot a basketball free throw. You take a shot (forward), see how far you missed — long and to the left (that's the "loss"), and adjust: a touch softer, aim a touch right. One shot barely changes you, but a thousand shots later your hands know the motion. A CNN learns the same way. One trip forward and one correction is one training step. Two named parts do it: backpropagation carries the blame backward, and a tiny weight update (gradient descent, from earlier modules) nudges each weight downhill on the loss.

What the free-throw picture gets right: you shoot, measure the miss, and make a small correction; one shot barely helps, but many shots teach the motion. Where it breaks down: a person consciously thinks about the fix; the network makes no plan — backpropagation is pure arithmetic that hands each weight the exact blame, and the nudge is automatic.

The loop, one lap at a time

End-to-end means one loop trains every layer at once — the conv filters that find edges and the head that reads them all learn together, from the same loss. One lap of the loop:

  1. Forward. Push a picture through the funnel → logits → softmax → probabilities.
  2. Score. Cross-entropy turns the probabilities and the true label into one loss number.
  3. Backward. Backpropagation carries that loss backward, handing each weight its share of the blame.
  4. Update. Nudge every weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss.

Repeat over thousands of pictures and the loss falls — the funnel gets better and better at seeing. Watch the loss drop as the laps pile up:

What you just saw: the loss starts high and falls fast, then flattens as the network gets good. This is the whole learning story. You now know not just how a CNN runs (forward to logits) but how it learns (softmax → cross-entropy → backprop → update, over and over). The exact math of the loss and the optimizer gets its own attention later — today you have the honest end-to-end picture.

9The bargain: far fewer weights, and it doesn't care where the cat sitsWhy it beats the old way

Back on Day 1 we met the clumsy old way — wire every pixel to every neuron — and saw it explode. Now that you've built the whole funnel and taught it to learn, you can see exactly why the CNN wins. Two reasons, and both come from one simple trick: the stencil reuses the same weights everywhere.

Think of one good rubber stamp versus a room full of custom sign-painters. A fully-connected net hires a separate painter for every single spot on the wall — millions of painters, each learning its one spot. A CNN buys one stamp and presses it everywhere. Far cheaper, and — here's the magic — because it's the same stamp, it recognizes the shape no matter where on the wall you press it. Reusing one small set of weights across the whole image is called parameter sharing.

What the stamp picture gets right: one small stamp is far cheaper than a painter-per-spot, and the same stamp recognizes its shape wherever you press it. Where it breaks down: a rubber stamp is fixed forever; a CNN's stamp is learned — the training loop you just met slowly adjusts the 9 weights until the stamp presses out exactly the pattern that helps tell classes apart.

Win 1 · the weight count stops exploding

Remember Day 1's blow-up: a 100 × 100 × 3 photo into 1,000 neurons needed 30 million weights — for one layer. A conv layer with, say, 16 filters of size 3 × 3 × 3 needs only 16 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 432 weights, no matter how big the picture is, because the same filter is reused at every spot. Watch the gap:

That green sliver is 432 next to 30,000,000. Fewer weights means less memory, faster training, and — crucially — the model needs far less data to learn, because it isn't trying to learn a fresh weight for every pixel.

Win 2 · it doesn't care where the cat is

The second win is subtler and lovely. Because the same stamp slides everywhere and pooling summarizes whole regions, the network fires for a cat whether the cat sits top-left or bottom-right. This is called translation tolerance — "translation" just means "sliding to a new position." A fully-connected net has no such gift: move the cat and completely different weights light up, so it has to re-learn the cat all over again in the new spot.

What you just saw: the cat moved, but the answer didn't — the sliding stamp finds its pattern wherever it lands. This is why CNNs took over vision. One shared, learned stamp gives you a tiny model that generalizes across position — the exact two things a fully-connected net could never do on raw pixels. But "tolerant of sliding" is not "tolerant of everything" — and knowing where that tolerance ends is the next puzzle.

10The honest limits: what a small CNN classifier still can't doWhere it stops

You built something real — but a good engineer knows exactly what their tool can't do. A small, shallow CNN classifier is powerful, yet it has clear edges. Knowing them now saves you from trusting it where it will quietly fail.

Think of a friend who can instantly name a song when it plays the normal way, but gets lost the moment you play it backwards, at half-speed, or with half the notes muted. The friend isn't dumb — they just only ever practiced the normal version. A small CNN is the same: it learned objects the way they usually appear, so it handles small shifts fine, but big twists on that — heavy rotation, big size changes, or lots of the object hidden — throw it off. These blind spots are its capability limits.

What the song-friend picture gets right: the model is great at the versions it practiced and shaky on big twists it rarely saw. Where it breaks down: a friend can reason their way through a backwards song if they think hard; a plain CNN cannot reason — it only pattern-matches what its filters learned, so an unfamiliar twist just gets a confident wrong answer.

Build up the limit, one twist at a time

Let's assemble the "not robust" limit by starting from an input the CNN nails and changing one thing at a time — watch the same cat slide past what the network can handle:

  • Step 0 — the normal cat. Upright, centered, full-size. Its trained filters match perfectly → confident cat ✓. This is the version it practiced.
  • Step 1 — slide it. Move the cat to the corner. The same sliding stamp still finds it → still cat ✓. (This is the translation win from the last concept.)
  • Step 2 — rotate it 80°. Now the edges point in directions the filters never trained on. The match weakens → cat? becomes unreliable.
  • Step 3 — shrink it to a speck. Pooling has already thrown away fine detail, and the object is now smaller than the receptive field expected → likely wrong.
  • Step 4 — hide most of it. With the body behind a box, too few of the learned parts are visible to assemble the whole → likely wrong.

Read the ladder left to right: sliding is free (steps 0–1), but each new twist — rotate, shrink, hide — chips away at the match until the confident answer is simply wrong. That is the "not robust" limit, assembled from one change at a time. And there's a second, different kind of limit:

  • **It only says what, never where. A classifier outputs one label for the whole image: "cat." It does not** draw a box around the cat, count how many cats, or say the cat is in the top-left. Tasks that need where — object detection, segmentation — need a different head, and you'll meet them on a later day.
⚠️
The quiet trap: a CNN classifier never says "I'm not sure." Show it something rotated, tiny, or half-hidden and softmax still returns a confident label — just possibly the wrong one. Treat a single label as a guess, not a certainty, especially on inputs unlike its training pictures.

**You now know the map and its edges.** This honesty is what separates someone who uses a model from someone who understands it. The good news: the "not robust" limit has a direct fix — show the network more variety while it learns — and that fix, plus two other things that can go wrong when you stack too eagerly, are the next concept.

11The stacking traps: three puzzles, and the fix for eachThree things that break

Stacking blocks is powerful, but if you stack carelessly three things can go wrong. The good news: each one is a well-understood puzzle with a known fix. Meet all three now so none of them ever surprises you.

Think of building a tall tower of blocks on the carpet. Three ways it can go wrong: (1) you glue the blocks so perfectly to today's exact wobble that a tiny nudge topples it — too specialized; (2) a message you tap at the top fades to nothing before it reaches the bottom blocks — the base never gets the memo; (3) you keep trimming each block a little and, a few floors up, two pieces no longer line up — the shapes stop fitting. Same three traps hit a deep CNN. Let's take them one at a time.

What the tower picture gets right: all three failures come from building too eagerly — over-fitting the exact blocks, losing the signal over height, or trimming until pieces mismatch. Where it breaks down: a block tower fails visibly (it falls); these three often fail quietly — the numbers still run, they just don't learn well — which is why you learn to spot them by name.

Trap 1 · Overfitting — it memorizes instead of learning

A model with lots of weights and too little data can just memorize the training pictures — like a student who memorizes the exact answers instead of learning the subject. It aces the pictures it saw and flops on new ones. That gap is called overfitting. The fix: give it less room to memorize and more variety to learn from — the named tools are data augmentation (show flipped/cropped/color-shifted copies so it never sees the same picture twice), dropout (randomly switch off some units during training so it can't rely on memorizing), weight decay (gently push weights toward small values), and simply more data or a smaller model. You'll meet augmentation and dropout properly on later days.

Trap 2 · Vanishing gradients — the deep stack stops learning

Remember the backward pass from the training loop? A correction signal travels backwards from the loss down through every layer. In a deep stack, each layer multiplies that signal by a small slope, and small × small × small… shrinks toward zero. By the time it reaches the early layers, there's almost nothing left — so the first layers barely learn. This fade is called vanishing gradients. Watch the fade — a signal of 1.0 multiplied by a 0.25 slope at each of five layers:

Read the ladder: 1.0 → 0.25 → 0.06 → 0.016 → 0.004. By layer 1 the signal is 0.004 — nearly gone, so the bottom layer hardly changes. The fix is to stop the signal from shrinking so fast: ReLU activations (its slope is a flat 1 for positive values, not a tiny fraction) and normalization — the named technique batch normalization keeps each layer's numbers in a healthy range so slopes stay reasonable. (That's literally tomorrow's lesson.) Deeper stacks add residual (skip) connections, which you'll meet later.

Trap 3 · Shape collapse — the grids stop lining up

Every conv or pool nibbles the grid smaller (Day 2). Stack enough "valid" (no-border) layers and the map shrinks to a 1 × 1 nub — or worse, to a size the next layer's kernel can't even fit on, and your code errors out. This is shape collapse. The fix is the tape-measure from Day 2: use padding ("same") to stop the shrinking where you don't want it, and track the shape at every layer so you catch a mismatch on paper before you run.

You now hold the three traps and their fixes. None of them is scary once named: overfit → add variety and hold back capacity; vanishing signal → ReLU + normalization; shape collapse → padding + track shapes. Every one of these fixes is a full lesson coming up — you've just met the whole map of "what could go wrong and what to do."

The day in a few beats:

  • The funnel. A CNN classifier = a feature extractor (conv/pool blocks that squeeze pixels into features) + a classifier head (a dense layer that reads features into one raw score per class).
  • The block. The repeating unit is conv → ReLU → pool: find patterns, add a bend, shrink. Stack it to build depth.
  • Thin but deep. Down the funnel, spatial size shrinks while channels (feature-detector stacks) grow. Depth is a separate ruler: channels = number of filters.
  • Edges to objects. Early layers spot edges; deeper layers, with bigger receptive fields, compose them into parts and whole objects — for free, from stacking.
  • Flatten & score. Flatten the final 3-D stack into one list, feed the head, get one logit (raw score) per class.
  • Scores → percents → learning. Softmax turns the logits into probabilities (slices of one pizza); cross-entropy measures how wrong they are (your surprise at the true class); and one training loop — forward, score, backprop the blame, nudge every weight — teaches the whole funnel end to end.
  • Why it beats the old way. Parameter sharing (one small stamp reused everywhere) slashes the weight count and gives translation tolerance — it finds the cat wherever it sits.
  • The limits. A small CNN isn't robust to big rotations, scale changes, or heavy occlusion, and it only says what, never where.
  • The three traps. Overfitting → augmentation/dropout/weight-decay; vanishing gradients → ReLU + batch-norm (+ residuals); shape collapse → padding + track shapes.
