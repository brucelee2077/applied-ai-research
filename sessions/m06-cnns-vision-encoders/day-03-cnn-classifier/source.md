**What the funnel picture gets right:** it goes wide-to-narrow, one direction, squeezing a big input down to one small output, and nothing important is lost on the way. **Where it breaks down:** a kitchen funnel only *moves* stuff, unchanged; this funnel *transforms* what flows through it — it doesn't just shrink the picture, it turns raw pixels into meaningful features, and then into an answer.

#### The two halves of every classifier
Every CNN classifier has exactly two parts, and it helps to name them now:

- The [[feature extractor||The stack of conv → ReLU → pool blocks at the top of a CNN. Its job is to turn raw pixels into useful features — edges, textures, parts, whole-object patterns.]] — the wide-to-narrow stack of conv/pool blocks. Its job: turn raw pixels into *features* (useful patterns like edges, then shapes, then "this looks like a cat's ear").
- The [[classifier head||The small dense (fully-connected) layer(s) at the bottom of a CNN. It reads the extracted features and outputs one score per class.]] — the small part at the narrow bottom. Its job: read those features and produce one score for each possible answer.

Feature extractor squeezes; head decides. That two-part shape is the *whole* design, and it is the thing you'll build today. And once it's built, three small extra pieces — which you'll meet in a moment — will teach the whole funnel to *learn*.

**What the LEGO picture gets right:** the whole tower is the same simple unit repeated, so once you understand one brick you understand the stack; and the order inside the brick is fixed. **Where it breaks down:** LEGO bricks are identical, but each block *learns its own* stencils, so block 2's filters are different from block 1's — same shape, different contents.

#### What each of the three steps does
You've met all three parts already. The block just puts them in a fixed order:

1. **Convolution** — slide the learned stencils across the incoming grid to make a stack of "found-it" maps. This is the pattern-spotting step.
2. **ReLU** — a [[ReLU||A tiny bend applied to every number: keeps positives, turns negatives into 0. It is written max(0, z). Without a bend, stacking layers buys nothing.]] bend on every number: keep positives, zero out negatives. Without this little bend, stacking many conv layers would collapse into one — the bend is what lets depth actually add power.
3. **Pooling** — shrink each map by summarizing small windows (e.g. keep the biggest value in every `2 × 2` square). This throws away exact positions but keeps *what* was found.

Why this exact order? Convolution finds patterns, the bend lets the network learn something a straight line can't, and pooling shrinks the result so the next block has less to chew on. Watch one block transform a small grid, step by step:

@@@ concept id=c3 tag="Thin but deep" title="The trade: the picture gets smaller but the stack gets thicker" gotit="Got thin-but-deep"
As the funnel squeezes, something surprising happens to the *shape*. The picture gets smaller across (fewer pixels), but *thicker* in a hidden direction (more stacked grids). Understanding this trade is the key to reading any CNN's shapes.

Think of **squeezing a wide, floppy sponge into a tall thin glass**. The sponge gets narrower — it no longer covers the table. But it also gets *taller* — it stacks up inside the glass. It's not gone; it's re-shaped: less wide, more deep. A feature map does the same as it goes down the funnel: its height and width shrink, but its [[channels||The number of stacked grids in a feature map (its depth). Each channel is one filter's found-it map — one kind of pattern the layer looks for. Also called feature maps.]] — the number of stacked grids, each looking for one kind of pattern — grow. Thinner across, deeper down.

**What the sponge picture gets right:** the total "stuff" is roughly kept — it just moves from wide-and-flat to narrow-and-tall as you squeeze. **Where it breaks down:** a sponge shrinks *passively* when you squeeze; a CNN grows its depth on *purpose* — you choose how many stencils (filters) each conv layer has, and that choice sets the new depth.

#### Why grow channels while shrinking pixels?
This is the deep bargain of a CNN, and it's worth saying plainly:

- **Shrinking pixels** (via pooling or striding, from Day 2) means each deeper unit covers a *bigger* chunk of the original picture — its receptive field grows. Deeper layers see the *big picture*, not single dots.
- **Growing channels** means the layer can look for *more kinds* of pattern at once. Early on you only need a few detectors (a handful of edge directions). Deep down you need many (ear-shaped things, eye-shaped things, whisker-shaped things), so you add more channels.

Each channel is one filter's [[found-it map||The single grid a convolution filter produces — one number per position saying how strongly that filter's pattern appears there. Also called a feature map or an activation map.]]: one grid saying "here is where *my* pattern shows up." Watch the trade play out across three blocks — pixels fall, channels climb:

@@@ concept id=c4 tag="Edges to objects" title="The ladder: early layers see edges, deep layers see whole things" gotit="Got the hierarchy"
Here is the most beautiful idea in the whole funnel. Nobody tells the network "look for eyes." Yet a trained CNN learns to spot edges in its early layers, parts in its middle layers, and whole objects deep down — all on its own. This build-from-simple-to-complex is why CNNs work at all.

Think of drawing a face the way you learned as a kid. First you draw a few **lines** — short strokes. Then those lines join into **shapes** — a circle, two dots, a curve. Then the shapes come together into a **face** — the whole thing you recognize. You never draw a "face" in one stroke; you build it up: lines → shapes → face. A deep CNN builds recognition the exact same way, layer by layer. This layered building has a name: [[hierarchical features||The way a CNN builds understanding in stages: early layers detect simple things (edges), middle layers combine them into parts, deep layers combine those into whole objects.]].

**What the drawing picture gets right:** simple things combine into more complex things, in stages, and each stage builds only on the stage below it. **Where it breaks down:** a child *plans* to draw a face; a CNN plans nothing — it just adjusts its stencils during training until this edge-part-object ladder happens to be the most useful way to tell classes apart.

#### Why depth *makes* the ladder possible
This ladder isn't a lucky accident — it falls straight out of two things you already know:

- **Receptive field growth (Day 2).** A single `3 × 3` stencil can only see a tiny patch — enough for a short edge, nothing more. But stack blocks and each deep unit's [[receptive field||The patch of the original image one deep unit can 'see'. It starts one kernel wide and grows with every block you stack, so deep units can react to big things.]] — the patch of the original image it can react to — grows. A deep unit sees a big enough region to hold a whole ear or eye.
- **Composition.** A middle layer doesn't look at raw pixels; it looks at the *edge-maps* the layer below produced. So it can learn "a corner = this edge meeting that edge." A deeper layer looks at *those*, learning "an eye = these corners and curves together." Each layer composes the one below.

Put simply: **small receptive field → simple features; big receptive field → complex features.** Watch the reach grow and the feature it can hold grow with it:

That is the whole reason a CNN is *deep* and not just wide.

@@@ concept id=c5 tag="Flatten & score" title="The bottom of the funnel: flatten, then read out one raw score per class" gotit="Got flatten & head"
We've squeezed the picture into a small, deep stack of features. Now comes the bottom of the funnel: turn that stack into one number per possible answer. Two small steps do it — *flatten*, then a *dense head*.

Think of tidying a messy desk of index cards into a single **stack, then reading them one by one** to decide. First you gather all the scattered cards (from every drawer and pile) into one neat pile — you don't lose any, you just line them up. Then you read the whole pile and make one call: "cat." The first step — pouring the whole 3-D feature stack into one long line of numbers — is called [[flatten||Reshaping the final 3-D feature stack (channels × height × width) into one long 1-D list of numbers, so a dense layer can read it. No numbers are added or removed.]]. It changes the *shape*, not the numbers.

#### The head: one raw score per class, called logits
Now the flattened list feeds a [[classifier head||One or more dense (fully-connected) layers that read the flattened features and output one number per class.]] — a small dense (fully-connected) layer, exactly the kind you built in early modules. If there are 10 possible classes, the head has 10 outputs: one number per class. Those raw output numbers have a name — [[logits||The raw, unbounded per-class scores a network outputs before they are turned into probabilities. A higher logit means the network favors that class more.]]. A bigger logit means "I favor this class more." They are *not yet probabilities* — just raw scores. A logit can be `8.2`, or `3.7`, or a hundred; there's no rule that they add up to anything tidy. Turning them into clean percentages is the very next step (softmax).

The dense head maps `1024 → 10`, giving 10 logits — one per class. **You've now traced the whole funnel top to bottom.** But 10 raw scores aren't yet an answer a human can read — the next concept turns them into clean percentages.

@@@ concept id=c6 tag="Scores → percents" title="Softmax: turn the raw scores into clean percentages" gotit="Got softmax"
The head gave us raw scores like `cat 8.2, dog 5.8, bird 3.7`. Those tell you the *order*, but not "how sure." Is `8.2` a near-certain cat, or a coin-flip? We want an answer a human can read: `cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%`. The step that does this is called **softmax**, and it's the first of three small pieces that turn our funnel from a *guesser* into a *learner*.

Think of slicing **one whole pizza among a few hungry friends**. Everyone gets a slice, so all the slices *together* make exactly one whole pizza — no more, no less. The hungrier friend (bigger appetite) gets a bigger slice, and the least hungry still gets a sliver. [[softmax||A step that turns a list of raw scores (logits) into probabilities: every output is between 0 and 1, and they all add up to 1. A bigger score gets a bigger share.]] slices up "100% of the belief" the same way: the class with the biggest logit gets the biggest slice of certainty, and every slice added together makes exactly `100%`.

**What the pizza picture gets right:** there is exactly one whole pizza (all shares add to 100%), and a bigger appetite (bigger score) means a bigger slice. **Where it breaks down:** pizza slices are cut by straight lines; softmax cuts by *exponentials* — it exaggerates gaps, so a score a little bit bigger can grab a *lot* more than its "fair" share (a `2×` bigger score can win far more than double the slice).

#### From words to numbers — how softmax cuts the slices
You don't need the equation to use softmax, but here's the one line, narrated in plain words: soften each score by raising `e` to it (this makes every value positive), then divide by the total so the slices add to one.

%%% formula
expr: softmax(scores)_i = e^(score_i) / (sum of e^(score) over all classes)
note: e^score makes each score positive; dividing by the total makes all outputs add up to exactly 1 (100%)
%%%

Numbers are far easier to *see* than to read. Predict which class wins the biggest slice, then reveal the exact percentages:

%%% demo id=softmax label="turn scores into percentages"
code: softmax([8.2, 5.8, 3.7])   # cat, dog, bird
out: [0.91, 0.08, 0.01]   → cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%   (they add to 1.00)
take: <b>Softmax = shares of one pizza.</b> The biggest score (cat 8.2) grabs the biggest slice (91%), and all three slices add to exactly 100%. Nothing changed about which class wins — softmax just made the confidence readable.
%%%

**Notice two things:** the *order* didn't change (cat was on top before and after), and the three percentages add to `1.00`. **You now have a readable answer** — a probability for every class. But a probability alone doesn't tell the network how to *improve*. For that, we need to measure how *wrong* the answer is — the next piece.

@@@ concept id=c7 tag="How wrong?" title="Cross-entropy: measure how wrong the guess was" gotit="Got the loss"
Softmax handed us `cat 91%, dog 8%, bird 1%`. Suppose the true answer really *was* "cat" — great, the model was 91% sure of the right thing, so it was only a *little* wrong. But if the true answer was "bird" and the model said `bird 1%`, it was *badly* wrong. We need one single number that captures "how wrong was this guess?" — small when the model is confident and right, big when it's confident and wrong. That number is called the **loss**, and for classification the standard one is **cross-entropy**.

Think of a **weather forecaster and how surprised you are**. If the forecaster says "99% chance of rain" and it rains, you shrug — no surprise, tiny penalty. If they say "1% chance of rain" and it pours, you're *shocked* — huge penalty. [[cross-entropy loss||A single number that measures how wrong a probability guess was: it is small when the model puts high probability on the true class, and grows large — toward infinity — as the probability on the true class drops toward zero.]] scores exactly that surprise: it looks *only* at the probability the model gave to the **true** class, and it grows as that probability shrinks. Confident and right → almost no loss. Confident and wrong → a giant loss.

**What the forecaster picture gets right:** you only care how much belief was placed on *what actually happened*, and being confidently wrong is punished far harder than being unsure. **Where it breaks down:** a forecaster's surprise is a feeling; cross-entropy is an exact number the computer can do arithmetic on — and it can shoot all the way to *infinity* if the model gives the true class a probability of exactly 0, something a human's surprise never does.

#### From words to numbers — the surprise, made exact
The one-line rule, in plain words: the loss is the *negative log* of the probability the model gave the true class. "Negative log" is just a knob that turns a probability near 1 into a number near 0, and a probability near 0 into a huge number — exactly the surprise shape above.

!!! c-info 📎 Optional (skippable)
For one image with true class `c`, cross-entropy is `L = −log( p_c )`, where `p_c` is the softmax probability the model gave the true class. Averaging this over a batch of images gives the batch loss the training loop minimizes. You never compute this by hand in practice — the library does it — but the shape (small when `p_c → 1`, huge when `p_c → 0`) is the whole idea.
!!!

Watch the penalty change as the model's confidence in the *true* class rises. Predict which row has the smallest loss, then reveal:

%%% demo id=xent label="see the penalty shrink as the model gets it right"
code: cross_entropy(true="cat", p_cat=[0.01, 0.30, 0.91, 0.999])
out: p_cat=0.01 → loss 4.61   |   0.30 → loss 1.20   |   0.91 → loss 0.09   |   0.999 → loss 0.001
take: <b>Loss = surprise at the true answer.</b> When the model gives "cat" only 1% it is badly surprised (loss 4.61); when it gives "cat" 99.9% the loss is almost 0. Getting the right class more confident always drives the loss down — that's the number training will push on.
%%%

**Notice:** the loss falls `4.61 → 1.20 → 0.09 → 0.001` as the true-class probability climbs `1% → 30% → 91% → 99.9%`. **You now have one honest number for "how wrong."** The last piece is the machine that *uses* that number to fix every weight — the training loop.

@@@ concept id=c8 tag="Learning to see" title="Train end-to-end: one loop that fixes every weight" gotit="Got training"
We have all three ingredients: the funnel makes logits, softmax makes probabilities, cross-entropy makes one "how wrong" number. Now the magic: the network *fixes itself*. It runs one picture forward, measures the loss, sends the blame *backward* through every layer, and nudges each weight a hair in the direction that would have been less wrong. Do that over many pictures and the whole funnel — every conv filter and every head weight — slowly learns to see. This whole cycle is called **training the network end to end**.

Think of learning to shoot a **basketball free throw**. You take a shot (forward), see how far you missed — long and to the left (that's the "loss"), and adjust: a touch softer, aim a touch right. One shot barely changes you, but a thousand shots later your hands *know* the motion. A CNN learns the same way. One trip forward and one correction is one [[training step||One cycle of learning: run a picture forward to get the loss, send the error backward to get each weight's blame, then nudge every weight a little to lower the loss.]]. Two named parts do it: [[backpropagation||The backward pass: it hands each weight the blame for the loss — how much that weight pushed the answer the wrong way — by carrying the error signal back through every layer.]] carries the blame backward, and a tiny **weight update** (gradient descent, from earlier modules) nudges each weight downhill on the loss.

#### The loop, one lap at a time
End-to-end means *one* loop trains *every* layer at once — the conv filters that find edges and the head that reads them all learn together, from the same loss. One lap of the loop:

1. **Forward.** Push a picture through the funnel → logits → softmax → probabilities.
2. **Score.** Cross-entropy turns the probabilities and the true label into one loss number.
3. **Backward.** Backpropagation carries that loss backward, handing each weight its share of the blame.
4. **Update.** Nudge every weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss.

Repeat over thousands of pictures and the loss falls — the funnel gets better and better at seeing. Watch the loss drop as the laps pile up:

@@@ concept id=c9 tag="Why it beats the old way" title="The bargain: far fewer weights, and it doesn't care where the cat sits" gotit="Got the win"
Back on Day 1 we met the clumsy old way — wire every pixel to every neuron — and saw it explode. Now that you've built the whole funnel *and* taught it to learn, you can see *exactly* why the CNN wins. Two reasons, and both come from one simple trick: the stencil reuses the same weights everywhere.

Think of one good **rubber stamp** versus a room full of custom sign-painters. A fully-connected net hires a separate painter for every single spot on the wall — millions of painters, each learning its one spot. A CNN buys *one stamp* and presses it everywhere. Far cheaper, and — here's the magic — because it's the *same* stamp, it recognizes the shape no matter *where* on the wall you press it. Reusing one small set of weights across the whole image is called [[parameter sharing||Using the same small set of filter weights at every position of the image, instead of a separate weight for every pixel. It slashes the weight count and makes the network care about a pattern, not its location.]].

#### Win 2 · it doesn't care *where* the cat is
The second win is subtler and lovely. Because the same stamp slides everywhere and pooling summarizes whole regions, the network fires for a cat whether the cat sits top-left or bottom-right. This is called [[translation tolerance||A CNN still recognizes an object when it shifts to a different position in the image, because the same filter slides everywhere and pooling summarizes regions. A fully-connected net has no such gift: move the cat and completely different weights light up, so it has to re-learn the cat all over again in the new spot.

But "tolerant of *sliding*" is not "tolerant of *everything*" — and knowing where that tolerance ends is the next puzzle.

@@@ concept id=c10 tag="Where it stops" title="The honest limits: what a small CNN classifier still can't do" gotit="Got the limits"
You built something real — but a good engineer knows exactly what their tool *can't* do. A small, shallow CNN classifier is powerful, yet it has clear edges. Knowing them now saves you from trusting it where it will quietly fail.

Think of a friend who can instantly name a song when it plays **the normal way**, but gets lost the moment you play it **backwards, at half-speed, or with half the notes muted**. The friend isn't dumb — they just only ever practiced the normal version. A small CNN is the same: it learned objects the way they usually appear, so it handles small shifts fine, but big *twists* on that — heavy rotation, big size changes, or lots of the object hidden — throw it off. These blind spots are its [[capability limits||The things a model reliably cannot do. For a small CNN classifier: it is not robust to big rotations, large scale changes, or heavy occlusion, and it only outputs a class — never where the object is.]].

**What the song-friend picture gets right:** the model is great at the versions it practiced and shaky on big twists it rarely saw. **Where it breaks down:** a friend can *reason* their way through a backwards song if they think hard; a plain CNN cannot reason — it only pattern-matches what its filters learned, so an unfamiliar twist just gets a confident wrong answer.

#### Build up the limit, one twist at a time
Let's assemble the "not robust" limit by starting from an input the CNN *nails* and changing one thing at a time — watch the same cat slide past what the network can handle:

- **Step 0 — the normal cat.** Upright, centered, full-size. Its trained filters match perfectly → confident `cat ✓`. This is the version it practiced.
- **Step 1 — slide it.** Move the cat to the corner. The *same* sliding stamp still finds it → still `cat ✓`. (This is the translation win from the last concept.)
- **Step 2 — rotate it 80°.** Now the edges point in directions the filters never trained on. The match weakens → `cat?` becomes unreliable.
- **Step 3 — shrink it to a speck.** Pooling has already thrown away fine detail, and the object is now smaller than the receptive field expected → likely wrong.
- **Step 4 — hide most of it.** With the body behind a box, too few of the learned parts are visible to assemble the whole → likely wrong.

Read the ladder left to right: sliding is free (steps 0–1), but each new twist — rotate, shrink, hide — chips away at the match until the confident answer is simply wrong. **That is the "not robust" limit, assembled from one change at a time.** And there's a second, different kind of limit:

- **It only says *what*, never *where*.** A classifier outputs one label for the whole image: "cat." It does **not** draw a box around the cat, count how many cats, or say the cat is in the top-left. Tasks that need *where* — object detection, segmentation — need a different head, and you'll meet them on a later day.

!!! c-warn ⚠️
<b>The quiet trap:</b> a CNN classifier never says "I'm not sure." Show it something rotated, tiny, or half-hidden and softmax still returns a confident label — just possibly the wrong one. Treat a single label as a *guess*, not a certainty, especially on inputs unlike its training pictures.
!!!

**You now know the map *and* its edges.** This honesty is what separates someone who uses a model from someone who understands it. The good news: the "not robust" limit has a direct fix — show the network more variety while it learns — and that fix, plus two other things that can go wrong when you stack too eagerly, are the next concept.

@@@ concept id=c11 tag="Three things that break" title="The stacking traps: three puzzles, and the fix for each" gotit="Got the fixes"

#### Trap 1 · Overfitting — it memorizes instead of learning
A model with lots of weights and too little data can just **memorize** the training pictures — like a student who memorizes the exact answers instead of learning the subject. It aces the pictures it saw and flops on new ones. That gap is called [[overfitting||When a model fits the training pictures so exactly it fails on new ones — it memorized instead of learning the general pattern. Caused by too many weights for too little data.]]. The fix: give it *less room to memorize and more variety to learn from* — the named tools are **data augmentation** (show flipped/cropped/color-shifted copies so it never sees the same picture twice), **dropout** (randomly switch off some units during training so it can't rely on memorizing), **weight decay** (gently push weights toward small values), and simply *more data* or a *smaller model*. You'll meet augmentation and dropout properly on later days.

#### Trap 2 · Vanishing gradients — the deep stack stops learning
Remember the backward pass from the training loop? A correction signal travels *backwards* from the loss down through every layer. In a deep stack, each layer multiplies that signal by a small slope, and small × small × small… shrinks toward zero. By the time it reaches the early layers, there's almost nothing left — so the first layers barely learn. This fade is called [[vanishing gradients||In a deep stack, the learning signal is multiplied by a small number at each layer, so it shrinks toward zero before reaching the early layers — which then barely learn.]]. Watch the fade — a signal of `1.0` multiplied by a `0.25` slope at each of five layers:

#### Trap 3 · Shape collapse — the grids stop lining up
Every conv or pool nibbles the grid smaller (Day 2). Stack enough "valid" (no-border) layers and the map shrinks to a `1 × 1` nub — or worse, to a size the next layer's kernel can't even fit on, and your code errors out. This is [[shape collapse||When repeated pooling and no-padding convolutions shrink the feature map until it is too small for the next layer, or until dimensions no longer line up.]]. The fix is the tape-measure from Day 2: use **padding** ("same") to stop the shrinking where you don't want it, and **track the shape at every layer** so you catch a mismatch on paper before you run.

The day in a few beats:
- **The funnel.** A CNN classifier = a **feature extractor** (conv/pool blocks that squeeze pixels into features) + a **classifier head** (a dense layer that reads features into one raw score per class).
- **The block.** The repeating unit is **conv → ReLU → pool**: find patterns, add a bend, shrink. Stack it to build depth.
- **Thin but deep.** Down the funnel, spatial size *shrinks* while **channels** (feature-detector stacks) *grow*. Depth is a separate ruler: channels = number of filters.
- **Edges to objects.** Early layers spot edges; deeper layers, with bigger **receptive fields**, compose them into parts and whole objects — for free, from stacking.
- **Flatten & score.** **Flatten** the final 3-D stack into one list, feed the **head**, get one **logit** (raw score) per class.
- **Scores → percents → learning.** **Softmax** turns the logits into probabilities (slices of one pizza); **cross-entropy** measures how wrong they are (your surprise at the true class); and one **training loop** — forward, score, backprop the blame, nudge every weight — teaches the whole funnel end to end.
- **Why it beats the old way.** **Parameter sharing** (one small stamp reused everywhere) slashes the weight count and gives **translation tolerance** — it finds the cat wherever it sits.
- **The limits.** A small CNN isn't robust to big rotations, scale changes, or heavy occlusion, and it only says *what*, never *where*.
- **The three traps.** Overfitting → augmentation/dropout/weight-decay; vanishing gradients → ReLU + batch-norm (+ residuals); shape collapse → padding + track shapes.

training loop | forward → loss → backpropagation → weight update, repeated over many pictures
backpropagation | the backward pass that hands each weight its share of the blame for the loss

overfitting | memorizing training pics instead of learning; fix with augmentation / dropout / weight decay
vanishing gradients | the learning signal fades over a deep stack; fix with ReLU + normalization
shape collapse | the map shrinks until layers no longer fit; fix with padding + tracking shapes
