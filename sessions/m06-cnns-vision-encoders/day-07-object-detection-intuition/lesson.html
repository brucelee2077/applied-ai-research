Module 1 · Represent · Day 3

Object DetectionIntuition

A classifier answers one question: "what is in this image?" But many real tasks need more — "what, and where?" A self-driving car must find every pedestrian and put a box around each one. Object detection does this: it draws boxes and labels each. Today is an awareness day. You will get the big picture and the two main styles, no heavy math.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain the jump from classification ("what") to detection ("what and where" with boxes), and describe the difference between one-stage and two-stage detectors at a high level.
1From "what" to "what and where"What is it
🧭
What this assumes: the CNN classifier from Day 3 (what is in the image) and the feature-extractor idea from Day 6. Today we add location. This is an awareness day — the goal is the shape of the idea, not the full math.

Classification vs detection

A classifier gives one label for the whole picture: "cat". Object detection finds every object, draws a box around each, and labels it: "cat here, dog there, ball in the corner".

The two extra outputs

For each object, a detector predicts two things:

  • A bounding box — a rectangle saying where the object is.
  • A class label with a confidence score — what the object is, and how sure the model is.

Two families of detectors

There are two common designs, differing in how they find the boxes:

  • A two-stage detector first proposes likely regions, then classifies each. Slower, often more accurate.
  • A one-stage detector predicts boxes and labels in one pass over the image. Faster, great for real time.

Both reuse a CNN backbone — the feature extractor from Day 6 — as their starting point. Keep going 👇

2Not just naming animals — circling themIntuition
🏷️
Naming (classification)

Imagine a page in a picture book. A classifier says "this page is about a farm". True, but it does not tell you the cow is on the left and the duck is by the pond.

✏️
Circling (detection)

A detector takes a pen and circles each animal, writing its name next to the circle: "cow", "duck", "dog". Now you know what is there AND where. That is the jump from classification to detection.

In one line: classification names the whole picture; detection circles and names every object inside it.

The two detector styles are two ways to do the circling. A two-stage detector first sketches rough circles where something might be, then checks each carefully. A one-stage detector circles and names everything in a single sweep — faster, which is why real-time systems like a car's camera use it.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: you circle only what catches your eye. A detector must consider many possible boxes everywhere, then throw away the overlapping and low-confidence ones (a cleanup step called non-max suppression). It is more systematic and less selective than a human with a pen.

4What a box is, and how "good" is measuredMechanism & why

Step 1 — in plain words

Each object gets a rectangle. The detector predicts the rectangle plus a class and a confidence. To grade a predicted box against the true box, we measure how much they overlap. More overlap = better box.

Step 2 — the overlap score (IoU)

🧮
IoU = area(overlap) / area(union)
# Intersection over Union; between 0 and 1

Symbols: IoU = overlap area divided by the combined area. A score of 1 means the predicted box sits exactly on the true box; 0 means they do not touch. Detectors usually count a box as correct when IoU is above a threshold, like 0.5.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say the true box and the predicted box each cover an area of 100, and they overlap in an area of 40:

🔢
overlap = 40
union = 100 + 100 − 40 = 160 # subtract the shared part once
IoU = 40 / 160 = 0.25

IoU = 0.25 is below a 0.5 threshold, so this box would be judged a poor match. If the boxes lined up better — say overlap 90 — the IoU would rise to 90/(100+100−90) = 90/110 ≈ 0.82, a good match.

One-stage vs two-stage — the trade-off

DesignHowTrade-off
Two-stage (e.g. Faster R-CNN)propose regions, then classify eachoften more accurate, slower
One-stage (e.g. YOLO)predict boxes + labels in one passfaster, great for real time
🔗
The big picture: detection reuses the CNN backbone you already understand and adds a "where" head on top. This is awareness-level — you do not need the full loss math, just the shape of the problem and the two families.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Even the simple IoU score has a quiet trap in how boxes are written down, and the one-stage vs two-stage choice is a real design decision.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): mixing up the box coordinate format. A box can be written as (x1, y1, x2, y2) — two opposite corners — or as (center_x, center_y, width, height). Both are four numbers. If your IoU code assumes corners but the boxes arrive as center-and-size (or the other way round), the math still returns a number between 0 and 1, so nothing errors. But it is the overlap of the wrong rectangles, so good predictions get scored as misses and your reported accuracy is quietly wrong. A senior engineer, in code review, checks that every box entering an IoU or loss function is in the one agreed format, and converts explicitly at each boundary.
⚖️
Trade-off: one-stage detectors buy speed, two-stage buy accuracy. A one-stage detector (like YOLO) predicts boxes and labels in a single pass, which is fast enough for real-time video but tends to be weaker on small or crowded objects. A two-stage detector (like Faster R-CNN) first proposes regions and then classifies each, which is more accurate but slower and heavier. Which family fits — real-time speed versus top accuracy under a latency and hardware budget — is the design-review decision.
