Inference Economics & Language-Assist · Week 6 · Day 1

Inference MechanicsPrefill vs. Decode

A trained transformer answers a prompt in two mechanically different phases: prefill reads your whole prompt in one big matrix-multiply pass, then decode writes the answer one token at a time, leaning on a growing KV cache. Today: why that split exists, and why it decides where the money goes when you serve a model.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain prefill vs. decode as different matrix shapes (matrix×matrix vs. matrix×vector), compute arithmetic intensity (FLOPs/byte) for each phase, explain why the KV cache exists, and connect this to real numbers from Pope et al. (2022) on PaLM 540B serving.
1What is inference, and why does it split in two?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you know what a transformer forward pass roughly does (matrix multiplies through layers), and you've seen the Roofline model from earlier scaling-law weeks (FLOPs vs. bytes moved, compute-bound vs. memory-bound). No prior serving/inference experience needed.

Training vs. inference — a quick reminder

Inference is the phase where you use an already-trained model to produce outputs. This is different from training, where the model's weights get updated. Today is entirely about inference.

Here's the surprising part: for an autoregressive transformer (one that predicts the next token, then the next, then the next), a single inference call is not one computation. It is two mechanically different sub-computations glued together.

Part 1: Prefill — reading your prompt

Prefill is the model reading your entire prompt at once — all T input tokens together — and computing, for every layer, the hidden states plus something new: the KV cache (the Key and Value vectors that attention will need on every later step). This happens once per prompt. Shape-wise, an activation matrix of shape [T, d_model] hits weight matrices of shape [d_model, d_model]. That's matrix times matrix.

Part 2: Decode — writing the answer, one token at a time

Decode (also called generation or autoregressive sampling) produces the output one new token per step. Each step takes just the newest token's activation — a [1, d_model] row vector — multiplies it by the same weight matrices, and appends that token's new K/V vectors onto the growing KV cache before attending over all of them. That's matrix times vector. Generating 100 tokens means running this small step 100 times in a row, each one depending on the previous one's output.

Why the KV cache exists

The KV cache is a piece of memory — not a matrix multiply — that stores every previous token's Key and Value vectors so decode never has to recompute them. Without it, producing token 500 would mean redoing all the attention work for tokens 1–499, even though those never change once they're computed. That's wasteful, so we cache them.

So what's today's catch?

Here's the twist that surprises people: because decode only processes one token's worth of activation per step, but still has to load the entire KV cache and the entire set of weight matrices from memory (HBM) every single step, decode's bottleneck is not "how much math can the chip do" — it's "how fast can we move bytes." That's why prefill and decode need completely different optimization strategies in production.

😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 同样的权重、同样的硬件，prefill 和 decode 的瓶颈却完全相反——一个卡在算力，一个卡在带宽。In plain terms: people assume one inference call has one bottleneck, so they benchmark a long prompt (mostly prefill, compute-bound, fast) and quote that speed — then real users, who mostly wait on decode (memory-bound, slow per token), find it sluggish. Seeing one call, two opposite regimes is the unlock.

2A translator reading, then dictatingIntuition

Picture a translator working through a stack of foreign documents.

📖
Prefill = reading and indexing

The translator sits down with your entire 20-page document and reads it front to back in one continuous pass, building a mental index of what every paragraph means and how paragraphs relate. Intensive, sustained brainwork that scales with how much text there is — lots of "compute."

🗣️
Decode = dictating, one word at a time

Now the translator speaks the translation aloud, one word per breath. Before every single word, they glance back at their entire index to stay consistent — even though the index itself never changes. Most of the work per word is the glance-back (fetching information), not deciding the word.

In one line: prefill is heavy, sustained thinking over the whole document at once; decode is a tiny decision per word, dominated by the cost of checking back over everything read so far.

直觉 / Intuition: prefill 像一口气读完整份文件（算得多），decode 像逐字口述、每个字前都要回看笔记（搬得多）。In systems terms: prefill is one big matrix×matrix (many FLOPs per byte of weights loaded → compute-bound); decode is matrix×vector (one row against the same weights → almost no FLOPs per byte loaded → memory-bandwidth-bound).

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a human translator's "index" stays roughly a fixed size in their head no matter how long the document is. The transformer's KV cache is not fixed size — it literally grows with every token read or generated, so the "glance-back" cost keeps increasing as the conversation gets longer, unlike a human's roughly constant recall time.

4The roofline verdict, and why it mattersHow & why

The rule

  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs done ÷ bytes moved from memory. It tells you whether a computation is limited by how fast the chip can do math (compute-bound) or by how fast it can move data (memory-bound).
  • Prefill: a `[T, d_model] @ [d_model, d_model]` matmul does about 2·T·d_model² FLOPs while loading the weight matrix costs a fixed number of bytes — so intensity scales up with T. At T=512, intensity ≈ 512 FLOPs/byte, well above a typical accelerator's ridge point (roughly 100–300 FLOPs/byte). Prefill sits in the compute-bound region.
  • Decode (batch size 1): the same weight matrix gets loaded, but now only [1, d_model] worth of activation multiplies against it — intensity collapses to about 1 FLOP/byte. Decode sits deep in the memory-bound region: the chip's matrix units mostly sit idle, waiting for weights to arrive from HBM.
✅
Prefill: intensity ≈ 512 FLOPs/byte → compute-bound.
Decode (B=1): intensity ≈ 1 FLOP/byte → memory-bound.
Same weight matrices, same hardware — completely different bottleneck, because decode processes only one token's activation per step.
🪜
Math Ladder — arithmetic intensity
1 · In words: for one matmul, divide the math you do by the bytes you must fetch — a high ratio means the chip is busy computing (compute-bound), a low ratio means it's waiting on memory (memory-bound).
2 · The formula: intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes_moved. For a [T,d]@[d,d] matmul: FLOPs ≈ 2·T·d², weight bytes ≈ 2·d² (bf16) → intensity ≈ T.
3 · Tiny numbers: prefill T=512 → intensity ≈ 512 FLOPs/byte (above the ~100–300 ridge → compute-bound); decode T=1 → intensity ≈ 1 FLOP/byte (far below ridge → memory-bound).
4 · Sanity check: intensity scales with the number of activation rows sharing the loaded weights — so batching B decode requests gives intensity ≈ B, which is exactly why bigger decode batches climb back toward compute-bound while a longer single prompt does not help decode.

Why it's the key insight for production serving

  • Prefill wants more compute — a bigger, faster matmul engine, because it's FLOP-bound.
  • Decode wants faster memory and bigger batches — batching concurrent requests together lets many activation rows reuse the same loaded weights, pushing intensity back toward compute-bound. This is why production systems obsess over decode batch size and continuous batching.
  • Multiquery attention (many query heads sharing one shared K/V head) shrinks the KV cache dramatically, which is the dominant memory cost during long-context decode — letting the same memory budget hold far more context.
🔗
Causal chain: Roofline model (earlier weeks) → apply it to real transformer matrix shapes → prefill is one big matmul (compute-bound) → decode is a repeated matvec limited by the KV cache and weight loads (memory-bound) → this split decides where engineering effort and hardware budget go in production.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The prefill/decode split is the whole game in serving. Here is the mistake it quietly hides, and the price you pay for the standard fix.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): benchmarking with a long prompt, then sizing the whole service from it. A common bug is to measure throughput by feeding one big prompt (that is mostly prefill, which is compute-bound) and quote that number for the service. Nothing errors — the benchmark runs and the tokens/sec looks great — but real users spend most of their time in decode, which is memory-bound and often much slower per token. You ship a system that hits its promised speed on the benchmark and misses it badly in production. In code review, a senior engineer checks that the latency test actually generates many tokens (a real decode loop), not just reads a long prompt, before trusting the number.
⚖️
Trade-off: bigger decode batches buy throughput but cost per-user latency. Batching many concurrent requests together raises decode's arithmetic intensity back toward compute-bound, so you serve far more total tokens per second per chip. But every user now waits for the whole batch to step forward together, so one user's time-per-token goes up. In a design review this is the core dial: an interactive chat product caps the batch size to protect latency, while an offline batch-generation job cranks it up to squeeze the hardware. Same model, opposite settings.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Inference splits into prefill — one big matrix×matrix over the whole prompt, compute-bound — and decode, a matrix×vector per token that reloads all the weights and the growing KV cache, so it's memory-bandwidth-bound. Arithmetic intensity makes it precise: prefill ≈ T FLOPs/byte (well above the ridge), decode at batch 1 ≈ 1 (far below). The nuance is the fix: batching concurrent decode requests raises intensity toward compute-bound (throughput up, per-user latency up), while a longer single prompt does not — so I benchmark a real decode loop, not a long prompt, or I ship a system that hits its number on the bench and misses in production."

Acceptance criteria

  • Build one toy transformer layer in JAX with configurable d_model, n_heads, d_head.
  • Run a prefill pass over a [B, T, d_model] batch (e.g. T=512) doing the full QKV projection + attention + output projection as one matmul-heavy pass.
  • Separately simulate a decode loop of T' steps (e.g. 100), each processing a [B, 1, d_model] activation against a growing KV cache implemented as a preallocated buffer that gets written into — not regrown every step (regrowing every step is itself a bug worth flagging).
