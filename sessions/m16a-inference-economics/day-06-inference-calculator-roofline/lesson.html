1What is a roofline model, and why do we need one?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Week 5's FLOPs-counting and memory-accounting work (you know how to count floating point operations and bytes for a transformer forward pass). No prior serving/inference knowledge needed.

The question changes: from "how much to train" to "how fast to serve"

In Week 5 you counted FLOPs (floating point operations — the additions and multiplications a chip does) to answer "how much compute do I need to train this model?" Today you take the exact same counting skill and point it at a new question: "once the model is trained, how fast can it generate text for a user?" This is called inference — running a trained model forward to produce outputs, as opposed to training it.

The two numbers that decide everything

A roofline model predicts the maximum possible speed of a computation on a given chip, using only two numbers about that chip:

  • Peak compute throughput — measured in FLOPs/s (floating point operations per second): how much arithmetic the chip's math units can do.
  • Peak memory bandwidth — measured in bytes/s: how fast the chip can move data between HBM (High Bandwidth Memory — the chip's main data storage) and the compute units that actually do the arithmetic.

Every workload hits one of these two ceilings first. If it hits the compute ceiling, it's compute-bound — the arithmetic units are the bottleneck. If it hits the memory ceiling, it's memory-bound — moving data is the bottleneck. Which one you hit depends on a single ratio called arithmetic intensity: FLOPs performed divided by bytes moved. High arithmetic intensity → compute-bound. Low arithmetic intensity → memory-bound.

Why transformer inference cares — a lot

Generating text with a transformer has two phases, and they sit in different regimes:

  • Prefill — processing the input prompt, all tokens at once, in parallel. Usually compute-bound.
  • Decode — generating one new token at a time, one after another. Usually memory-bound, because every single token requires reloading the entire model's weights from HBM, while the actual arithmetic per token is tiny.

Pope et al. (2022), "Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference," formalized this analysis for huge models on TPU pods. With careful partitioning and int8 weight quantization (storing weights in 8-bit numbers instead of 16 or 32-bit, to cut memory traffic), they hit 29ms per-token latency at low batch size and 76% MFU (model FLOPs utilization — the fraction of the chip's peak compute you actually use) at large batch size, on a 540-billion-parameter model.

Today's artifact: turn the math into a program

An inference calculator is just this roofline math turned into code. Feed it a model size, a hardware spec (bandwidth + FLOPs/s), and a batch size — it predicts tokens-per-second before you run anything on real hardware.

2A busy kitchen: the chef, the runner, and the fridgeIntuition

Picture a busy restaurant kitchen. The chef is compute — she can chop, sear, and plate incredibly fast. But every dish needs ingredients, and only one runner can carry them from the walk-in fridge (memory) to the chef's station.

👩‍🍳
compute-bound = chef is the bottleneck

If a dish needs one ingredient that's already sitting on the counter, the chef just cooks — she never waits on the runner. The chef's speed is what limits you.

🏃‍♂️🧊
memory-bound = the runner is the bottleneck

If a dish needs the runner to walk to the fridge and back for every single ingredient, one at a time, the chef sits idle waiting. No amount of chef skill helps — you need a faster runner, or a runner who carries more per trip.

Prefill is like a big catering order: you send the runner to the fridge once, grab everything for 50 dishes in one trip, and the chef stays busy the whole time. That's compute-bound.

Decode is like a customer ordering one item at a time, and each item needs a fresh fridge trip. The runner (memory bandwidth) is the bottleneck, and the chef (compute) is often waiting. That's memory-bound.

直觉 / Intuition: 大单一次跑冰箱（prefill，厨师一直忙 = compute-bound），零点一次跑一趟（decode，厨师干等 = memory-bound）。In systems terms: compare a kernel's arithmetic intensity (FLOPs ÷ bytes) to the chip's ridge point (peak FLOPs/s ÷ bandwidth); step time is just max(flops/peak_flops, bytes/peak_bw) — the whole roofline in one line.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: in a real kitchen, the runner and chef can't work on the exact same dish at the exact same instant. But on a real chip, compute and memory transfers genuinely overlap — the chip prefetches the next weights while still computing on the current ones. The roofline model is a ceiling on what's achievable, not a claim that the two never happen simultaneously.

4The rules of the roofline, and why it mattersHow & why

The rules

  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs performed ÷ bytes moved. It has units of FLOPs/byte.
  • Ridge point = peak FLOPs/s ÷ peak memory bandwidth. This is the arithmetic intensity where the two ceilings cross. Below it, you're memory-bound no matter how fast the chip's math units are. Above it, you're compute-bound and memory bandwidth stops mattering.
  • Time for a step = max(flops / peak_flops_s, bytes_moved / peak_mem_bw). You're bound by whichever ceiling you hit first — that max() is the entire roofline model in one line.
  • Decode's arithmetic intensity ≈ batch size B — in the weight-bound regime this calculator targets. Weights are read from HBM once per forward pass and reused across every sequence in the batch (it's the same weight matrix for all of them), so bytes moved from weights stay roughly fixed while FLOPs scale with B. (This ignores the KV cache from Days 1 & 3 — its bytes grow with B too, so in long-context, high-batch serving it can dominate memory traffic and make intensity saturate below B. Today's calculator deliberately isolates the weight-loading term.)
✅
ridge_point = peak_flops_s / peak_mem_bw
For H100: 989.5e12 / 3.35e12 ≈ 295 FLOPs/byte. Below 295, memory-bound. Above 295, compute-bound.
🪜
Math Ladder — the roofline in one line
1 · In words: a step takes as long as the slower of "do the FLOPs" and "move the bytes"; the ridge point is the intensity where those two costs are equal.
2 · The formula: t = max(FLOPs/peak_flops_s, bytes/peak_bw); ridge = peak_flops_s/peak_bw; a kernel is memory-bound iff its intensity = FLOPs/bytes < ridge.
3 · Tiny numbers: H100 → ridge = 989.5e12/3.35e12 ≈ 295. Decode at batch 1 has intensity ≈ 1 (≪295) → memory-bound; a 2048-token prefill has intensity ≈ 2048 (≫295) → compute-bound.
4 · Sanity check: decode intensity ≈ B, so it climbs toward the ridge as you batch — but only if you count all bytes moved; include the KV cache (which also grows with B) and intensity saturates below B, so you stay memory-bound longer than the weights-only estimate predicts.

Why it's a superpower

  • Predict before you run — no GPU/TPU needed to know roughly what throughput to expect. Two numbers about the chip, two numbers about the model, and one max().
  • Explains why prefill and decode need different serving strategies — the ~300x gap between prefill's compute-bound ceiling and decode's memory-bound ceiling (at batch size 1) is why production systems use techniques like continuous batching and disaggregated prefill/decode serving.
  • Tells you exactly what to fix — if you're memory-bound, batching or better quantization helps; if you're compute-bound, only a faster chip (or fewer FLOPs) helps. The roofline tells you which lever to pull before you waste engineering time on the wrong one.
🔗
Causal chain: Week 5's FLOPs/bytes counting → arithmetic intensity (FLOPs ÷ bytes) → compare to the chip's ridge point → compute-bound or memory-bound → that tells you which ceiling limits your tokens/sec, and which lever (batching, quantization, faster chip) actually helps.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

The roofline is one max(), so it always returns a confident number. That is exactly why a misread arithmetic intensity is so dangerous — the calculator never tells you it was fed the wrong bytes.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): counting only the weight bytes in the intensity. The clean rule "decode intensity ≈ batch size B" holds only when weights are the sole thing read from HBM. Real decode also streams the entire KV cache every step, and those bytes grow with B and with context length. If you plug only weight bytes into the denominator, your computed intensity looks like it climbs with B and you predict you will reach the ridge point and become compute-bound — but in long-context, high-batch serving the KV traffic dominates, intensity saturates below B, and you stay memory-bound. Nothing errors: the calculator returns a plausible tokens/sec that is simply too optimistic, and you over-promise throughput. In code review, a senior engineer asks "does the bytes-moved term include the KV cache at the target batch and context length, or just the weights?" before trusting a roofline estimate for a production regime.
⚖️
Trade-off: the roofline predicts the ceiling, not the achieved fraction of it. Modelling a step as max(flops/peak_flops, bytes/peak_bw) is what makes it a one-line, no-GPU-needed estimate — but it assumes you actually hit peak bandwidth and peak FLOPs, which real kernels rarely do (imperfect memory access patterns, launch overhead, and low utilization commonly leave you at 50–70% of a ceiling). In a design review the decision is how much slack to build in: quote the raw roofline as an upper bound for a rough plan, but for a capacity commitment, derate it by a measured efficiency factor rather than treating the ceiling as the delivered number.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "A roofline needs two chip numbers (peak FLOPs/s, bandwidth) and one work number (arithmetic intensity = FLOPs/bytes); step time is max(flops/peak_flops, bytes/peak_bw), and the ridge point peak_flops/bw (~295 for an H100) tells you which ceiling binds. Prefill sits above the ridge (compute-bound), decode at batch 1 sits at intensity ≈ 1 (memory-bound), and that ~300× gap is why prefill and decode get different serving strategies. The nuance is honesty about the denominator and the ceiling: count the KV cache bytes at the real batch/context or you'll over-predict throughput, and derate the raw roofline by measured efficiency before you commit capacity — kernels rarely hit peak."

⚠️
Gotcha: GPU spec sheets often quote peak FLOPs/s "with sparsity" — double the dense number. NVIDIA's H100 datasheet lists ~1979 TFLOPs/s bf16 "with sparsity," but the real dense number to use here is ~989.5 TFLOPs/s. Using the sparsity-inflated number silently halves your ridge point and makes everything look twice as memory-friendly as it really is. State which figure (dense vs. sparse) you used, explicitly, in your writeup.
