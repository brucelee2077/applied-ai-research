Module 9e · Scale · Day 5

Finished Goods,
Not Hours Lit

The finale. You have kept the run alive — mesh, checkpoints, bit-exact resume, elastic recovery. But how do you score a month-long run? Not by how long the machines were powered on. A giant run lives or dies on one number: goodput — the slice of wall-clock time that was actually useful training. Restarts, stragglers, and slow saves all steal it. Today you learn to measure it and see where it leaks.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: define goodput as useful training time divided by wall-clock time, explain MFU (model FLOPs utilization), and name what steals goodput — restarts, stragglers, and checkpoint stalls.
1 · WHAT IS ITScoring a run by useful work, not uptime✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Days 1–4 you know the pieces that keep a run alive — the mesh, checkpoints, bit-exact resume, elastic recovery. Today we put a single number on how well all of that is working.

The wrong way to score a run

You could measure a month-long run by how many hours the machines were switched on. But powered-on is not the same as working. Some of those hours were spent restarting after a crash, waiting on a straggler, or stalled writing a checkpoint. Those hours cost money but taught the model nothing.

The right number: goodput

Goodput is the fraction of real time that went to useful training. If the run was on for 100 hours but only 90 of them were actually training (the other 10 were restarts and stalls), goodput is 90%. It answers: of the time we paid for, how much actually moved the model forward?

A partner number: MFU

Goodput asks "was the run training or stalled?". A second number, MFU (model FLOPs utilization), asks "while training, how hard were the chips actually working?". A FLOP is one math operation. Chips have a top speed; MFU is the fraction of that top speed the model really reaches. Both numbers must be high for a run to be efficient.

What steals goodput

Everything from this week shows up here. Restarts (from crashes) burn time re-loading. Stragglers make every step wait on the slowest chip. Checkpoint stalls pause training if saving is not async. Each one is a leak in goodput. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONA factory measured by finished goods✓
🏭
Finished goods per day

A good factory manager does not brag "the lights were on 24 hours". They ask "how many finished products came out today?". Lights-on time is what you pay for. Finished goods are what you actually get. A factory jammed for half the day made goods for only half the day, no matter how long the lights burned.

⚙️
And how fast the line ran

Even when the line was moving, it might have crawled — half its top speed. So there are two questions: how much of the day was the line running at all, and while running, how fast did it go? Both must be high to make a lot of goods.

In one line: lights-on time is wall-clock time, finished goods are useful training, and jams are restarts, stragglers, and checkpoint stalls. Goodput is "what fraction of the day the line was running".

How fast the line ran while moving is MFU. A run with high goodput but low MFU is a factory that runs all day but slowly. You want both high: rarely jammed, and fast when moving.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a factory jam is loud and obvious — you see the pile-up. A training goodput leak can be quiet: a straggler slowing every step, or a background stall you only spot by carefully timing steps and comparing against the ideal.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYWorking out goodput, hour by hour✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Take the total time the run was going (wall-clock). Subtract every hour that was not useful training — restarts, straggler stalls, checkpoint pauses. What is left is useful training time. Goodput is useful time divided by total time. It is always between 0 and 1, and you want it close to 1.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
goodput = useful_training_time / wall_clock_time
useful_training_time = wall_clock_time − wasted_time
# wasted_time = restarts + straggler stalls + checkpoint stalls

Symbols: wall_clock_time = total time the run was going. wasted_time = all the non-training hours. useful_training_time = the rest. goodput = the fraction that was useful, from 0 to 1.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say a run was going for 100 hours. Restarts cost 4 hours, stragglers cost 5 hours, and non-async checkpoint stalls cost 1 hour.

🔢
wasted = 4 + 5 + 1 = 10 hours
useful = 100 − 10 = 90 hours
goodput = 90 / 100 = 0.90 # 90%

cut stragglers to 1h → useful 94h → goodput 0.94

90% goodput means 10 of every 100 paid hours taught the model nothing. On a run costing millions, that 10% is a fortune. Fix the biggest leak — here the 5-hour straggler bill — and goodput climbs. Every technique this week is a tool to raise this one number.

Why the two numbers work together

  • Goodput = was the run training or stalled? Raised by async saves, elastic recovery, and straggler hunting.
  • MFU = while training, how hard were the chips working? Raised by good mesh placement (Day 1) and efficient kernels.
  • Real throughput ≈ goodput × MFU × peak speed: both fractions must be high, or the run wastes the hardware you paid for.
🔗
The big picture: this whole module was one story — keep a 10,000-chip, month-long run alive and efficient. Goodput is the scoreboard. Bringing up the mesh, saving full checkpoints, resuming bit-exact, and recovering from failure all serve this one number. That is the defining staff systems skill.
