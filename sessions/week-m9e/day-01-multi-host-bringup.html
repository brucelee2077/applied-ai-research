Module 9e · Scale · Day 1

Bringing Up
Many Hosts as One

To train a giant model you do not use one chip. You use ten thousand. But ten thousand chips are not automatically one machine — someone has to wire them into a single team that shares work every fraction of a second. Today you learn how many hosts become one big virtual computer: the device mesh, the fast links inside a pod, the slower links between pods, and why where you place each chip decides whether your run is fast or painfully slow.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain what a device mesh is, tell apart the fast in-pod links (ICI) and the slower cross-pod network (DCN), and say why mapping the logical mesh onto the physical links well keeps group communication fast.
1 · WHAT IS ITWiring many computers into one big machine✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Modules 9c–9d you know that a big model is split across many chips (sharding), and that the chips must share numbers during each training step. Today we zoom out: how do you turn thousands of separate chips into one machine that acts as a team?

One chip is never enough

A frontier model has so many numbers inside it that no single chip can hold it or train it in a sane amount of time. So you use many chips. A host is one physical computer that holds a few chips. Put many hosts together and you get thousands of chips. But by themselves they are strangers — they do not know they belong to the same run.

The device mesh: chips arranged in a grid

The first job is to line up every chip into one shared map. That map is the device mesh. Think of it as a big grid where every chip gets an address, like "row 3, column 7". Once every chip has an address, the software can say "all chips in the same row, add your numbers together" — and the whole cluster acts as one machine.

Two kinds of wire: fast and slow

Not all connections between chips are equal. Chips that sit close together (inside the same pod) are joined by very fast, short cables. This fast in-pod link is called ICI (Inter-Chip Interconnect). Chips in different pods must talk over a normal, slower data-center network called DCN (Data Center Network). ICI is like whispering to the person next to you. DCN is like mailing a letter across town.

Placement decides speed

The chips that talk the most should sit on the fast wires. If you accidentally put heavy talkers on the slow DCN, the whole run crawls. So bring-up is not only "turn on the chips" — it is "arrange the mesh so the busiest talking stays on ICI". Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONSeating a huge banquet✓
🍽️
The banquet

You are seating 10,000 guests for a dinner. Some guests must talk to each other constantly all night. Others barely speak. If you seat two constant talkers at opposite ends of the hall, they will spend the whole night shouting across the room — and everyone waits for them.

🪑
Sit talkers together

The smart plan: seat the guests who talk most at the same table, side by side. Then their chatter stays quiet and quick. Only the light, rare messages need to cross the whole hall. Seat well, and the dinner flows. Seat badly, and it stalls.

In one line: the guests are chips, a table is a pod, "talking a lot" is the heavy group communication of a training step, and the seating plan is the device mesh mapped onto physical links.

Talking within one table is ICI — fast and quiet. Shouting across the hall is DCN — slow and it holds everyone up. Good bring-up puts the loudest talkers on the same table.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: at a real dinner guests choose their own moments to speak. In a training run every chip must finish talking at the exact same instant before the next step begins — one slow "shout" across the hall freezes the entire cluster, not just one table.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYWhy link speed decides the step time✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Every training step, the chips share numbers with each other. This sharing is called a collective. How long it takes is simple: the amount of data to move, divided by how fast the wire moves data. A fast wire (ICI) finishes quickly. A slow wire (DCN) takes much longer to move the same data.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
time = data_size / link_speed
# data_size = how many bytes must cross the wire
# link_speed = bytes per second the wire can move

Symbols: time = seconds this sharing takes. data_size = number of bytes to send. link_speed = how many bytes per second the wire carries. Same data, faster wire, smaller time.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say one collective must move 1,000,000,000 bytes (1 gigabyte). ICI moves 500 gigabytes per second. DCN moves 25 gigabytes per second.

🔢
on ICI: 1 GB / 500 GB/s = 0.002 s # 2 milliseconds
on DCN: 1 GB / 25 GB/s = 0.040 s # 40 milliseconds

DCN is 20× slower for the exact same data

The same sharing costs 2 milliseconds on ICI but 40 milliseconds on DCN — twenty times slower. Do that every step for a month and the difference is enormous. That is why placement matters: put the heavy, every-step sharing on ICI.

Why this is a big deal

  • The collective waits for the slowest chip: a group share only finishes when the last chip finishes. One chip stuck on a slow DCN link freezes all the others.
  • Placement is a real decision: the mesh must be laid out so chips that share the most sit on ICI. This is the whole point of "mapping the logical mesh onto physical links".
  • It happens thousands of times: the sharing repeats every single step, so small per-step waste multiplies into weeks of lost time.
🔗
The big picture: a 10,000-chip run is only "one machine" because the mesh and the fast links let every chip agree on numbers each step. Tomorrow you learn how to save that machine's full state so a month-long run can survive a crash.
