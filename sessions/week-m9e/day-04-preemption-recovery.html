Module 9e · Scale · Day 4

Swapping a RunnerMid-Race

On a 10,000-chip run, something fails almost every hour: a chip dies, a host is taken away, a link goes bad. You cannot restart the whole month each time. So the run must survive losing a piece — detect the failure, swap in a healthy replacement, and continue. And there is a quieter enemy: one chip that is not dead, just slow. Because every chip waits for the slowest one at each group step, a single straggler can stall all ten thousand.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain preemption (a chip or host is taken or dies mid-run), describe elastic recovery (swap in a replacement and continue without a full restart), and explain why one straggler stalls the whole run.
1Surviving a chip that dies mid-runWhat is it
🧭
What this assumes: from Days 2–3 you know how to save the full state and resume it bit-exact. Today: hardware fails during the run. How do you keep going without throwing away hours of progress every time one piece breaks?

Failure is constant, not rare

With ten thousand chips running for a month, something breaks almost every hour. A chip dies. A host reboots. The cloud takes a machine back. When a machine is taken away or dies mid-run, that is called preemption. You cannot restart the whole month each time — that would mean never finishing.

The fix: elastic recovery

The run must be able to lose a piece and keep going. Elastic recovery means: notice the failure, grab a healthy spare chip or host, reload the last checkpoint into it, and continue the run. "Elastic" because the run stretches to fit whatever hardware is currently working, instead of demanding the exact same machines forever.

The quiet enemy: the straggler

Not every problem is a clean death. Sometimes a chip is alive but slow — a bad link, a hot chip that throttles. That slow chip is a straggler. Because every group step (a collective) only finishes when the slowest chip finishes, one straggler makes all ten thousand chips wait.

So two jobs, not one

Keeping a giant run alive means handling both: recover fast when a piece dies (preemption → elastic recovery), and hunt down the one slow chip before it drags the whole run (straggler mitigation). Keep going 👇

2A relay race with spare runnersIntuition
🏃
Swap the runner

A relay team is running a long race. One runner twists an ankle. A good team does not send everyone back to the start — they wave in a fresh runner from the bench, hand over the baton at the last checkpoint, and the race goes on. Losing a runner costs seconds, not the whole race.

🐢
The slow lap

Now imagine one runner is not hurt, just tired and slow. The baton can only pass when that runner reaches the line, so the whole team waits for the slowest lap. The team is only as fast as its slowest runner on each handoff.

In one line: the runners are chips, the baton handoff is a group step (a collective), the bench is spare hardware, and the last handoff line is the checkpoint you resume from.

Waving in a fresh runner is elastic recovery after a preemption. The tired, slow runner is the straggler — and the team waits for them at every handoff.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a relay baton passes once per lap. In training, all ten thousand chips must hand off together, at the exact same instant, on every single step. So one slow or missing chip freezes the entire group far more tightly than one slow runner slows a relay.

4Why the slowest chip sets the paceMechanism & why

Step 1 — in plain words

Every group step waits for the slowest chip. So the time of one step is not the average chip's time — it is the maximum chip's time. If 9,999 chips finish in 100 milliseconds and one straggler takes 500, the whole step takes 500. The fast chips just sit and wait for the slow one.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
step_time = max( time of every chip )
# NOT the average — the slowest one
slowdown = straggler_time / normal_time
# how many times slower the step becomes

Symbols: step_time = how long one group step takes. max(...) = the largest, the slowest chip. straggler_time = the slow chip's time. normal_time = a healthy chip's time. The step runs at the pace of the slowest.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say a healthy chip does one step in 100 ms, but one straggler takes 500 ms.

🔢
no straggler: step_time = max(100, 100, …) = 100 ms
one straggler: step_time = max(100, …, 500) = 500 ms

slowdown = 500 / 100 = 5× slower — every step
over the whole run: the straggler steals 80% of the time

One slow chip out of ten thousand makes every step 5 times slower. The other 9,999 chips are wasted, waiting. That is why finding and replacing a straggler matters as much as recovering from a clean death.

Why this shapes how you run

  • Detect fast: watch each chip's step time; when one lags far behind, flag it as a straggler.
  • Replace, do not restart: elastic recovery reloads the last checkpoint into a spare and continues — losing minutes, not the whole run.
  • Keep spares ready: a healthy bench of chips means recovery is a quick swap, not a long wait for new hardware.
🔗
The big picture: a giant run stays alive by treating failure as normal — swap dead pieces, hunt slow ones. Tomorrow, Day 5, ties it together: goodput — the fraction of wall-clock time that is actually useful training, which restarts and stragglers steal.
