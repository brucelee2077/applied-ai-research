1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a screencast, and what is the Chinchilla scaling law?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you built a tiny transformer earlier in the course and you can read basic Python. No video-recording or scaling-law knowledge needed — we start from zero.

First idea: a "screencast"

A screencast is a screen recording of your computer with your voice narrating over it. You point at code or an idea and explain it out loud, as if teaching a friend sitting next to you.

Why does this matter for a job? A frontier lab cannot read your mind from a GitHub repo. A 6-to-12-minute video shows three things a repo hides: how you think, whether you can defend a design choice, and whether you can explain hard ideas to a team. Hiring guides (like Vlad Feinberg's, DeepMind's pre-training lead) say that communication signal is exactly what separates strong candidates.

Second idea: a "scaling law"

A scaling law is a simple math rule that predicts how good a model will be before you train it. It uses three numbers:

  • N = number of parameters (the model's learned weights).
  • D = number of training tokens (a token is a chunk of text, roughly a word-piece).
  • C = compute (total math operations, measured in FLOPs — floating-point operations).

The rule is written as a loss curve. Loss is a score where lower means better:

🧮
L(N, D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β
E = the irreducible loss you can never beat (the noise floor of language itself). A/N^α = error from too few parameters. B/D^β = error from too little data. α and β are small exponents (≈0.34 and ≈0.28 in the Chinchilla fits) that say how fast each error shrinks.

The Chinchilla result

The Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022) found that given a fixed compute budget C, there is one best split between model size N and data D. Earlier models (GPT-3, Gopher) were too big and undertrained. For compute-optimal training, N and D should grow equally — double the model, double the data. Rule of thumb: about 20 tokens per parameter. Because C ≈ 6·N·D, you can turn "I have budget C" into "the best model is N* params and D* tokens" with a little calculus. That derivation is what you show on screen.

The thing you walk through: the Addition Transformer

The Addition Transformer is a tiny transformer (the neural-network design behind modern LLMs) trained to do one toy task: add two numbers, e.g. take "15+27=" and output "42". It is small enough to train on a laptop, but real enough to contain every real transformer part: embedding, attention, MLP, and output head.

2 · INTUITIONA student, a budget of study-hours, and one exam✓

Think of training a model like preparing a student for one big exam. Your only real limit is total study-hours — that is your compute budget C. You can spend those hours two ways.

🧠
Bigger brain (more N)

Hire a smarter, bigger-brained tutor — more parameters. Kaplan's early advice was "mostly hire the genius tutor", so people built huge models.

📚
More practice (more D)

Give the student more practice problems to read — more data. A merely-clever tutor who has done thousands of problems beats a genius who barely studied.

The Chinchilla answer: the paper ran the experiment about 400 times and found the smarter split. For every extra unit you spend making the tutor bigger, spend an equal unit on more practice. That is why 70B-parameter Chinchilla beat 280B-parameter Gopher using the same study-hours.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real student gets tired and forgets. A transformer trained one pass over fresh data has no fatigue — in the Chinchilla regime it sees most tokens only once, so "more practice" never causes the boredom or burnout a human tutor would hit.

4 · HOW & WHYHow to derive (N*, D*), and why a screencast earns the offer✓

The derivation you show on screen

  • Start from the loss curve: L(N, D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β. Lower L is better.
  • Use the compute rule C ≈ 6·N·D (forward + backward pass costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token). This turns "I have budget C" into a constraint linking N and D.
  • Minimize L subject to that constraint. The lowest-loss point is where the iso-compute line just touches (is tangent to) the lowest loss contour — that tangent point is (N*, D*).
  • The answer: N and D scale together. Roughly ~20 tokens per parameter in the classic fit.
✅
C ≈ 6·N·D  +  minimize L(N, D)  ⟹  (N*, D*) at the tangent point
"Fix the compute, and there is exactly one best trade between a bigger model and more data." That single point is the punchline.

Why a screencast earns the offer

  • It shows thinking, not just output — a repo shows what you built; a voice-over shows why you built it that way.
  • It forces you to defend a choice — e.g. learned vs sinusoidal position, why d_model=64, why 4 heads. Defending one alternative on camera is a strong signal.
  • It proves you can teach — frontier labs are teams. If you can explain a transformer in 8 minutes, you can explain your work to colleagues.
⚠️
The biggest trap: do not confuse Kaplan (2020) and Chinchilla (2022). Kaplan said spend most of a bigger budget on more parameters — which led to giant, undertrained models like GPT-3. Chinchilla re-ran the experiments, fixed how the learning-rate schedule was set relative to the training horizon, and found N and D scale equally. If your video says "scaling laws tell us to just make the model bigger," a reviewer knows you only read the older paper.

7 · PRODUCERecord the screencast — your first portfolio piece✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): record a high-fidelity screencast (6–12 min) that (a) walks through the JAX Addition Transformer code from scratch, defending each design choice, and (b) demonstrates the Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling-law derivation live on screen.

Acceptance criteria

  • Video is 6–12 minutes; audio is clear and narrated (not a silent screen-capture).
  • The walk-through covers all five parts in order: embedding, positional encoding, attention (Q/K/V + QKᵀ/√d + softmax), MLP+residual, output head — each with a spoken reason why.
  • You defend at least one choice against an alternative (e.g. learned vs sinusoidal position; why d_model=64; why 4 heads).
  • You show the derivation on screen: state L(N,D)=E+A/Nᵅ+B/Dᵝ, state C≈6ND, solve for (N*, D*) for a concrete budget, and end with the ~tokens-per-param takeaway.
  • addition_transformer.py runs end-to-end and the trained model correctly outputs the sum for at least 5 held-out examples.
⚠️
Do not record the "it works" moment on an untrained model — its argmax output is random garbage and will show a wrong sum on camera. Train first, then record the demo.
