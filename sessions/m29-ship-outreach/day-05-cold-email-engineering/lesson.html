1What is a cold email to a frontier lab — and why is it an engineering artifact?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: nothing about hiring. It helps if you have already built the earlier-week artifacts, but this lesson explains every term. No prior outreach experience needed.

Three plain-word definitions first

A cold email is a message you send to someone who does not know you, asking for something — here, a short conversation or a role. A frontier lab is one of the small number of organizations training the largest, most capable AI models (the kind of place that ships state-of-the-art language models). Outreach just means reaching out first, before anyone invites you.

The core reframe

At a frontier lab, the thing that is not scarce is enthusiasm. Everyone who writes is excited. The scarce resource is verifiable technical signal: concrete evidence that you can already do the work. So a cold email to a frontier lab is really an engineering artifact, not a letter. Its whole job is to hand the reader a few clickable, checkable proofs of competence — and then get out of the way.

The three proofs this lesson packages

The curriculum asks you to present three artifacts. Here is what each word means:

  • A Transformer implementation. The Transformer is the neural-network architecture behind modern large language models. It uses attention: every token (word-piece) looks at every other token to decide what matters. Building one from scratch shows you understand the machine, not just its API.
  • Scaling-law proofs. A scaling law is an empirical rule: model loss (prediction error) falls as a smooth power-law function of model size, data, and compute. "Proofs" means your own small experiments that reproduce the straight-line-on-a-log-log-plot behavior, so you can talk about compute budgets like an insider.
  • Pallas kernel optimizations. Pallas is JAX's language for writing custom GPU/TPU kernels — small programs that run directly on the accelerator and control exactly how data moves through fast on-chip memory. In Pallas you call pl.pallas_call, describe how the big array is chopped into tiles with a BlockSpec, iterate those tiles over a grid, and write results into output references (o_ref). Writing one shows you can make hardware go faster — the daily job at a lab.

What "ruthlessly technical" means

It means: no adjectives about how excited you are, no life story. Every sentence either states a result or links to something the reader can open and inspect. The banned category is generalized passion — sentences like "I've always been passionate about AI" carry zero signal and are the first thing that gets you deleted.

2Two contractors bid for the same kitchenIntuition

Picture a homeowner about to spend a lot of money renovating a kitchen. Two contractors email them. Which one gets hired?

📝❌
Contractor A — the story

Three paragraphs about how much they love craftsmanship and have dreamed of woodworking since childhood. Warm, sincere — and impossible to check. The homeowner starts skimming by paragraph two.

📸✅
Contractor B — the proof

Four photos of kitchens they built last year, a one-line note ("cut tile-waste in half"), and their license number. The homeowner hires B before finishing A's email.

In one line: a frontier-lab cold email is Contractor B. The photos are your Transformer repo, the "half the tile-waste" is your kernel speedup number, and the license is your reproducible scaling-law plot.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a contractor's photos are hard to fake and easy to judge at a glance, but code is easy to claim and takes real effort to verify. So your links must be instantly checkable — a clean README, a one-command run, a plot with labeled axes — because the reader spends maybe 30 seconds deciding whether to open a second tab. Polished, runnable proof beats an impressive-sounding claim.

4The rules of a proof-of-work email, and why they workHow & why

The rules

  • Subject = one specific technical phrase. Not "Job inquiry." Something like "2.56x fused-kernel speedup + reproduced Chinchilla scaling." The subject is your first proof.
  • Structure is: who → 3 linked proofs → one ask. One line saying who you are, then three bullet lines — each ending in a link — then a single concrete request. Nothing else.
  • Order the proofs understand → result → speedup. Transformer (I understand the machine) → scaling-law plot (I ran a real experiment) → Pallas kernel (I made hardware faster).
  • Every number is tethered to a link. A speedup or exponent that the reader cannot open is treated as false. Put the link right next to the number.
  • Delete every line with no result, number, or link. That deletes all "passion," all "dream company," all "eager to learn."
  • Keep the body ≤150 words and end with one low-cost ask ("15 minutes on kernel work?"), never an open-ended "let me know your thoughts."
✅
A strong proof line: "Reproduced Chinchilla scaling across 4 model sizes; loss ~ C-0.05 (plot + code: <link>)."
One result, one number, one openable link, zero adjectives about you.

Why it works

  • It respects the 30-second budget. A hiring manager scans, does not read. Structure and links let them verify competence fast, then decide to reply.
  • It shifts trust from your words to your artifacts. You are not asking them to believe you; you are handing them things they can check themselves.
  • It filters out the noise everyone else sends. When 95% of inbound is enthusiasm, three checkable proofs stand out instantly.
🔗
Causal chain: enthusiasm is not scarce → words are unverifiable → the reader trusts only what they can open → so the email becomes a stack of links → each link is a proof from an earlier week → reply.

7Write the email — three linked proofs, zero passionProduce

Today's task: write a concise, ruthlessly technical frontier-lab outreach email (a Markdown draft) whose body is three linked proofs — a from-scratch Transformer, your reproduced scaling-law result, and a measured Pallas kernel speedup — with zero lines of generalized passion.

Acceptance criteria

  • Body is ≤150 words; subject line is one specific technical phrase (not "Job inquiry").
  • Exactly 3 proof lines, each ending in a real, openable link (repo / plot / benchmark log). No unlinked claims.
  • Zero sentences with "passion", "passionate", "dream", "always wanted", "eager to learn", or any adjective describing your excitement — a grep for these returns nothing.
  • The Transformer, scaling-law, and Pallas kernel proofs appear in that order (understand → result → speedup).
  • Every number stated (speedup, scaling exponent) sits next to the link that verifies it.
  • Ends with exactly one concrete, low-cost ask ("15 minutes on kernel work?"), not "let me know your thoughts."
⚠️
The trap: stripping out passion and pasting in unlinked brags ("built a highly optimized Transformer with 10x faster attention"). To the reader this is worse than passion — it signals someone who overstates and can't back it up. A claim without a working link is treated as false. A single dead link, or a repo that errors on git clone && run, turns your strongest asset into your biggest liability.
