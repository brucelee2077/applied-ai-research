1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a FLOP, and what is bandwidth?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know a transformer has layers of matrix multiplications, and you can multiply and divide big numbers. No prior "systems math" needed — we start from two words.

Two words first

A FLOP is one floating-point operation — a single multiply, or a single add, on decimal numbers. When someone says a model "costs 10²⁴ FLOPs to train," they mean the hardware performed that many multiplies and adds. A multiply-add (one multiply then one add) is therefore 2 FLOPs.

Bandwidth is how fast a chip can move numbers between its big slow memory — called HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory, the RAM sitting right next to the compute chip) — and the tiny fast registers where the math actually happens. It is measured in bytes per second.

Why count FLOPs at all?

Because before you launch a training run that costs millions of dollars, you want to predict how long it takes. Two numbers give you that.

First — the compute cost. The near-universal rule from the Scaling Book is that training a dense transformer takes about 6 × N × D FLOPs, where N is the number of tokens you train on and D is the number of model parameters. Where does the 6 come from? The forward pass uses every parameter in one multiply-add: 2 FLOPs per parameter per token. The backward pass (computing gradients) costs about twice that: 4 more. So 2 forward + 4 backward = 6.

Second — the memory cost. To do any operation the chip must first load the input numbers from HBM, then later store the result. If an operation does very few FLOPs per byte it loads, the chip finishes the math and sits idle waiting for more data — it is memory-bound. If it does many FLOPs per byte, the memory keeps up and the math units stay busy — it is compute-bound.

The tool that decides which case you are in

The roofline model. Define arithmetic intensity = (FLOPs done) ÷ (bytes moved). Compare it to the hardware's ridge point = (peak FLOP/s) ÷ (peak bandwidth in bytes/s). Above the ridge → compute-bound (good, the expensive math units are busy). Below → memory-bound (the chip starves).

📦
Same idea in one line: compute time = FLOPs ÷ peak FLOP/s, memory time = bytes ÷ bandwidth, and your real runtime is the larger of the two.
That is the whole mental model: count FLOPs, count bytes, take the max.
⚠️
The one trap to remember: the training constant is 6, but a single inference forward-pass token costs only ~2·D FLOPs. Do not use 6 for serving-cost estimates or you overstate cost by 3×.
📚

2 · INTUITIONA professional kitchen: chef vs. runner✓

Picture a busy restaurant kitchen. The chef is the compute unit — dazzlingly fast at chopping and cooking (those are the FLOPs). The pantry at the back is HBM, holding all the ingredients. The runner who carries trays of ingredients from pantry to the chef's station is the memory bandwidth.

🏃‍♂️🍅
Runner-bound = memory-bound

A dish needs a whole tray but only three knife-strokes (low intensity). The chef finishes instantly and stands idle waiting for the next tray. The kitchen is capped by the runner.

👨‍🍳🔪
Chef-bound = compute-bound

One small tray feeds twenty minutes of intricate work (high intensity). The runner easily keeps ahead and the chef never stops. Now the kitchen is capped by the chef.

Arithmetic intensity is just: how many knife-strokes does the chef get out of each tray the runner delivers? The ridge point is the exact ratio of chef-speed to runner-speed where the two are perfectly matched — neither waits on the other.

In one line: your throughput is capped by whichever is slower, the chef (compute) or the runner (memory). Count both times, take the max.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real chip can overlap loading and computing (the runner walks while the chef cooks), and it has a tiny fast fridge at the station (on-chip SRAM / cache) that the pantry picture hides. So the true story includes reuse and pipelining — but the core lesson still holds.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of the arithmetic, and why it matters✓

The rules you narrate

  • Training cost ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs. N = tokens, D = parameters. The 6 splits as 2 (forward) + 4 (backward). Inference is different: ~2·D per token — never reuse the 6.
  • One matmul of shapes (M×K)·(K×N) costs 2·M·K·N FLOPs (the factor 2 is the multiply-add), and moves roughly (M·K + K·N + M·N)·bytes_per_number bytes: load both inputs, store the output.
  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes. Ridge point = peak FLOP/s ÷ peak bandwidth. Above ridge = compute-bound; below = memory-bound.
  • Runtime = max(compute time, memory time), because a chip overlaps the two but cannot make either disappear.
✅
intensity = 2·M·K·N ÷ (M·K + K·N + M·N)·bytes
Raise M (batch/rows) with K, N fixed: the K×N weight matrix is loaded once but reused across all M rows, so FLOPs-per-byte climbs and the op moves toward compute-bound.

Why this is the skill labs test

  • Predict before you spend — two numbers (FLOPs, bytes) turn a million-dollar run into a wall-clock estimate on a whiteboard.
  • Find the bottleneck — the roofline tells you whether to buy more compute or faster memory. They are not interchangeable.
  • Where the FLOPs live — inside a layer the FFN matmuls (~4d wide) dominate; softmax, LayerNorm, and bias adds are negligible next to the big matmuls. Knowing this tells you what to optimize.
⚠️
Roofline pitfall: the naive intensity assumes you load every input exactly once and ignores on-chip reuse (tiling / cache). A matmul that looks memory-bound on paper can be compute-bound in practice because a tile of weights is reused across many rows without re-reading HBM. Always ask "how many times is each loaded byte actually reused?" — attention's QKᵀ / AV have low reuse at small batch, which is exactly why long-context inference is memory-bound even when the FFN matmuls are not.
🔗
Causal chain: count multiply-adds → 6·N·D gives compute time · count bytes crossing HBM → gives memory time → the larger one is your runtime → the ratio of the two is arithmetic intensity → place it on the roofline vs. the ridge point → compute-bound or memory-bound verdict.
