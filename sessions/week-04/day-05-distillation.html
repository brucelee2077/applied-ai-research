Phase 1 · Week 4 · Day 5 (Fri)

Distillation
A Small Model That Learned From a Big One

A big model is smart but slow and expensive to run. What if a tiny model could copy most of its skill? Knowledge distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher — not just its answers, but its whole way of being unsure. Today: how, and why it keeps inference cheap.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain what a teacher, a student, and soft targets are; use a temperature to soften a teacher's outputs and reveal its "dark knowledge"; and write the distillation loss that pulls a student toward the teacher.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is knowledge distillation?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you can read Python/NumPy, and you know a classifier outputs a probability for each class (a softmax: a list of numbers between 0 and 1 that add up to 1). No prior distillation experience needed.

The problem: the best model is often too big to run

A large model can be very accurate, but it is slow and costs a lot to run on every request. This cost is called inference — the work of using a trained model to make predictions. You want that accuracy, but at a fraction of the cost.

The idea: train a small model to copy a big one

You keep the big accurate model — call it the teacher. You build a much smaller model — the student. Then you train the student to imitate the teacher's outputs. This copying process is called knowledge distillation. The result is a small, cheap model that keeps a lot of the teacher's quality.

The twist: copy the whole answer, not just the top one

A normal training label is a hard label: a single correct class, like "this image is a 3." That throws away a lot of information. The teacher knows more than that. When it looks at a handwritten "3", it might also think it looks a little like an "8" and not at all like a "1". That relative nuance across the wrong answers is called dark knowledge.

To let the student learn this nuance, you train it on soft targets: the teacher's full probability list, not just its single top pick. To spread those probabilities out and make the nuance visible, you divide the teacher's raw scores by a number called the temperature (written T) before the softmax. A higher T makes the probabilities softer and more spread out.

📦
student learns from → teacher's soft probabilities (temperature T)
The student trains to match these soft targets — and, if you want, the true hard label too. Then you deploy only the small student.
📚
Official docs (optional): Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network (Hinton, Vinyals, Dean, 2015) · Vlad Feinberg — Distillation Walkthrough
2 · INTUITIONAn expert tutor who shows their doubt✓

Imagine two ways of learning from a teacher.

🔑
Hard label = a bare answer key

The key says only: "the answer is A." You learn A is right. You learn nothing about why, or how close B was, or that C was clearly wrong. Most of the teacher's thinking is thrown away.

🧑‍🏫
Soft targets = an expert tutor

A good tutor says: "It's mostly A, but B is plausible, and definitely not C." You absorb that nuance. You learn far more than a bare answer key could ever teach.

In one line: the teacher's soft probabilities carry rich extra hints — its confidence and its near-misses. The student that learns from those hints becomes better than one trained on bare labels alone.

The temperature T is the tutor's willingness to talk out loud. A cold tutor (low T) just blurts the top answer. A warm tutor (high T) explains all the options and how likely each one feels — exposing the dark knowledge.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: the teacher is not a person and has no real "understanding." It is a fixed set of numbers. Distillation just copies its output distribution — it does not copy any deeper reasoning.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of distillation, and why it works✓

The rules

  • Soften with temperature. Take the teacher's logits z, divide by T, then softmax: softmax(z / T). Bigger T → softer, more spread-out probabilities that expose the near-misses. T = 1 is the normal softmax.
  • Soften the student the same way. The student produces its own logits, softened by the same T, so the two are comparable.
  • Match the two distributions. The distillation loss is KL(teacher_soft ‖ student_soft). KL divergence is a number that measures how far apart two probability distributions are; it is 0 when they match. Minimizing it pulls the student's soft output toward the teacher's.
  • Optionally add the true label. You can also add the normal cross-entropy on the real hard label: total = α · KL(...) + (1 − α) · CE(student, true_label). This keeps the student honest about the ground truth.
✅
loss = KL(softmax(z_teacher / T) ‖ softmax(z_student / T))
"Soften both with the same T, then measure the gap." Add cross-entropy on the true label if you want a mix of teacher hints and ground truth.

Why it works

  • More signal per example. A hard label gives one bit ("it's a 3"). Soft targets give a whole distribution — the student learns which classes look similar, which is far richer training signal.
  • Cheap at deploy time. The teacher is only used during training. You ship the small student, so inference stays cheap and fast.
  • Keeps most of the quality. A well-distilled student often reaches close to the teacher's accuracy at a fraction of the size and cost.
🔗
Causal chain: big accurate teacher → soften its outputs with temperature T → exposes dark knowledge (relative probabilities of wrong answers) → student matches those soft targets via KL → a small model with most of the teacher's skill, cheap to serve.

Today's task: write a tiny distillation step. Soften a teacher's logits with a temperature T, soften a student's logits the same way, and compute the KL distillation loss between them.

Acceptance criteria

  • Define teacher logits z_teacher and student logits z_student (e.g. length-3 vectors) with NumPy or JAX.
  • Write soften(z, T) that returns softmax(z / T). Print the teacher's probabilities at T = 1 and at T = 4 to show they spread out.
  • Compute kl_loss = sum(teacher_soft * (log(teacher_soft) − log(student_soft))) — the distillation loss.
  • Print the loss. As a check, confirm the loss is ~0 when the student's logits equal the teacher's.
