Sharding & Parallelism · Week 4 · Day 3

Pipeline ParallelismLayers Across Devices & the Bubble

Your model is too big for one device. One fix: put different layers on different devices and pass the batch through, stage by stage — like an assembly line. But there's a cost: the bubble, the idle time while the line fills up and drains. Today: how to shrink it.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain what a pipeline stage is, describe why the bubble wastes device time, show how micro-batching shrinks it, and compute the bubble fraction (p-1)/(m+p-1) for any number of stages and micro-batches.
1What is pipeline parallelism, and what is the bubble?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you know a neural network is a stack of layers (layer 1 feeds layer 2, which feeds layer 3, and so on), and that a device is one GPU or one TPU chip. No prior parallelism experience needed.

The problem: the model is bigger than one device

A large model can have hundreds of layers and billions of weights. Sometimes the whole model does not fit in the memory of a single device. You cannot run what you cannot store. So you must split the model across several devices.

One way to split is by layer. This is pipeline parallelism (PP): you cut the stack of layers into groups and put each group on a different device.

The idea: layers become stages

Say the model has 8 layers and you have 4 devices. You could do:

  • Device 1 holds layers 1–2
  • Device 2 holds layers 3–4
  • Device 3 holds layers 5–6
  • Device 4 holds layers 7–8

Each device now holds one stage: a stage is just "the group of layers living on one device." A batch of data flows through the pipeline stage by stage. Device 1 does its layers, hands the result to Device 2, which hands its result to Device 3, and so on. This is exactly how work moves down an assembly line.

The catch: the bubble

Here is the problem. When Device 1 is working on the first layers, Devices 2, 3, and 4 have nothing to do yet — the data has not reached them. They sit idle. This wasted idle time is called the bubble. The same thing happens at the end: once the data has passed Device 1, it goes idle while the later devices finish.

So a naive pipeline wastes a lot of device time waiting. The fix — micro-batching — is the heart of today's lesson, and we'll build it up in the playground.

📚
Official docs (optional): GPipe: Efficient Training of Giant Neural Networks (Huang et al., 2018) — the paper that introduced pipeline micro-batching.
😕
为什么这里容易卡住 · Why this trips people up: 把模型按层切开听着简单，但"为什么设备会空转、又怎么靠 micro-batch 把空转填满"这一步，不在脑子里跑一遍时间线就想不通。In plain terms: splitting by layer is easy to picture, but the bubble — why devices sit idle and how micro-batching overlaps them — only clicks once you trace the timeline step by step.
2An assembly line of workersIntuition

Picture a factory line with 4 workers. Each worker does one step, then passes the product to the next worker. The product must travel down the whole line before it is finished.

🧍💤💤💤
one product = huge bubble

Send in a single product. Worker 1 builds their part, then workers 2, 3, 4 wait. When it reaches worker 4, workers 1, 2, 3 are done and idle. Most of the line stands still — that idle time is the bubble.

🧍🏗️🏗️🏗️
many products = full line

Now feed in many small products back-to-back. As soon as worker 1 passes product A to worker 2, worker 1 starts product B. Soon every worker is busy on a different product. The line stays full.

In one line: devices are the workers, layers are the work each worker does, and feeding many small micro-batches (small pieces of the batch) back-to-back keeps every device busy — so the bubble shrinks.

直觉 / Intuition: 把设备当流水线工人，层是每人做的工序 —— 只送一个产品，大家轮流干、其余人干等(bubble)；送很多小产品(micro-batch)，很快人人都在忙。The technical point is that feeding m micro-batches back-to-back overlaps the stages, so the idle bubble fraction (p−1)/(m+p−1) shrinks as m grows.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real workers can each build a whole product alone. Pipeline devices cannot — each device only holds some of the layers, so every micro-batch must pass through all stages in order. The dependency is strict.

4The rules of the pipeline, and why micro-batching winsHow & why

The rules

  • Split the model's layers into p stages, one per device. A batch flows through stage 1, then stage 2, ..., then stage p.
  • A stage can only start once the stage before it has passed along its result. This strict order is why early devices work first and later devices wait.
  • The bubble is the total idle time. It has two parts: the fill (start-up, while later stages wait for data to arrive) and the drain (wind-down, while early stages wait for the last data to finish).
  • Micro-batching: split the batch into m smaller micro-batches and feed them in one after another. While stage 1 works on micro-batch 2, stage 2 can already work on micro-batch 1. The stages overlap, so idle time shrinks.

The bubble fraction

✅
bubble fraction ≈ (p - 1) / (m + p - 1)
p = number of stages, m = number of micro-batches. It is the share of device time that is wasted (idle). Fill + drain together cost about p - 1 time slots; total useful work spans about m + p - 1 slots.

The key insight: p - 1 is fixed by how many stages you have, but you can make m as large as you like. Push m up and the fraction falls toward zero. That is the whole trick.

Why it matters

  • It lets huge models fit — no single device needs to hold all the layers, so you can train models far bigger than one chip's memory.
  • The bubble is the price — split naively (m = 1) and you waste most of your expensive devices. Micro-batching buys most of that time back.
  • It composes — pipeline parallelism stacks with tensor parallelism (Day 2) and data parallelism (Day 1). Real large-scale training uses all three together.
🔗
Causal chain: model too big for one device → split layers into stages across devices → strict stage order creates idle bubble → split the batch into many micro-batches → stages overlap → bubble fraction (p-1)/(m+p-1) shrinks as m grows.
🪜
Math Ladder — the pipeline bubble fraction
1 · In words: the share of device time wasted waiting is the fixed fill+drain cost divided by the total time; adding micro-batches grows the denominator, so the waste shrinks.
2 · The formula: bubble ≈ (p − 1) / (m + p − 1) — p = number of stages (devices); m = number of micro-batches; p−1 = fill + drain slots (fixed by stage count); m+p−1 = total slots the work spans.
3 · Tiny numbers: p=4 → m=1: 3/4 = 0.75 (75% idle!); m=8: 3/11 ≈ 0.27; m=32: 3/35 ≈ 0.09. More micro-batches, less bubble.
4 · Sanity check: as m → ∞ the bubble → 0 but never reaches it (the p−1 numerator never disappears). If your formula hits exactly 0 at a finite m, you dropped the p−1.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Overlapping micro-batches is what buys the bubble back, but overlap is also where the subtlest pipeline bug lives — a weight that changed underneath a micro-batch.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): stale weights across micro-batches. The backward pass for a micro-batch must use the same weights its forward pass used. If a stage applies an optimizer update before every in-flight micro-batch has finished its backward — or if the forward and backward of a micro-batch straddle a weight update — the gradient is computed against weights that no longer exist. Nothing crashes: shapes match, the loss keeps dropping, but the update is subtly wrong and training converges slower or to a worse point than the same model on one device. A senior engineer checks in code review that the schedule (e.g. 1F1B) delays the optimizer step until all micro-batches for the step have completed backward, so every micro-batch's forward and backward see one consistent weight version.
⚖️
Trade-off: shrink the bubble vs. hold more activations. Raising m drives the bubble fraction (p-1)/(m+p-1) toward zero, so it is tempting to push m as high as possible. But every in-flight micro-batch must keep its forward activations resident until its backward pass reaches it, so peak activation memory grows with how many micro-batches are outstanding. In a design review the decision is how far to push m: far enough to hide the bubble, not so far that activation memory overflows the device or per-micro-batch overhead (communication, kernel launches) eats the savings.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Pipeline parallelism splits a model's layers into p stages, one per device, so a model too big for one chip fits — but the strict stage order leaves later devices idle at the start (fill) and early ones idle at the end (drain): the bubble, fraction (p−1)/(m+p−1). Micro-batching feeds m small batches so stages overlap and the bubble shrinks toward zero as m grows. The nuance is the silent failure: a micro-batch's backward must see the same weights as its forward, so if an optimizer step lands mid-flight (not a 1F1B schedule) the gradient is stale — no crash, just worse convergence — and raising m costs activation memory."
