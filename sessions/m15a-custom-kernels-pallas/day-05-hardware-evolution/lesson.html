Hardware Evolution
FlashAttention 1 → 2 → 3

Thursday you learned why tiling and online softmax keep the giant attention matrix out of slow off-chip memory. Today you trace how that same algorithm's low-level implementation was rebuilt twice more, once per GPU generation, to squeeze out the speed each new chip made possible.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain what changed between FlashAttention-1, -2, and -3 (and why each rewrite was forced by specific hardware, not just cleverness), and sketch the producer/consumer warp split that FA3 pioneered on Hopper — the same pattern you'll need to hand-tune in raw CUDA/Pallas next week with ThunderKittens.
1 · WHAT IS ITSame algorithm, three kernels — what does that mean?✓
🧭
What this assumes: Thursday's lesson on FlashAttention's core idea — tiling Q, K, V into blocks and using online softmax so the full N×N attention score matrix never gets written to HBM. No new algorithm today.

The problem: one algorithm, three GPUs

Here is the twist for today: FlashAttention-1, FlashAttention-2, and FlashAttention-3 compute the exact same math. Tiled QK^T, online softmax, then multiply by V — never materializing the full score matrix. So why three papers, three years apart?

Because a GPU is not just "a fast calculator." It is a specific piece of hardware with specific parts: a huge slow memory called HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory, about 80GB, sits off-chip), a tiny fast memory called SRAM (about 20MB, sits on-chip, also called "shared memory" or SMEM), and units that do the actual math, called Tensor Cores. Each new GPU generation changes what these parts can do. When the hardware changes, the fastest way to run the same math changes too — even though the math itself does not.

The three rewrites, in one sentence each

  • FlashAttention-1 (2022, A100): stop making so many trips to slow HBM. Keep tiles in fast SRAM instead. This is called being IO-aware — counting memory trips as the real cost, not raw math operations.
  • FlashAttention-2 (2023, A100): the memory problem is fixed, but now most of the GPU's parallel workers sit idle. Split the work better so more of them stay busy at once.
  • FlashAttention-3 (2024, H100): a brand new GPU (Hopper) arrives with parts that can work without waiting on each other. Redesign the kernel so loading data and doing math happen at the same time, on separate hardware units.

So what's today's catch?

The catch is that FA2 is not "a smarter FA1," and FA3 is not "a smarter FA2." FA1 to FA2 is a work-scheduling fix — still one instruction at a time, in order. FA1/FA2 to FA3 is a completely different execution style — multiple things happening simultaneously, on purpose. Mixing these two kinds of change up is the single most common mistake people make when they first read this history.

📚
Official papers (optional, cited exactly as published): FlashAttention (2205.14135) · flash-attention GitHub repo · FlashAttention-2 (2307.08691) · FlashAttention-3 (2407.08608) · Tri Dao's FA3 blog post
2 · INTUITIONA kitchen redesigned three times✓

Picture one restaurant kitchen, redesigned three times as it gets busier and gets new equipment.

🧊➡️🧑‍🍳
FA1: stop walking to the freezer

The owner notices cooks keep walking to the walk-in freezer (HBM) mid-dish. Fix: prep in small batches, keep ingredients on the countertop mini-fridge (SRAM), never make a special trip to the freezer while cooking.

👩‍🍳👨‍🍳👩‍🍳
FA2: give everyone a station

The freezer-trip problem is fixed, but only 1 of 4 cooks works at a time — they take turns. Fix: assign fixed stations so all 4 cooks chop, grill, and plate at once, in parallel.

🎢🔥
FA3: a conveyor belt + two stoves at once

A new expediter's conveyor belt (TMA) carries ingredients to a station automatically, without a cook stopping to fetch them. And a rule: the slow sauce-reduction step (softmax) runs on one stove while the fast grill (matmul) runs on another — nobody just stands there waiting.

In one line: the recipe (the math) never changes. What changes is how the kitchen (the GPU) is organized to cook it — and each redesign follows a new piece of equipment arriving.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real cooks can freely improvise mid-task — grab a knife if the conveyor belt is briefly idle. FA3's "producer" and "consumer" warps cannot. Each warp is locked into one role (only load, or only compute) for the entire kernel run. There is no equivalent of a cook switching jobs mid-shift.

4 · HOW & WHYThe three engineering problems, and why each is different✓

Three separate problems, not one

  • Memory traffic (FA1's problem). Standard attention writes the full N×N score matrix to HBM, then reads it back for softmax, then reads it again for the final multiply. FA1's tiling and online softmax mean the score matrix is never fully written to HBM at all — traffic drops from O(N²) to roughly O(N²d/M), where M is SRAM size.
  • Work partitioning (FA2's problem). Even with memory fixed, FA1 only reached 25–40% of the A100's peak FLOPs/s. The bottleneck was occupancy — how many of the GPU's parallel workers (Streaming Multiprocessors, or SMs) have work at any instant — and wasted shared-memory traffic between warps (a warp = 32 threads moving in lockstep). FA2 fixed this with better parallelization across sequence length and less redundant warp-to-warp traffic.
  • Asynchrony (FA3's problem). FA2 is still synchronous: one warp does load, then compute, then softmax, then the next load — one thing at a time, in order. Hopper (H100) gave software two new pieces of hardware that let load and compute happen simultaneously: TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator, a dedicated async copy engine) and async WGMMA (a warpgroup-wide matmul instruction that frees the issuing warp immediately).

Why this progression matters

✅
Warp-specialization + pingpong scheduling. FA3 splits warps into producer warps (only issue TMA loads) and consumer warps (only issue WGMMA + softmax), running as a software pipeline. Then it deliberately schedules warpgroup A's slow softmax to run while warpgroup B's fast matmul executes on the Tensor Cores — then swaps roles next iteration.
This is why FA3 lifted H100 utilization from FA2's 35% to 75% — it hides the slow non-matmul work entirely under the fast matmul work.

Why it's a genuinely new engineering step, not a tuning tweak

  • FA1 → FA2 is a scheduling fix. Same synchronous, one-instruction-at-a-time execution model — just distributed better across more workers.
  • FA2 → FA3 is a different execution model. Asynchronous, hardware-specialized warp roles. This only became possible because Hopper's TMA and WGMMA exist — Ampere (A100) had no equivalent.
  • Sets up next week. Hand-tuning this level of asynchrony in raw CUDA/Pallas is exactly what FA3's authors had to do by hand — which is why a dedicated DSL (ThunderKittens) exists to make this pattern reusable.
🔗
Causal chain: tiling + online softmax (Thursday) solves memory traffic → work-partitioning (FA2) solves occupancy on the same hardware → new Hopper primitives (TMA, async WGMMA) enable a new execution model → warp-specialization + pingpong scheduling (FA3) hides softmax's slowness under matmul's speed.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Asynchrony is what makes FA3 fast, and it is also what makes it dangerous: the moment load and compute run at the same time, forgetting a single wait turns a fast kernel into a quietly wrong one.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): the missing async wait (consuming a tile before TMA finishes). In the synchronous world (FA2) the load fully completes before compute starts, so the data is always ready. FA3 breaks that: a producer warp issues a TMA copy and moves on, and a consumer warp must wait on that copy's completion before doing the matmul. Drop that wait and the consumer reads the tile while it is still being filled — it does not crash, and on your machine the copy may usually finish first, so tests pass. On a busier chip or a longer sequence the timing shifts and the output is quietly wrong, differently each run. In code review, a senior engineer checks that every async TMA load has a matching wait (or barrier) before its data is used, and treats a green run on one GPU as no proof at all that the synchronization is correct.
⚖️
Trade-off: peak utilization vs. hardware lock-in and complexity. Warp-specialization and pingpong scheduling lifted the H100 from 35% to 75% utilization — a large real win. But that win is bought by writing directly against Hopper's TMA and async WGMMA, so the kernel is tied to one hardware generation: Ampere has no equivalent, and the next chip may need a rewrite. It is also far harder to reason about than a synchronous loop, which is exactly why a DSL like ThunderKittens exists to make the pattern reusable. In a design review, the call is whether this kernel is hot and long-lived enough to justify hand-owning a hardware-specific asynchronous pipeline rather than accepting a simpler, more portable kernel that leaves some peak on the table.
