Mixture-of-ExpertsFundamentals

A dense transformer's feed-forward block makes every token pay the full compute cost of every weight. What if a token only needed to visit a handful of specialists out of hundreds? Today: how Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) decouples how much a model knows from how expensive each token is.

1What is a Mixture-of-Experts layer, and why does it exist?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Week 6's inference mechanics — prefill vs. decode, the memory wall, and roofline analysis (memory-bound vs. compute-bound). No prior MoE experience needed.

The problem: dense models couple "how much it knows" to "how much it costs"

A standard ("dense") transformer feed-forward block (FFN) uses the exact same weight matrices for every single token that passes through it. If you want the model to know more things, your only lever is to make those weight matrices bigger. But then every token pays the full compute cost of that bigger matrix, in every layer, on every forward pass. Capacity and cost are locked together.

This matters even more once you know Week 6's framing: bigger dense models push you further into being compute-bound per token, since every parameter you add gets multiplied against every token. What if you could add far more parameters without paying for them on every token?

The answer: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)

A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces one dense FFN with N separate small FFNs, called experts — each one is just a normal FFN with its own independent weights, nothing exotic. Sitting in front of the experts is a small learned network called the gating network (or router) — usually one linear layer plus a softmax. For each token, the router picks the top k experts (often k=1 or k=2, far smaller than N, which can be hundreds) and sends the token only to those. Every other expert does zero work for that token.

📦
This is called conditional computation or sparse activation: which parameters get used depends on the input itself, instead of being fixed.
Result: total parameters scale with N (you can have thousands of experts), but compute (FLOPs) per token scales only with k. Capacity and per-token cost are decoupled.

Where this came from

Shazeer et al. 2017 ("Outrageously Large Neural Networks") first made this work at scale, introducing noisy top-k gating and an auxiliary loss to fix load imbalance. GShard (2020) ported the idea to Transformers with top-2 routing and automatic-sharding annotations for splitting one MoE layer across thousands of accelerator cores. Switch Transformer (2021) simplified to top-1 routing, added a hard per-expert capacity limit, and scaled to 1.6 trillion parameters.

📚
Official papers (optional): Switch Transformer · Shazeer et al. 2017 · GShard · Inference mechanics (Pope et al.) · MoE systems talk
2A hospital of specialists, not one overworked doctorIntuition

Imagine a large hospital with 128 specialist doctors — cardiologist, dermatologist, oncologist, and so on — instead of one general practitioner who is expected to personally know all of medicine.

🧑‍⚕️
Dense = one GP knows everything

To make the GP smarter, you'd have to stuff more and more medicine into one brain. Every patient still only sees this one (now huge) brain.

🏥
MoE = 128 specialists, triage nurse routes you

A patient (a token) walks in. A triage nurse (the router) reads the symptoms and sends them to the 1 or 2 most relevant specialists — not all 128.

In one line: the hospital as a whole holds huge collective expertise (128 specialists' worth = large parameter count), but any one patient's visit only consumes 1-2 doctors' time (small compute per token).

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real triage nurse uses good judgment. The router starts as a raw, untrained linear layer and has to learn sensible routing purely from the training signal — left alone, it tends to collapse onto favoring a few "popular" doctors, which is why an explicit load-balancing loss must force it to spread patients around. Also, a real specialist can gracefully decline an overbooked slot; in Switch Transformer, when an expert's fixed capacity is exceeded, excess tokens are simply dropped — not politely rescheduled.

4The rules of routing, and why it matters for inferenceHow & why

The rules

  • The router is one linear layer W_g plus softmax. It maps each token's hidden vector to a probability over N experts.
  • KeepTopK keeps only the top k logits and sets the rest to -infinity before the softmax — not after. The unselected experts get exactly zero gate weight, and their matmuls are never computed at all.
  • Each expert's fixed capacity C caps how many tokens it will accept per batch. Overflow tokens are dropped for that layer and pass through via a residual/skip connection instead — a real design decision in Switch Transformer, not a hypothetical.
  • An auxiliary load-balancing loss is added to the task loss to stop the router from collapsing onto a few popular experts.
✅
G(x) = softmax(KeepTopK(H(x), k))
"Score every expert, keep only the top k scores, softmax over just those." The other N−k experts receive no gradient from this token — not a small one, exactly zero.

Why it matters — especially for inference

  • Capacity without per-token cost — total parameters scale with N (hundreds to thousands of experts), but FLOPs per token scale only with k, independent of N.
  • Memory-bound, not compute-bound — connecting to Week 6: since routing is data-dependent, all experts' weights must sit in accelerator memory even though only k are touched per token. MoE inference pushes further into the memory-bound regime from Pope et al.'s roofline framing, even though its per-token FLOPs stay low.
  • Distributed by construction — GShard's sharding annotations let one MoE layer split across thousands of cores, with dispatch/un-dispatch implemented as all-to-all communication.
🔗
Causal chain: dense FFN couples capacity to per-token cost → MoE breaks that coupling via sparse activation → cheap per-token compute, but huge total memory footprint → directly reshapes the memory/compute ratio from Week 6's roofline analysis.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

MoE gets its power by only running a few experts per token. That same design hides two things a staff engineer looks for: tokens that quietly get no expert at all, and the memory bill you pay for experts you rarely use.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): dropped tokens at the capacity cap. Each expert accepts at most C tokens per batch. When more than C tokens route to one expert, the overflow tokens are dropped — they skip the expert's FFN and pass through untouched on the residual path. There is no crash and no error: the batch still runs and loss still goes down. But those tokens got a shallower computation than the rest, so quality is quietly worse for exactly the inputs that were most popular. In code review a senior engineer asks to log the per-batch drop rate (tokens dropped ÷ tokens routed); a healthy run keeps it low and stable, and a rising drop rate flags trouble long before the eval metric moves.
⚖️
Trade-off: FLOPs scale with k, but memory scales with N. Sparse activation buys you a huge parameter count at low per-token compute — that is the whole point. What you pay is memory: because routing is data-dependent, every expert's weights must sit in accelerator memory even though only k of N are used for any given token. In a design review this is the decision to make on purpose: more experts raise total capacity and the memory footprint (and push you deeper into the memory-bound regime) without raising per-token FLOPs, so you are trading VRAM and sharding complexity for capacity, not trading compute.

7Build a from-scratch MoE gating layer in JAXProduce

Today's task (from the curriculum): implement a single MoE feed-forward layer in pure JAX — router, noisy top-k gating, stacked expert FFNs, capacity overflow, and the load-balancing loss — on a synthetic batch of token vectors.

Acceptance criteria

  • Router: one linear layer producing logits over N=8 experts (N configurable).
  • Noisy top-k gating per Shazeer et al. (test both k=1 and k=2), with the noise term only active in a "training mode" flag.
  • N independent 2-layer expert FFNs as a stacked weight tensor [N, d_model, d_ff] (e.g. d_model=64, d_ff=256, N=8), dispatched with jnp.take / vmap / one-hot masking.
  • A per-expert capacity limit with explicit drop-and-residual overflow handling.
  • The load-balancing loss L_aux = alpha * N * sum(f_i * P_i), computed and printed per batch.
  • On a synthetic batch of 512 tokens: prints per-expert token counts, the argmax expert-choice histogram, and L_aux.
  • Demonstrates the imbalance failure mode: a skewed router gives a large L_aux; a balanced one gives a small L_aux.
  • Demonstrates capacity overflow: with capacity_factor=1.0 and a skewed router, at least one expert overflows; an assertion confirms overflowed tokens' outputs equal their residual input.
  • A parameter/FLOPs sanity print: total parameters scale with N, while FLOPs-per-token stays roughly constant as N grows with k fixed.
