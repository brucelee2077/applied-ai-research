Mixture of Experts · Week 7 · Day 2

MoE Scaling DeviationsWhy Sparse Models Are Data-Hungry

Yesterday you met Mixture-of-Experts: many "expert" sub-networks, a router picks a few per token. Today's question is sharper: if you have a fixed compute budget, does the Chinchilla recipe you already trust for dense models — split it evenly between parameters and data — still hold? It does not, and the reason changes how you'd actually train one.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain why MoE's compute-optimal token-to-parameter ratio is not the dense ~20-tokens-per-parameter rule, define "granularity" and why it forces a refit of the scaling law, and never again confuse total parameters with active parameters when plugging numbers into C ≈ 6ND.
1What is a "scaling law," and what breaks for MoE?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: Day 1's MoE fundamentals (experts, router, top-k, sparse activation) and basic transformer training vocabulary (parameters, tokens, FLOPs). No prior scaling-law math needed.

Quick recap: what is a Mixture-of-Experts layer?

A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces one dense feed-forward network (FFN) with many parallel copies of that FFN, called experts, plus a small router (also called a gate) that picks which expert(s) handle each token. Only a handful of experts — often 1 or 2 out of dozens or hundreds — actually run for any given token. This is called conditional computation or sparse activation: total parameter count across all experts can be huge, but the FLOPs spent per token stay roughly fixed, because the experts that were not picked simply never get multiplied into that forward pass.

What is a scaling law?

A scaling law is a fitted equation that predicts a model's loss (how wrong its predictions are, on average) from the resources you spend on it — mainly parameters N, training tokens D, and total compute C. The standard approximation is C ≈ 6ND FLOPs — this "6" comes from counting multiply-adds in a transformer's forward and backward pass, the same convention Kaplan et al. and the Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022) both use.

Compute-optimal means: for a fixed budget C, what is the best way to split it between N and D to get the lowest possible loss? For dense transformers, Chinchilla's answer was: split it roughly evenly — both N and D should grow as C^0.5. That is where the famous "~20 tokens per parameter" rule of thumb for dense models comes from.

Today's catch: MoE breaks that even split

MoE models activate only a few experts per token, so most of an MoE model's total parameters sit idle for any single token. Krajewski et al. (2024, Scaling Laws for Fine-Grained Mixture of Experts) and Clark et al. (2022, DeepMind's Unified Scaling Laws for Routed Language Models) both found the same thing: adding more total parameters to an MoE model helps loss much less, per parameter, than it does for a dense model. To reach the same loss as a dense model, an MoE model needs relatively more training tokens per active parameter than the Chinchilla ratio predicts. This is why the curriculum calls MoE "significantly more data-hungry" — and why today's job is understanding exactly where that extra hunger comes from.

📚
Primary sources (optional): Feinberg — Princeton talk on MoE scaling · Fedus et al. — Switch Transformer (arXiv:2101.03961) · Lepikhin et al. — GShard (arXiv:2006.16668)
2A consulting firm of 200 narrow specialistsIntuition

Think about hiring for a big consulting firm.

🧑‍💼🧑‍💼🧑‍💼
Dense = 10 generalists

Every project, all 10 show up and everyone contributes a little. To make the firm smarter, hire more generalists (more N) or give the existing ones more casework (more D) — roughly balancing the two works best.

🧑‍🔬×200
MoE = 200 specialists, 2 called per project

Total headcount (total parameters) explodes, but billable work per project (active compute per token) barely changes. Most specialists sit on the bench for any given client.

The catch: hiring specialist #187, an expert in some rare niche, doesn't make the firm smarter on your average client's problem — unless the firm sees enough client projects (data) that specialist #187 actually gets called in often enough to get good at it, and gets correctly matched to the right work by the dispatcher (router). So the value you extract from all that specialist headcount depends far more heavily on volume of casework (D) than the generalist firm did. Adding specialists without adding enough casework leaves most of them undertrained and mismatched — capacity that exists on paper but is never cashed in as skill.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real MoE experts aren't trained on disjoint human-chosen niches. The router itself learns — and constantly reshuffles — which tokens go where during training, with no guarantee of clean specialization. Also, unlike consultants who keep accumulated case history, an expert's "usefulness" in the scaling-law sense is about statistical training-signal density per expert, not literal memory of past cases.

4The rule: active parameters, granularity, and a steeper betaHow & why

The rules

  • The dense loss form is L(N, D) = E + A/N^alpha + B/D^beta: irreducible entropy E, a parameter-limited term, a data-limited term. For dense models, Chinchilla's fitted alpha ≈ beta, giving the balanced N_opt ∝ C^0.5, D_opt ∝ C^0.5 split.
  • In MoE, the router picks k experts out of E_total per token. Only the FLOPs of those k experts count toward C ≈ 6ND — so "N" in that formula must mean active parameters (N_active = k × params-per-expert), never total parameters (N_total = E_total × params-per-expert).
  • Granularity (G_gran, from Krajewski et al. 2024) splits each expert into more, smaller experts — same total expert-layer parameters, same active FLOPs, more distinct experts the router can choose among.
  • Fitting L(N, D, G_gran) needs an extra granularity/interaction term beyond the plain dense form — a single global beta does not describe MoE. The fitted data exponent behaves as if it were steeper for MoE.
✅
D_opt / N_active_opt for MoE grows faster with C than the dense ~20-tokens/param ratio.
Same compute budget as a dense model, but the compute-optimal split calls for measurably more tokens relative to active parameters — not fewer parameters.

Why it matters

  • Idle parameters need more data to earn their keep — each MoE expert only gets gradient signal from the fraction of tokens routed to it, so reaching the same per-parameter training adequacy as a dense parameter (which sees every token) needs proportionally more total training tokens.
  • Total parameters are cheap; data is the real cost — Shazeer's own phrase for MoE, "outrageously large," refers to total parameter count. It is cheap to add more experts, but it only pays off if D is scaled up to match.
  • Production consequence — Mixtral, DeepSeek-MoE, and Switch-C are all trained on token counts far exceeding what a same-active-size dense model would use, precisely because of this steeper data exponent.
🔗
Causal chain: sparse routing (Day 1) → most expert parameters see only a fraction of tokens → per-parameter training signal is diluted → fitted data exponent effectively steepens → compute-optimal D/N_active ratio rises above the dense ~20-tokens/param rule.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

MoE scaling has one number that everyone mixes up (which "N" goes into the formula) and one temptation that always looks free (just add more experts). Both fail quietly — the run trains fine and only the final quality tells you something was off.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): sizing the token budget from total parameters. If you plan D with the dense ~20-tokens-per-parameter rule but use N_total (all experts) instead of N_active (routed experts), the arithmetic tells you to train on far more tokens than a same-FLOP dense model — which sounds right, so nobody questions it. But the two mistakes can also point the other way: teams reuse a dense token budget for a much larger total-parameter MoE and quietly undertrain it. Either way there is no crash and loss still falls — the sparse capacity just never earns its keep, and the model underperforms a dense baseline at the same active size. In code review a senior engineer checks exactly which parameter count feeds the token-budget calculation, and confirms C ≈ 6 × N_active × D uses active, not total, parameters.
⚖️
Trade-off: cheap total parameters bought with expensive data. Adding experts (raising N_total) barely moves per-token FLOPs, so it looks almost free — that is why MoE is called "outrageously large." What you pay is on the data side: because each expert sees only its slice of tokens, the compute-optimal D/N_active ratio is higher than dense, so more experts only pay off if you also scale D up to feed them. In a design review the choice is capacity-per-FLOP versus the data (and data-collection) budget needed to train that capacity to adequacy.

7Measure the beta gap yourself, at toy scaleProduce

Today's artifact: a small reproducible JAX experiment that empirically tests the qualitative claim — MoE's compute-optimal token/parameter ratio grows faster than a dense model's as compute budget increases — on a toy scale where full training runs are cheap.

Acceptance criteria

  • sessions/m14a-mixture-of-experts/day-02-moe-scaling-deviations/experiment.py trains a small dense transformer and a small top-k routed MoE transformer (matched active-FLOPs budget per step) at 3–4 increasing compute budgets, and fits a two-term power law L ≈ E + B/D^beta separately for dense and MoE runs.
  • results.csv (or results.json) records final loss per (model_type, N_active, D) triple — at least 2 model types × 3 budgets = 6+ rows.
  • plot_beta_comparison.png shows a loss-vs-tokens log-log plot for dense vs. MoE, with the fitted beta exponents annotated on the plot — two distinct fitted lines.
  • writeup.md states the measured beta_dense vs. beta_moe, whether the direction matches Krajewski et al./Feinberg's qualitative claim, and one caveat about how far a toy-scale run can be trusted to reproduce a trillion-parameter-regime finding. A negative or null result is an acceptable, honest outcome — say so if the toy scale is too small to show the effect.
  • Runs end-to-end on CPU or a single GPU in under ~20 minutes total.
⚠️
The gotcha: never conflate total parameters with active parameters when plugging a number into C ≈ 6ND or comparing an MoE model's size to a dense model's "fairly." A 47B-total-parameter MoE model might have only ~13B active parameters per token — comparing its total count against a 13B dense model's token requirements produces a wildly wrong compute-optimal estimate. Always ask "active or total?" first.
