Module 22c · Generate · Day 2

Flow
Matching

Diffusion models make beautiful images, but they take many small, noisy, winding steps to get there — slow. Flow matching asks a simpler question: what if you learned the straight-line direction from noise to data, and just followed it? Instead of many random-looking steps, the model learns one thing — a velocity field, an arrow at every point telling you which way to move. Follow the arrows along a nearly straight path and you reach real data in far fewer steps. This is the engine inside frontier image and video models.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain flow matching as learning a velocity field that carries noise to data along near-straight paths, say why straight paths need fewer sampling steps than diffusion's winding ones, and compute one straight-line velocity by hand.
1 · WHAT IS ITLearn the direction from noise to data✓
🧭
What this assumes: from Day 1 you know a flow carries simple noise to complex data. You have also met diffusion (Module 22b): it starts from noise and removes a little noise at a time over many steps. Today you learn a cleaner, faster way to make the same journey.

The problem: diffusion is slow

A diffusion model reaches data by taking many small denoising steps — often dozens or hundreds. Each step is cheap, but you need so many that generating one image is slow. The path it takes through the space is also winding, not direct.

The idea: learn a velocity field

Flow matching trains the model to output a velocity field — at every point, an arrow saying "move this way, this fast" to head from noise toward data. To make a sample, start at a noise point and follow the arrows forward in time. The model only ever has to answer one question: which way should this point move right now?

The trick: make the target path straight

You get to choose the training target. Rectified flow uses the simplest possible path: the straight line from a noise sample to a data sample. Along a straight line the correct velocity is constant — always pointing the same way. Learning a constant-direction arrow is easy, and following it is fast.

Why straight beats winding

A winding path forces you to take many tiny steps, or you overshoot the curve. A straight path can be crossed in a few big steps — even, in the ideal case, one. Fewer steps means faster generation with the same quality. That speed is why flow matching powers today's frontier image and video systems. Keep going 👇

2 · INTUITIONA straight highway vs a winding mountain road✓
🛣️
The straight highway

Two towns, Noise and Data. Flow matching builds a straight highway between them. On a straight road you can point the wheel once and drive fast — a few big presses of the pedal and you arrive. That is following a constant-direction velocity.

⛰️
The winding mountain road

Diffusion takes the mountain road between the same two towns. It bends left and right the whole way, so you must slow down and steer at every turn — many tiny careful moves. Same start and end, far more steps.

In one line: flow matching learns an arrow at every point that steers noise toward data along a near-straight road, so you reach data in a few big steps instead of many small ones.

The velocity field is like a giant map covered in arrows. At any spot you look down, read the arrow, and take a step that way. Because the target road is straight, the arrows all point roughly the same direction, so a few steps carry you the whole way. On a winding road the arrows keep changing, which is why diffusion needs so many.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real highway is one fixed road for everyone. In flow matching each noise starting point has its own straight line to its own data point, and the model learns the whole field of these lines at once — not a single shared road. The paths can also bend a little in practice, which is why we say "nearly" straight.

4 · MECHANISM & WHYThe straight-line velocity, worked out by hand✓

Step 1 — in plain words

Pick a noise point and a data point. Draw the straight line between them. To travel that line in one unit of time, the velocity you need is simply "where you are going minus where you started". That single arrow points from noise straight at the data, and on a straight line it never changes. The model is trained to output this arrow at every point and time.

Step 2 — the formula

🧮
velocity = data − noise
position(t) = noise + t · velocity
# t goes from 0 (all noise) to 1 (all data)

Symbols: noise = the random starting point. data = the real target point. velocity = the arrow to follow, equal to data − noise. t = time, running from 0 to 1. At t=0 you are at the noise; at t=1 you have arrived at the data. The velocity is the same at every t, which is what makes the path straight.

Step 3 — a worked example

Say the noise point is 2 and the data point is 8 (using single numbers to keep it simple):

🔢
noise = 2, data = 8
velocity = data − noise = 8 − 2 = 6

halfway (t=0.5): 2 + 0.5·6 = 5
arrival (t=1.0): 2 + 1.0·6 = 8 # exactly the data

The velocity is 6 the whole way. At half time you are at 5 (right in the middle), and at full time you land on 8 — exactly the data. Because the arrow never changed, one big step of size 6 would also land on 8. Straight path, few steps.

Why this is a big deal

  • Fewer sampling steps: a straight path can be crossed in a handful of big steps, so generating an image is much faster than diffusion's many small ones.
  • Simple training target: the model only has to predict data − noise, a plain arrow, instead of a noisy score at every level.
  • Same or better quality: with far fewer steps, flow-matching models match or beat diffusion, which is why frontier image and video systems use them.
🔗
The big picture: flow matching keeps Day 1's "carry noise to data" idea but drops the strict-reversibility rule for speed, learning a velocity field instead. Tomorrow you meet consistency models, which go one step further: jump straight to the endpoint in a single leap.
