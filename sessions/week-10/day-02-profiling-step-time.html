Profiling
the Step Time

Wall-clock timing tells you your training step took 43ms. It never tells you why. Today you open the black box with JAX's built-in profiler: capture a real device trace of your addition-transformer's training step, and read it to catch operations that aren't compiling efficiently to hardware.

1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a profiler, and what is a trace?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you already have a @jax.jit-wrapped training step for your addition-transformer from earlier weeks. No prior profiling experience needed.

The problem: "43ms" tells you nothing

You wrap your training step in @jax.jit, time it with time.time(), and get "43ms per step". That number tells you that something happened. It tells you nothing about why it took 43ms instead of 20ms. Was the matrix multiply the bottleneck? Was the accelerator sitting idle, waiting on something? You can't tell from one number.

JAX's jit already does a lot of automatic optimization behind the scenes — merging small operations together, choosing good memory layouts, overlapping compute with communication. But it is not perfect, and your own code can accidentally defeat it. You need a way to look inside the compiled function.

The answer: a trace

A trace is a recording of every operation that ran on every device (and on the host CPU) during a time window, with a start time, a duration, and a name for each one. It is a very detailed receipt for one training step — not just the total, but every line item.

When JAX JIT-compiles your Python function, the XLA compiler breaks it into a graph of low-level operations: matrix multiplies, reshapes, elementwise adds, and "fusions" that merge several small operations into one hardware kernel. Each of these is called an XLA op. The trace shows these XLA-level names, not your original Python line numbers — that is the first surprise for newcomers: you will not see attention_block, you will see something like fusion.312.

📦
with jax.profiler.trace(log_dir): ...  →  writes a trace file recording exactly what every device and the host did, microsecond by microsecond.
Think of wall-clock timing as a stopwatch on the whole race. A trace is the full slow-motion replay, with a timestamp on every single move.

So what's today's catch?

You need a way to actually view a trace file. That is what a trace viewer is: a timeline UI — either TensorBoard's Profile tab, or the standalone Perfetto UI — that renders the trace as horizontal tracks (one per device core, one for the host), where each operation is a colored rectangle whose width is its duration. Gaps between rectangles are idle time, and spotting those gaps is the single most useful skill in this whole exercise.

2 · INTUITIONThe Formula 1 pit stop replay✓

Think of your training step as a pit stop in a Formula 1 race. Two ways to measure it:

⏱️
wall-clock = stopwatch on the whole stop

The pit crew chief holds a stopwatch on the entire stop and says "11.2 seconds." True, but useless for improvement — you don't know which part was slow.

🎥
trace = multi-camera slow-motion replay

A timestamped overlay on every crew member: front-left tire (2.1s), fuel hose (waited 3.4s for the last stage), rear jack (0.9s, fully overlapped with tire work). Now "why was it slow" is a fact, not a guess.

In one line: wall-clock timing tells you the step was slow; a trace tells you which specific operation — or which idle gap — made it slow.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: in a pit stop, one idle crew member just means that person wasn't the bottleneck. On a TPU, one engine (say, the matrix unit) sitting idle while another engine (the vector unit, or a data-copy engine) works can be totally fine — good scheduling deliberately overlaps independent work. You must check whether the critical path (the longest dependency chain across all tracks) has gaps, not whether any single track has a gap.

4 · HOW & WHYThe profiler API, and the three failure classes✓

The core API, by increasing control

  • with jax.profiler.trace(log_dir): — a context manager. Wrap the code you want profiled (typically several training steps, skipping the first step or two which include compile time) and it writes trace files when the block exits.
  • jax.profiler.start_trace(log_dir) / jax.profiler.stop_trace() — the manual form. Useful when "start" and "stop" happen in different functions, or you want to start after step N to skip warmup.
  • jax.named_scope("name") (usable inside jitted code) or the jax.profiler.annotate_function decorator — inject human-readable labels into the trace, so "the FFN block" doesn't just show up as fusion.3142.
  • jax.profiler.save_device_memory_profile(filename) — a separate one-shot snapshot of device memory (not a timeline), useful for OOM debugging. A different day's problem, same module.
✅
with jax.profiler.trace(log_dir): ...; jax.block_until_ready(loss)
"Record everything the device and host do during this block, and don't let the block exit until the device has actually finished."

Three failure classes to distinguish

  • Compute-bound — the matrix/vector units are busy the whole time. Good, or a "how do I make the math itself faster" problem.
  • Communication-bound — the device is waiting on collectives, like an all-reduce across chips.
  • Host-bound — the device sits idle waiting on Python, data loading, or dispatch overhead. The most common first-timer bug.
🔗
Causal chain: jit compiles your step into an opaque program → wall-clock timing can't see inside it → jax.profiler.trace() records every op with a timestamp → the trace viewer's stats table ranks them by total time → you classify the step and name the specific offender.
