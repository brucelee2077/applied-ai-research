Phase 1 · Week 2 · Day 1 (Mon)

Rooflines
Arithmetic Intensity

Your code is slow. Is it slow because the chip can't do math fast enough — or because it can't feed the math fast enough? These are two totally different problems with two totally different fixes. Today: one simple number, the arithmetic intensity, that tells you which one you have.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: compute the arithmetic intensity of any operation, read a roofline plot, and say — with numbers — whether a matmul or an elementwise add is compute-bound or memory-bound.
1 · WHAT IS ITTwo ceilings, and the one number that picks between them✓
🧭
What this assumes: you can read basic code and multiply numbers. That is all. No hardware or performance background needed. We define every term the first time it appears.

First, three words

A FLOP is one "floating-point operation" — a single multiply or add on decimal numbers. When people say a chip does "300 TFLOP/s", they mean it can do 300 trillion of these math operations per second. That is its peak compute: the top speed of the math engine.

Memory bandwidth is how many bytes of data the chip can move between its memory and its math engine per second, measured in bytes/s. A byte is 8 bits; a normal 32-bit number is 4 bytes. Before the math engine can multiply two numbers, someone has to fetch those numbers from memory. That fetching happens at the bandwidth speed.

The key idea: a chip has two speed limits, not one

People imagine a chip has a single "speed". It has two, and they are independent:

  • Peak compute — how fast it can do math (FLOP/s). Think: how fast the workers work.
  • Peak memory bandwidth — how fast it can deliver data to those workers (bytes/s). Think: how fast supplies arrive.

Whichever runs out first is your real speed limit. Fast workers are useless if supplies never arrive; endless supplies are useless if the workers are slow. Your job is to find out which one is holding you back.

The one number: arithmetic intensity

Arithmetic intensity (AI) is the ratio of "math done" to "data moved" for an operation:

🧮
arithmetic intensity = FLOPs performed / bytes moved to and from memory
"For every byte I bring in from memory, how many math operations do I get out of it?" High AI = you squeeze lots of math from each byte. Low AI = you barely compute before needing the next byte.

AI decides which ceiling you hit. The achievable speed is simply whichever limit you reach first:

🧮
achievable performance = min( peak_FLOP/s , AI × bandwidth )
Low AI → the AI × bandwidth term is small → memory-bound (starved for data). High AI → that term is huge → you hit the flat peak_FLOP/s ceiling → compute-bound. The AI where they cross is the ridge point.

Why this exists

Before you spend a week optimizing, you want to know: am I fighting the math engine or the memory pipe? The answer completely changes the fix. This whole framework is called the roofline model — a plot with a slanted "memory roof" that rises with AI, then a flat "compute roof" once AI is high enough. You will draw one in the playground.

2 · INTUITIONA kitchen with a narrow delivery door✓

Picture a restaurant kitchen. The chefs are the math engine — they do the cooking (the FLOPs). The delivery door is the memory bandwidth — every ingredient must come through it. There is exactly one door, and it opens at a fixed speed.

🚪🥕
Low AI → the door is the bottleneck

Each delivery gets barely chopped before the next one is needed. The chefs stand around waiting. Adding more chefs (faster compute) does nothing — the door is the limit. This is memory-bound.

👨‍🍳🍲
High AI → the chefs are the bottleneck

One delivery feeds a huge, slow-cooked dish. The door is idle most of the time; the chefs are flat out. Now a faster door does nothing — you need more chefs. This is compute-bound.

Arithmetic intensity is "dishes cooked per delivery." Cook a lot per delivery (high AI) and chefs are the limit. Cook barely anything per delivery (low AI) and the door is the limit — no matter how many chefs you hire.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real chip doesn't fully stop the door while cooking. Deliveries and cooking overlap. The roofline model assumes perfect overlap and gives you the best case — the ceiling you can approach, not always reach.

4 · HOW & WHYThe roofline rules, and why they decide your fix✓

The rules

  • Count the FLOPs (math done) and the bytes moved (data read from and written to memory) for your operation. Divide: that is the arithmetic intensity (AI).
  • Compare AI to the chip's ridge point, which is peak_FLOP/s ÷ bandwidth. Below the ridge → memory-bound. Above it → compute-bound.
  • Achievable speed is min( peak_FLOP/s , AI × bandwidth ). On a roofline plot this is a slanted line (memory roof) rising until it meets a flat line (compute roof) at the ridge point.
  • The fix depends on the side you are on. Memory-bound? Move fewer bytes — fuse operations, use smaller data types, reuse data on-chip. Faster math will not help. Compute-bound? Do fewer or cheaper FLOPs, or use faster math units. More bandwidth will not help.
✅
ridge_point = peak_FLOP/s / bandwidth
For a chip at 300 TFLOP/s and 2 TB/s: ridge = 150 FLOP/byte. An op must reuse each byte at least 150 times before the chefs, not the door, become the limit.

Why it matters so much

  • It stops you optimizing the wrong thing. Speeding up math on a memory-bound kernel is wasted effort — the door is still the limit.
  • It explains real systems. Big matmuls in training are compute-bound (huge reuse). Loading model weights once per token during LLM decode is memory-bound (almost no reuse per byte).
  • It is one cheap number. You compute AI on paper in seconds, before writing any kernel, and it tells you which battle you are actually fighting.
🔗
Causal chain: an op has a fixed FLOP count and byte count → their ratio is AI → AI vs. the ridge point says compute-bound or memory-bound → that verdict tells you which optimization can possibly help.

7 · PRODUCEBuild a roofline calculator✓

Today's task: write a small tool that takes an operation's cost and a chip's spec, and tells you whether the operation is compute-bound or memory-bound — with numbers.

Acceptance criteria

  • Write a function roofline(flops, bytes, peak_flops, bandwidth).
  • It computes AI = flops / bytes and the ridge point = peak_flops / bandwidth.
  • It decides the bound: "memory-bound" if AI < ridge, else "compute-bound".
  • It prints the achievable throughput = min(peak_flops, AI * bandwidth) in FLOP/s.
  • Run it on both walkthrough cases (1024³ matmul, and x+y for 1e6 floats) with peak = 300e12 and bandwidth = 2e12, and confirm the verdicts match Section 5.
