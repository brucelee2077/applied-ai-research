ShardingSplitting Arrays Across Devices

One chip can only hold so much. When an array or model is too big for a single device, you cut it into pieces — one per device. That is sharding. But split data can't stay split forever: to finish the math, devices must talk. Today: how sharding works and the three ways devices share results.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: explain what a device, mesh, shard, and collective are, and describe the three core collectives — AllGather, ReduceScatter, and AllReduce — and when you need each.
1What is sharding, and why do devices need to talk?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: basic Python/NumPy — you can picture an array like [1, 2, 3, 4]. No prior knowledge of hardware, parallelism, or collectives needed. We define every term here.

First, four words

Before anything else, four plain definitions. Every term today is built from these.

  • A device is one chip that does math — one GPU or one TPU. It has its own memory and can only hold so much.
  • A mesh is a group of devices arranged so they can work together — think of 4 chips wired into a small grid.
  • A shard is one piece of a big array. If you cut an array into 4 pieces, each piece is a shard, and each device holds one shard.
  • A collective is an operation where all the devices talk at once to combine or share their shards. It is how split data gets recombined.

The problem: the array is too big for one chip

Modern models have billions of numbers. A single device does not have enough memory to hold all of them. So you cut the big array into pieces and give one piece to each device. This is sharding — splitting an array across devices so each holds only part of it.

Sharding solves the memory problem. But it creates a new one: each device now only sees its own piece. To finish most computations, a device needs data that lives on other devices. So the devices must communicate.

The answer: three collectives

There are three core ways devices share their shards. You will see all three today, but here is the one-line version:

📦
AllGather — every device ends up with a full copy of everyone's shards.
ReduceScatter — the devices sum their contributions, and each device keeps just one slice of that sum.
AllReduce — the devices sum everything, and every device ends up with the full summed result. (It equals a ReduceScatter followed by an AllGather.)

So what's today's catch?

The catch is cost. These collectives move a lot of data between chips over the wires that connect them (the interconnect). When you scale to hundreds of devices, that communication — not the math — is often the slowest part. Knowing which collective you are paying for is the whole game.

2A group project with a shared documentIntuition

Four people have to handle one giant document. It is too long for any one person to hold in their head. So they split it: each person takes one section. That split is sharding — each person (device) holds one shard.

Now they need to combine their work, and there are three ways to do it:

📄📄📄📄
AllGather = everyone photocopies everything

Each person photocopies everyone else's section. When they finish, all four hold a full copy of the whole document. Nothing is summed — it is just gathered.

➕✂️
ReduceScatter = add tallies, keep your slice

Each person is counting votes. They add up all four tallies into one grand total, but each person only walks away with their own portion of that total — not the whole thing.

➕📄
AllReduce = add, then everyone holds the total

They add up all four tallies into one grand total, and every person ends with the same complete total. This is the most common one in training.

🔗
AllReduce = ReduceScatter + AllGather

First everyone keeps a slice of the sum (ReduceScatter). Then everyone photocopies the slices to reassemble the whole sum (AllGather). Two steps, one result.

In one line: shard to fit; then use a collective — gather to get a full copy, reduce-scatter to keep a slice of the sum, all-reduce to give everyone the full sum.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: real devices don't take turns like polite people. Collectives are carefully scheduled so many chips send and receive at the same moment over the interconnect — and that traffic is what costs time.

4The three collectives, and why communication is the bottleneckHow & why

The rules

Say we have 4 devices. Each device i holds a shard x_i. The three collectives differ in what each device ends up holding:

  • AllGather — no math, just sharing. Every device ends up holding the full stacked array [x_0, x_1, x_2, x_3]. Use it to reassemble a full copy from shards.
  • ReduceScatter — reduce (usually sum) then split. Devices sum their contributions element by element, then each device keeps only one slice of that sum. Use it to spread a summed result so no device holds the whole thing.
  • AllReduce — reduce, then everyone gets the whole result. Devices sum everything, and every device ends up with the identical full sum. Use it when all devices need the same combined answer (for example, summed gradients in training).
✅
AllReduce = ReduceScatter → AllGather
First each device keeps a slice of the sum (ReduceScatter). Then those slices are gathered back to everyone (AllGather). That is why AllReduce moves roughly twice the data of a single gather.

Why communication is the scaling bottleneck

  • Collectives move a lot of data — every device has to send and receive shards over the interconnect, and the total traffic grows with model size and device count.
  • The wires are the slow part — chips do math extremely fast, but moving numbers between chips is far slower. At large scale, devices often sit idle waiting for a collective to finish.
  • So the goal is to move less — good sharding layouts pick collectives that minimize how much data crosses the interconnect, and overlap communication with computation where possible.
🔗
Causal chain: models too big for one chip → shard across devices → devices hold only pieces → collectives recombine them → collective traffic dominates at scale → communication, not math, sets the speed limit.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Sharding is easy to get numerically wrong in a way that still produces a plausible-looking loss curve, and it is easy to shard along the axis that costs the most traffic.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): a missing (or wrong) collective, so replicas silently disagree. If each device holds a shard and you forget the AllReduce on gradients — or use AllGather where you needed a summing reduce — every device keeps updating its own copy with only its local information. Nothing crashes: shapes still match, the step runs, and the loss even goes down for a while. But the replicas drift apart and the effective batch is wrong, so the model trains to a worse point than you think. In code review, a senior engineer checks that every quantity which must be identical across devices (gradients, batch-norm stats, the final summed result) actually passes through a reduce collective, not just a gather.
⚖️
Trade-off: fit a bigger model vs. pay interconnect traffic. Sharding lets you hold a model that no single chip could store, which is the only way to train the largest models at all. The cost is that recombining shards means collectives, and collective traffic grows with model size and device count until the wires — not the math units — set your speed. In a design review, choosing the sharding layout is choosing which axis to split so the collectives you trigger move the fewest bytes across the interconnect.
