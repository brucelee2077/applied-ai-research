Hardware Physics · Week 2 · Day 5

Memory FootprintWhere Your GPU RAM Goes

Your model has 1 billion parameters. That sounds like 4 GB of weights. So why does training crash on a 16 GB GPU before you even feed it data? Because training memory is four things, not one — and one of them is a hidden 2x multiplier. Today: exactly where every byte goes.

🎯
By the end, you'll be able to: name the four things that eat memory during training (weights, gradients, optimizer state, activations), compute the "static" cost of ~16N bytes for the Adam optimizer, and explain why activations are often the biggest term of all.
1What actually uses memory when you train a model?What is it
🧭
What this assumes: you can write a training loop and you know a model has parameters (numbers it learns). No prior knowledge of memory accounting needed. We define every term as it appears.

The puzzle: 4 GB of weights, 16 GB crash

A model's size is usually reported as its parameter count — say 1 billion parameters (we write this N = 1e9). Each number in fp32 (32-bit floating point) takes 4 bytes. So the weights are 4 × 1e9 = 4 GB. Yet training that model on a 16 GB GPU often fails immediately. Where did the other 12+ GB go?

The answer: "the model" is only one of four things living in memory during training. Let's name all four.

The four memory tenants

  • Weights — the parameters themselves, the numbers the model learned. This is what you think of as "the model." Cost in fp32: 4N.
  • Gradients — for every weight, training computes one number saying "nudge this weight up or down." So gradients are a same-size mirror of the weights. A gradient is the slope of the loss with respect to one weight. Cost: another 4N.
  • Optimizer state — extra bookkeeping the optimizer keeps per weight. The optimizer is the rule that turns gradients into weight updates. The popular Adam optimizer keeps two running averages per weight (called the first moment m and second moment v). That is the famous Adam 2x: two full copies of the weights. Cost: 2 × 4N = 8N.
  • Activations — the intermediate outputs of each layer, saved during the forward pass so the backward pass can compute gradients. An activation is a layer's output that must be kept around until backprop uses it. This term does not scale with N — it scales with batch size × sequence length × number of layers, and it is often the biggest term of all.
📦
The static budget (before activations): weights 4N + gradients 4N + Adam state 8N = 16N bytes.
For 1B params in fp32 that is 16 GB — already at the limit of a 16 GB card, and we haven't stored a single activation yet.

So what's today's catch?

The catch is that people size their GPU by the weights alone (4N) and forget the other three tenants. The static cost is 16N with Adam, and then activations pile on top — and activations grow with your batch. Knowing this lets you predict crashes before they happen.

2Packing a suitcase for a training tripIntuition

Think of training as packing for a long trip. The clothes you fold in are the model. But a suitcase always ends up holding much more than just the clothes.

👕
Clothes = weights (4N)

The actual thing you came to bring — your parameters. One clean copy. This is the number everyone quotes.

🧺
Laundry bag = gradients (4N)

A mirror-image dirty-laundry bag: one dirty item for every clean item. Gradients are a same-size shadow of the weights.

🧴🧴
Two toiletry kits = Adam state (8N)

Adam insists on packing two full kits — the moments m and v. That doubles the weight-sized load. This is the 2x multiplier.

🧳
Unpacked stuff = activations

Everything you spread out during the trip and can't fold back until you're done. It grows with how much you do (batch × sequence × layers) and often fills the whole room.

In one line: the clothes are only a quarter of the load. Weights + gradients + two Adam kits are the fixed 16N; the unpacked activations pile on top and often dwarf the rest.

直觉 / Intuition: 把训练想成打包行李 —— 衣服(权重)只是四样东西之一：还有脏衣袋(梯度)、两个洗漱包(Adam 的 m、v)、以及摊开收不回去的东西(activations)。The technical point is that the static load is 16N (weights + grads + Adam), and activations pile on top and grow with batch × seq until they dwarf the rest.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a suitcase has a fixed size, but activations are not fixed — you choose them. A bigger batch or longer sequence unpacks more stuff, so activation memory can balloon far past everything else.

4The arithmetic of the four termsHow & why

The rules (fp32, 4 bytes per number)

  • Weights = bytes_per_param × N = 4N. In fp32 each parameter is 4 bytes.
  • Gradients = 4N. One gradient per weight, same dtype, so it matches the weights exactly.
  • Optimizer state (Adam) = 2 × 4N = 8N. Adam keeps two per-weight buffers, m and v. That is the 2x multiplier. (Plain SGD keeps none → 0; SGD with momentum keeps one → 4N.)
  • Static total with Adam = 4N + 4N + 8N = 16N, independent of batch size.
  • Activations ≈ (a per-token cost that depends on the architecture) × batch × sequence × layers. It scales with what you feed the model, not with N.
✅
static = 16 × N × (bytes_per_param / 4)
For 1B params in fp32: 16 × 1e9 = 16 GB before a single activation. Switch to bf16 (2 bytes) and the static term roughly halves — but activations may still dominate.

Why activations often win

  • They scale with your workload. Double the batch, roughly double the activations. The other three terms don't budge.
  • They stack per layer. A deep model saves an activation at every layer for the backward pass, so long networks accumulate a lot.
  • The escape hatch: gradient checkpointing. Instead of saving every activation, you save a few "checkpoints" and recompute the rest during the backward pass. You trade more compute for less activation memory.
🔗
Causal chain: parameters need a value (weights) → training needs a slope per value (gradients) → Adam needs two running averages per value (8N, the 2x) → the forward pass must remember its outputs for backprop (activations, batch-dependent) → total memory = 16N + activations.
🪜
Math Ladder — the 16N static-memory rule
1 · In words: training keeps roughly four model-sized things in memory, and only one of them is the weights you quoted.
2 · The formula: static = weights + grads + optimizer = 4N + 4N + 8N = 16N (fp32) — N = parameter count; 4N = weights (4 bytes each); 4N = one gradient per weight; 8N = Adam's two per-weight buffers m, v. Activations pile on top and scale with batch × seq.
3 · Tiny numbers: N = 1e9, fp32 → weights 4 GB + grads 4 GB + Adam 8 GB = 16 GB static, before a single activation.
4 · Sanity check: static should be about 4× the weights with Adam. If your budget ≈ 1× the weights, you counted only parameters — the classic OOM surprise.

The staff lens — one silent failure, one trade-off

Memory math is where a "why did this run out of memory?" question gets answered before you launch, and the classic mistake is counting only the weights.

⚠️
Failure mode (silent): budgeting for weights alone and forgetting the 4x. A beginner sizes the job by parameters times bytes — 1B params in fp32 = 4 GB — and picks hardware around that. But training also stores gradients (another 4N) and Adam's two buffers (8N), so the real static cost is 16N, four times the weights, before a single activation. This is not a code bug that throws early: your notebook may run on a tiny batch, look fine, then blow up only at the real batch size or later in training when activations pile on top. In code review, a senior engineer checks that the memory estimate includes gradients, optimizer state (Adam = 2x weights), and activations — not just the parameter count — and that it uses the actual training dtype.
⚖️
Trade-off: Adam's fast, stable convergence vs. 2x the weight memory. Adam keeps a per-weight momentum m and variance v, which is why it trains reliably with little tuning — but those two buffers cost 8N, the whole 2x optimizer-state term. Plain SGD keeps none (0) and momentum-SGD keeps one (4N), so they free memory you could spend on a bigger model or batch. In a design review, choosing the optimizer is partly a memory decision: you weigh Adam's convergence quality against the parameters or batch size that its extra state forces you to give up.
🎤
Say this in an interview: "Training memory is four tenants, not one: weights (4N in fp32), gradients (4N), Adam's two moment buffers (8N), and activations — so the static cost is 16N, four times the weights people quote, and activations stack on top and scale with batch × seq, so they're usually what OOMs you. The nuance is the silent failure: sizing hardware by parameter count alone runs fine on a tiny batch, then blows up at the real batch or later in training. My knobs are dtype (bf16 halves static), optimizer (SGD drops the 8N), and gradient checkpointing (trade compute to shrink activations)."
