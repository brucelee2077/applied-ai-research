Deriving the
Scaling Law

Yesterday you saw that bigger models get better. Today you measure the exact relationship: you fit a straight line through the "valley floor" of loss curves to pull out the exponents that say how loss shrinks as you add parameters (N) and data (D) — the same method the Chinchilla paper used.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: read a power law as a straight line in log-log space, find the compute-optimal model size at each budget, fit the exponents with np.polyfit, and say whether a task looks Chinchilla-balanced or Kaplan-lopsided.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a scaling law, and how do you derive one?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you know what a model, a training run, and a "loss" number are (yesterday's lesson). No prior scaling-law math needed — we start from zero.

The problem: frontier models cost millions

Training a big model can cost millions of dollars. Nobody wants to spend that money and then find out the result was bad. So teams want to forecast how good a big model will be before they train it, using only cheap small runs. A scaling law is the equation that lets them do this.

First, one word we use everywhere: the loss is a single number that measures how many mistakes a model makes. Lower loss means fewer mistakes. Training tries to push the loss down.

The shape of the law: a power law

Across many experiments, loss falls in a very regular way as you add resources. It follows a power law:

🧮
L ≈ C · X^(-p)
L = loss, X = the resource (model size or data), C = a constant, and p = the exponent — the single most important number. It tells you how fast loss drops when you add more resource.

Here is the trick that makes today easy: a power law looks like a straight line when you plot it on log-log axes (the log of loss against the log of X). The slope of that line is exactly -p. So "fitting the scaling law" really means "find the slope of a line in log-log space."

Three new terms you need

  • N = the number of parameters (the weights inside the model). More N = a bigger brain.
  • D = the number of training tokens (pieces of data the model reads). More D = more studying.
  • FLOPs = floating-point operations, the unit of compute cost. For a transformer, compute ≈ 6 · N · D (a handy rule: each token costs about 6 operations per parameter). Your budget is a fixed number of FLOPs, and the question is: spend it on a bigger model (N) or more data (D)?

Chinchilla vs. Kaplan

Chinchilla is the 2022 paper (Hoffmann et al.). They trained over 400 models and found that for a fixed compute budget, N and D should grow together, roughly 1:1 — every time you double the model, double the data. The rule of thumb: about 20 tokens per parameter. This corrected the earlier Kaplan et al. (2020) law, which said to mostly grow N and under-fed models on data. Chinchilla-70B beat the much larger Gopher-280B using the same compute — proof that most big models of that era were undertrained.

The method you reproduce today

Sweep model size. For each compute budget, plot loss against N and find the minimum — the model size that gives the lowest loss at that budget. Collect those minima across several budgets, and fit a power law through them. The exponents of that fit are the scaling law.

📚
Official sources (optional): Chinchilla — Hoffmann et al. 2022 · Kaplan et al. 2020 · Feinberg — Princeton talk (2025)
2 · INTUITIONPacking a fixed-weight suitcase✓

Imagine you are packing for a trip and the airline caps your suitcase at a fixed weight — that cap is your compute budget. You can pack a heavier camera (a bigger model, N) or more clothes (more data, D), but the scale limits the total.

📷🧳
Kaplan traveler

"Buy the biggest camera you can, skimp on clothes." Travelers who followed this kept arriving underdressed — the model was undertrained, starved of data.

⚖️🧳
Chinchilla traveler

The seasoned packer who has weighed thousands of bags: for every kilo of camera gear, add roughly a kilo of clothes. Keep N and D balanced.

The power law is the smooth curve you would draw through the best-packed bag at every weight limit. Its slope tells you how much better your trip gets each time you are allowed a heavier suitcase — that slope is the exponent you are hunting for today.

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a suitcase has one obvious best packing, but a model's loss is noisy and measured with error. You never see one clean minimum — you fit a curve through a scatter of noisy minima and trust the trend, not any single point.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of fitting a scaling law, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • Compute is C = 6·N·D. Fix C, and every model on that budget is one point on an IsoFLOP curve (loss vs N). It is U-shaped: too small a model underfits; too big a model wastes budget on too few tokens. The bottom is the compute-optimal N*.
  • Collect N* (and its matching D*) from several budgets. Those minima are the only points the law lives in.
  • Fit on log-log axes: a, b = np.polyfit(np.log(C), np.log(N*), 1). The slope a is the exponent in N* ∝ C^a. Never fit on raw values.
  • Since C ≈ 6·N·D, the two exponents must satisfy a + b ≈ 1 (compute conservation). Chinchilla: a ≈ b ≈ 0.5 (equal growth). Kaplan: a ≈ 0.73 (favor N, starve D).
✅
a, b = np.polyfit(np.log(C_budgets), np.log(N_star), 1)
"Fit a line in log-log space through the optimal model sizes; the slope a is the data-hunger exponent." a≈0.5 means grow N and D equally.

The one mistake to avoid

⚠️
Do not fit the law through all your runs. Regressing loss on N across every point gives a meaningless average slope, contaminated by under- and over-sized models. The scaling law lives only in the trail of valley floors — the per-budget minima.

Why it matters

  • Forecast before you spend — cheap small runs predict the loss of a run you cannot afford to try twice.
  • Split a budget correctly — the exponents tell you, for a fixed C, exactly how big to make the model and how much data to feed it (~20 tokens per parameter).
  • Catch undertraining — Chinchilla-70B beat Gopher-280B at equal compute purely because the exponents said "more data, smaller model."
🔗
Causal chain: loss follows a power law → power laws are straight lines in log-log → fit only the per-budget minima → slope = exponent → the exponents tell you how to spend compute.

7 · PRODUCEFit a real scaling law on a synthetic task✓

Today's task: build a reproducible experiment that fits a power-law scaling curve on a synthetic integer-addition task, extracts the compute-optimal exponents, and compares them against the Chinchilla (~0.5/0.5) vs Kaplan (~0.73/0.27) regimes.

What it does

  • Generates a synthetic addition dataset (add two integers of varying digit length).
  • Sweeps a grid of model sizes N and token counts D; groups runs into IsoFLOP buckets by C = 6·N·D.
  • Finds the loss minimum in each bucket, then fits N* ∝ C^a and D* ∝ C^b with np.polyfit on log-log data.
  • Fits the parametric form L(N,D) = E + A/N^α + B/D^β.

Acceptance criteria

  • bash run.sh runs end-to-end, exits 0, and writes a log-log fit plot into the experiment folder (no plt.savefig outside the artifact dir).
  • At least 4 distinct IsoFLOP buckets, each with a printed loss minimum and its (N*, D*).
  • Reports fitted exponents a and b to 3 decimals, plus a plain-English interpretation.
  • Reports fitted α, β, floor E, and the fit R².
  • test_fit.py passes: asserts a + b ≈ 1.0 ± 0.1 and that injecting a known exponent (e.g. a=0.5) is recovered within ±0.05.
  • RESULTS.md says whether the task lands closer to Chinchilla or Kaplan, why, and the tokens-per-parameter ratio at the largest budget.
