Phase 3 · Week 11 · Day 4 (Thu)

Mixture of Experts
Architecture Upgrade

Your addition-transformer has one dense feed-forward block that every token flows through. Today you rip that out and replace it with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer — many separate feed-forward "experts" plus a small router that decides which expert each token visits. Same move Switch Transformers made on top of the original Transformer.

🎯 By the end, you'll be able to: explain why sparse MoE decouples total parameters from compute-per-token, build a top-1 router with a fixed-capacity dispatch buffer in Flax, compute the load-balancing auxiliary loss, and swap it into your Week 9 dense model as a drop-in replacement.
1 · WHAT IS ITWhat is a Mixture of Experts, and why does it exist?✓
🧭
What this assumes: you have a working Flax transformer block (Week 9) with a dense feed-forward network (FFN), and you've already derived a Chinchilla-style scaling law for it (Week 9–11 Mon–Wed). No prior MoE experience needed.

The block you're about to rip out

Inside every transformer block sits a feed-forward network (FFN): two nn.Dense layers with a nonlinearity between them. Every single token — no matter what it is — flows through the same FFN, using all of its parameters. That's called a dense layer: dense in the sense that every parameter participates in every forward pass.

The problem dense layers create

If you want a model that "knows more," the usual move is to make the FFN bigger — more parameters. But bigger parameters means more compute (FLOPs) for every single token, even simple ones. Total knowledge and compute-per-token are welded together. You can't grow one without growing the other.

The answer: Mixture of Experts

Mixture of Experts (MoE) breaks that weld. Instead of one FFN, you build N separate, identically-shaped FFNs called experts. A tiny extra layer called the router (also called the gate) looks at each token and decides which expert(s) should handle it. Switch Transformers — the model that made this simplification popular — use top-1 routing: each token goes to exactly one expert, its single highest-scoring choice.

📦
More experts = more total parameters = more total "knowledge capacity." But each token still only visits one expert, so the compute (FLOPs) it costs stays close to what a single dense FFN would cost.
This is sparse activation: only a fraction of total parameters participate in any single forward pass. It decouples "how much the model knows" from "how much compute it takes to answer."

So what's today's catch?

Routing is data-dependent — you don't know in advance how many tokens will pick each expert. But JAX needs fixed array shapes to compile code. That tension is the heart of today's engineering: a router, an expert capacity cap, and a load-balancing auxiliary loss to stop the router from playing favorites. We build all three below.

2 · INTUITIONA triage nurse in the emergency room✓

Think of a hospital emergency room. A dense FFN is one general-practice doctor seeing every patient. An MoE layer adds a triage nurse and a hallway of specialists.

🩺
Dense FFN = one GP doctor

Every patient sees the same doctor, a jack-of-all-trades. Every patient takes roughly the same amount of the doctor's time and expertise, no matter what's wrong.

🏥
MoE = triage nurse + specialists

The nurse (router) looks at each patient (token) and sends them to one specialist (expert) — cardiologist, pediatrician, orthopedist. The hospital now has far more total expertise, but each patient still occupies only one specialist's time.

In one line: more specialists on staff means more total expertise (more parameters); each patient still sees only one specialist (compute per token stays low).

⚠️
Where the analogy breaks: a real triage nurse uses medical judgment. The router is just a learned linear layer with no built-in notion of "this is a math token, send it to the math expert." Any specialization that emerges is a side effect of gradient descent, not designed intent — experts often split by surface statistics like token frequency, not clean human categories. And sometimes too many patients get sent to the same specialist — her waiting room fills up (expert capacity overflow), and the patients who don't fit just don't get seen this round (token dropping). If the nurse plays favorites and always sends people to the fastest specialist, the others sit idle and never get practice — this is exactly why the load-balancing auxiliary loss exists, like a hospital administrator forcing the nurse to spread patients around evenly.

4 · HOW & WHYThe rules of MoE routing, and why it matters✓

The rules

  • The router is a single nn.Dense(num_experts, use_bias=False) mapping d_model → num_experts, followed by nn.softmax, giving one probability distribution per token over all experts.
  • Top-1 routing: expert_index = argmax(router_probs) picks each token's single best expert. gate_value = max(router_probs) is the router's confidence, and it scales the chosen expert's output.
  • Expert capacity caps how many tokens any one expert can process: capacity = (tokens_per_batch / num_experts) * capacity_factor. It must be a fixed integer — JAX needs static shapes to compile.
  • Tokens beyond capacity are dropped: their expert output becomes zero, and they pass through only via the residual connection — not a bug, an explicit, accepted trade-off.
✅
expert_capacity = int((tokens_per_batch / num_experts) * capacity_factor)
Dispatch uses a fixed-size one-hot tensor [tokens, num_experts, expert_capacity], so overflow becomes a countable, fixed-shape masking operation instead of a variable-length filter.

Why it's a superpower

  • Decoupled scaling — total parameters grow with num_experts; active compute per token stays close to one expert's cost.
  • Drop-in replacement — the MoE layer's output shape matches the dense FFN's exactly, so nothing else in the transformer block needs to change.
  • Needs a governor — without the load-balancing auxiliary loss, the router collapses onto a few favorite experts and the rest go untrained ("dead experts"): L_aux = num_experts * Σᵢ(f_i · P_i), added to the loss scaled by a small coefficient (Switch Transformer uses α = 0.01).
🔗
Causal chain: dense FFN (Attention Is All You Need) → fixed compute per token limits total capacity → MoE splits the FFN into N experts + a router → top-1 dispatch with fixed capacity keeps JAX shapes static → sparse activation decouples parameters from compute → this week's weekend lab repeats the IsoFLOPs matrix on this new architecture.

7 · PRODUCESwap the dense FFN for an MoE layer with top-1 gating✓

Today's task (from the curriculum): modify the existing Flax addition-transformer (built Week 9 Friday, trained Week 9 Saturday–Sunday) by replacing its dense feed-forward nn.Module with a new MixtureOfExperts nn.Module containing a router/gate and top-1 dispatch logic.

Acceptance criteria

  • Forward pass on a dummy batch (batch=8, seq=16, d_model=64, num_experts=4) produces output shape identical to the dense FFN's output shape ([8, 16, 64]) — proof it's a drop-in replacement.
  • A printed/logged count of dropped tokens for at least one deliberately-imbalanced routing case — the capacity-overflow mechanism, implemented and observable.
  • The auxiliary load-balancing loss (L_aux = num_experts * Σᵢ(f_i · P_i)) computed and unit-tested against a hand-computed value for a small toy router-probability array.
  • A backward pass (jax.grad) confirmed to produce non-error, correctly-shaped gradients for every expert's parameters — including experts that received zero tokens that forward pass (their gradient should be exactly zero, not NaN or a shape mismatch).
  • pytest experiments/week11_jax/thu_moe_gating/ passes with no failures.
  • Total parameter count of the new MoE model printed and compared against the Week 9 dense baseline's parameter count, showing the expected roughly num_experts-times growth in total (not active) parameters.

⚠️
Gotcha: do not implement routing with boolean masking or jnp.where/x[mask] dynamic-shape gathers — the resulting shape depends on the data, and JAX can't trace that under jit. Always dispatch into a fixed [num_experts, expert_capacity, d_model] buffer via one-hot matmul. Separately: f_i (token-count fraction, from non-differentiable argmax) must have gradients stopped, while P_i (softmax probabilities) stays differentiable — mixing this up silently corrupts the router's gradients.
